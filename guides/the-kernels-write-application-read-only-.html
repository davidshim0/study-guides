DIMES 2026 · A Field Guide

Making the DIMM honest, not DRAM-shaped

Optane tried to hide a phase-change memory's quirks behind a DRAM-compatible interface — and the hiding cost more than the quirks did. AROM's fix: the application never writes LtRAM at all. Only the kernel does, during migration.

📄 "The Kernel's Write: Application Read-Only Memory" · Shim, Mohr, Levis 🎓 Assumes tiering/CXL background — primers skip the basics ⏱ ~25 min read
✍️

LtRAM is READ-ONLY to applications — only the KERNEL WRITES it, and only while migrating a page from DRAM into LtRAM.
Enforced by copy-on-write: an application write instead faults and migrates the page back to DRAM before the store applies.

Before you begin

Quick glossary, then six primers

You already know tiering, CXL, and the mm stack — these skip that and focus on what's specific to LtRAM and this paper.

1Why this isn't "just another tiering paper"▾

TPP, Memtis, HeMem, Colloid, Alto — all place a slower tier below DRAM and pay a latency tax only on reads that miss to it. LtRAM is different: it's proposed to sit alongside DRAM as main memory, and the paper's own §6 point is sharp: "misplacement in LtRAM pays a per-store copy cost on the critical path," whereas CXL tiering misplacement only slows reads.

Now you're ready: read every claim here as a HW/SW interface redesign problem, not a hot/cold placement policy problem — the placement policy (§5.2.1) is almost the least novel part.
2The three LtRAM drawbacks, precisely▾

Not "slow" in general — three specific shapes: asymmetric read/write latency (Table 1: reads near DRAM, writes 10×–1000× slower), limited endurance (10⁵–10⁸ writes/cell vs DRAM's ∞), and coarse write granularity (page or 256 B, vs a 64 B cache line).

Now you're ready: every hardware mechanism in §3.2 and every OS mechanism in §4.2 maps to exactly one of these three drawbacks — use that as your index while reading.
3Optane copied the SSD flash translation layer — and that's the villain▾

To stay DRAM-compatible, Optane hid wear-leveling, remapping, and buffering inside the DIMM (an AIT + write/read SRAM buffers), exactly the FTL pattern from SSDs. The paper's core empirical claim (§3.2) is that this hiding — not the 3D-XPoint media itself — is what made Optane slow and expensive.

Now you're ready: AROM's fix isn't a better FTL, it's removing the FTL and giving that job to software that already has better information (page tables, dirty bits, scan intervals).
4CoW as an endurance mechanism, not just an isolation mechanism▾

You know CoW from fork() and COW-based zero-copy. Here it's repurposed: every LtRAM page is permanently write-protected, and a write fault is the *only* path back to a writable copy — so CoW is doing double duty as both "protect applications from writing" and "generate the event that returns a page to DRAM." The separate migration into LtRAM (the wear-leveling-relevant one) is a distinct, OS-scheduled process — the periodic dirty-bit scan gated by the token allocator — not something the CoW fault triggers.

Now you're ready: the mm changes are smaller than they look — mostly extending the existing CoW fault handler's trigger condition, not new page-fault machinery.
5The stack property analog here: LRU stack ⟷ dirty-bit scan▾

Where UCP's monitor exploits the LRU stack property for cheap measurement, AROM exploits an already-existing kernel structure — the per-PTE dirty bit and periodic reclaim-style scanning — for cheap read-mostly classification. No new hardware counters, no new page-table bits; it's Linux's existing accessed/dirty tracking repurposed with a coarser threshold.

Now you're ready: the classifier's imprecision (a page can pass the threshold, then get written next interval) is the direct analog of a stale UCP partition between 5M-cycle re-decisions — same class of staleness bug, different mechanism.
6What "matching DRAM performance" means here▾

The paper's goal is narrower than it sounds: match DRAM on read-mostly workloads, at the cache-line access level, in isolation (§5.3's Figure 4 projections). It explicitly does not yet have application-level or mixed-workload benchmark numbers — those are future work.

Now you're ready: treat every latency number in this guide as a per-access, isolated projection, not a measured end-to-end speedup — that distinction matters when you compare this to throughput numbers you're used to from tiering papers.
The whole story in one place

AROM in a nutshell

The cost problem, why Optane's fix backfired, and the interface that replaces it.

DRAM is now over half of Azure server cost [18,23], and DRAM cost-per-bit scaling has slowed for a decade due to fundamental capacitor-scaling limits [19] — a squeeze the 2025–26 AI demand surge made worse (DIMM prices roughly doubled in 1Q26 [6,27]). LtRAM technologies (RRAM, PCM, FeFET, MRAM) offer a way out: DRAM-competitive reads at lower cost/bit, at the price of slow/coarse writes and bounded endurance.

Intel Optane, the one LtRAM ever shipped as a DIMM, tried to hide those costs behind a DRAM-compatible interface, borrowing the SSD flash-translation-layer pattern: an on-DIMM Address Indirection Table, wear-leveling logic, and read/write buffers. The paper's case study (§3.2) shows this hiding is what actually hurt: a 75% write-throughput drop from read-modify-write on sub-256 B stores, 60 μs wear-leveling tail spikes (>160× typical), 76–200 ns added per read for AIT lookups, and a ~67–70% read-bandwidth collapse under mixed read/write traffic.

AROM inverts the approach: instead of hiding LtRAM's asymmetry, expose it, and give the OS — which already tracks pages, dirty bits, and access patterns — the job the controller used to do. The invariant: LtRAM is read-only to applications; only the kernel writes it, via CoW migration on a write fault. This lets the controller shrink to three jobs (cache-line reads, 4 KB page writes, health reporting), eliminating the AIT and its overheads entirely.

The payoff, projected: LtRAM candidates come in 26–79% faster than Optane's ~305–374 ns random-read latency, and land between 9% faster and ~220% slower than DRAM's 80 ns — a large improvement over Optane's DRAM-incompatible-in-practice performance, at a fraction of the controller complexity.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • The bottleneck was never the media — it was the DRAM-compatible interface Optane built around it (§3.2's conclusion).
  • AROM: LtRAM is read-only to apps; only kernel migration writes it, enforced by CoW.
  • Controller shrinks to 3 jobs: cache-line reads, 4 KB page writes, per-block health export. No AIT, no on-DIMM DRAM cache.
  • OS gains 3 jobs: page placement (dirty-bit scan), CoW migration back to DRAM, token-based wear-leveling (r = NE/T).
  • Misplacement here costs a write-path CoW copy, not just a slow read — sharper penalty than CXL tiering.
  • Projected: 26–79% faster than Optane; 9% faster to ~220% slower than DRAM, at cache-line read granularity, in isolation.
  • Prototype on Enzian with NOR flash validates the interface; NOR's own slow media (not a real LtRAM candidate) makes its absolute numbers an outlier.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about AROM

Each stands alone; every card ends with where it breaks.

The invariant · AROM

A library's reference copy

Anyone can read the reference copy at the desk, but no patron may write in it — only the librarian, who re-binds a fresh, editable copy when it needs a change. That's AROM: applications read LtRAM freely; only the kernel "re-binds" a page (migrates it to DRAM) when a write must happen.

Where it breaks: the librarian re-binds occasionally; the kernel does it on the first write to an LtRAM page, migrating it to DRAM before the store applies — later writes to that same page land on the DRAM copy directly and don't re-fault, unless the OS later migrates it back into LtRAM.
Optane's fix · AIT

A landlord who never updates the room map

The building looks like one address, but a hidden ledger secretly tracks which physical room each tenant is really in, consulted on every visit. That's the AIT — every read stops there first. AROM removes the ledger: the kernel already knows exactly where each page lives, via the page table it maintains anyway.

Where it breaks: the landlord's ledger also did bad-room (bad-block) avoidance — the controller keeps a small SRAM-resident remap table for that; the paper doesn't spell out whether it's consulted on every read or kept off the hot path.
Granularity fix · 4KB writes

Paying in bricks, not loose sand

Optane's controller had to read a whole 256 B XPLine, mix in a 64 B write, and write it all back — like buying one grain and being billed for a full brick. AROM writes only at 4 KB page granularity, a clean multiple of every LtRAM's native write unit (Table 1), so the read-modify-write step disappears structurally.

Where it breaks: the kernel isn't batching application writes into LtRAM — application writes never reach LtRAM at all, they're diverted to DRAM via CoW. The 4 KB write unit only applies to the kernel's own DRAM→LtRAM page migrations; an application writing single bytes directly to LtRAM media would reintroduce the mismatch.
Endurance · Token allocator

A rationing book, not a fuel gauge

Instead of watching each cell's remaining life like a fuel gauge (as a hardware AIT tracks wear per block), the OS issues rationing tokens at a fixed rate r = N·E/T; a migration into LtRAM must spend one. Unused tokens can bank up over idle periods for a later burst, so the long-run average provably stays in budget without ever recording per-block erase counts.

Where it breaks: rationing controls total spend, not who spends it — the paper is explicit (§5.2.2, §7) that this bounds total wear but doesn't guarantee it's spread evenly across the chip's physical regions.
Open problem · huge pages

One bad apple, whole crate returned

A 2 MB huge page is one migration unit for TLB efficiency. If even one 4 KB slice inside it turns write-heavy, the entire crate — including the still-read-mostly apples — gets sent back to DRAM as a single CoW fault, inflating exactly the write traffic AROM exists to avoid (§7).

Where it breaks: unlike a real crate, you *can* split it — 4 KB sub-pages restore fine-grained CoW at the cost of extra PTEs and TLB pressure, a tradeoff the paper leaves open.
Classifier · dirty-bit scan

An attendance sheet, not a heart monitor

Every interval T, the kernel checks who's been "absent" (dirty bit clear) since last roll call and marks them migration candidates — cheap, but coarse: it tells you who *didn't* write recently, not who's truly read-mostly going forward.

Where it breaks: a student who's quiet for one roll call and then loud right after (a page that passes the threshold then gets written next interval) looks identical to a permanently quiet one until it's too late — the paper flags this as its acknowledged imprecision (§5.2.1).
The full explanation

How it actually works

The Optane failure modes, then the interface and OS mechanisms that replace them.

1 · What Optane's controller cost, mechanism by mechanism

Table 2 of the paper maps four controller mechanisms to four measured overheads:

MechanismOverheadAROM's fix
Cache-line/XPLine mismatch75% write throughput drop; 2× random-read latencyCache-line reads, page writes — OS coalesces
Wear-leveling (opaque)Migration every ~3.4 MB written; ~60 μs tail (>160×)OS-side, paced by tokens
Address Indirection Table76–200 ns added per read; 94% of reads miss the AIT cacheRemoved — no on-module map
Workload-agnostic design~67–70% read-bandwidth drop at 50/50 R/WRead-mostly by design; OS-paced writes

2 · Enforcing AROM: the read/write path

Reads never change: cache-line granularity, straight to media. Writes are where AROM does its work.

3 · The thin controller (device side)

Three jobs only: accept cache-line reads; accept 4 KB page writes; report per-block health metadata so the OS can seed wear-leveling state. The prototype adds two device-local optimizations that don't violate this: an SRAM write-combining buffer that acknowledges writes before the slow NOR program completes (so other reads aren't stalled), and an erase command exposed to — rather than triggered by — the OS, so erases are scheduled during low read pressure. The only structure retained is a small, SRAM-resident bad-block remap table.

4 · The OS side: placement, migration, wear-leveling

LtRAM is a new Linux zone with its own free list, placed at the top of the zone fallback order so nothing lands there by accident. Three policies:

  • Allocation: provably read-only data (code, shared libs, read-only mmaps) goes straight to LtRAM; everything else starts in DRAM.
  • Migration to LtRAM: every scan interval T, PTEs with a clear dirty bit become candidates, migrated in scan order subject to the token budget.
  • Migration to DRAM: a write fault on an LtRAM page triggers the CoW copy in the figure above; back-pressure (admission control, DRAM-occupancy watermarks) prevents a phase-change from exhausting the DRAM free list.
# token-based wear-leveling (§5.2.2)
r = N · E / T     # N pages, E rated erases/page, T-second deployment lifetime
on migration_to_ltram(page):
    if tokens_available > 0: consume_token(); proceed
    else: defer(page)   # surplus candidates roll to next interval — benign, even helpful
💡
Why deferral helps, not just costs: a candidate that survives being deferred and is still read-mostly next interval is a safer LtRAM bet than one migrated immediately — the token limit doubles as a second filter (§5.2.2).

5 · Projected latency, decomposed

Figure 4 splits end-to-end 128 B read latency into interconnect (40 ns, constant), controller, and media. The NOR prototype measures controller+media directly at 500 ns (180 ctrl + 320 media); adding the paper's 40 ns projected interconnect estimate gives NOR's 540 ns end-to-end figure. Reclocking the controller's 36-cycle budget from NOR's 200 MHz to a DDR5-5600 clock instead projects 13 ns of controller latency for the other candidate technologies.

Follow the numbers

Worked examples

Four traces using the paper's own figures.

① The 75% write-throughput collapse

  1. Sequential writes hit ~2.3 GB/s (write-combining buffer coalesces sub-XPLine writes cleanly).
  2. Random 64 B writes each need a 256 B XPLine read-modify-write: 256/64 = 4× amplification.
  3. Amplified throughput ≈ 2.3 GB/s ÷ 4 ≈ 0.575 GB/s — the paper reports ~0.56 GB/s measured.
  4. Degradation = (2.3 − 0.56)/2.3 ≈ 75.7%, matching the paper's "~75%."
🍯
AROM's 4 KB writes are 16× the 256 B XPLine, so they're never sub-granularity — the RMW path is structurally impossible, not merely rarer.

② Why the AIT cache misses 94% of the time

  1. AIT cache size: 16 MB DRAM buffer, entries at 4 KB granularity.
  2. Entries cached ≈ 16 MB ÷ (entry size). At ~8 B/entry (valid+tag+LRU+dirty+ECC bits) that's 16 MB / 8 B = 2,097,152 entries.
  3. Address space covered = 2,097,152 × 4 KB ≈ 8 GB.
  4. On a 128 GB DIMM: 8 GB / 128 GB = 6.25% covered — matching the paper's "~6%."
  5. So 93.75% ≈ 94% of random accesses must miss the AIT cache and pay the full 427 ns path.
💡
This isn't a tuning problem — no realistic on-DIMM SRAM/DRAM cache covers a modern DIMM's full address range at 4 KB granularity. Removing the AIT removes the miss, not just the miss rate.

③ Instantiating the token allocator (illustrative — not paper numbers)

The paper gives only the formula r = N·E/T. Applying it to a plausible deployment:

  1. N = 2.5×10⁸ pages (≈1 TB of LtRAM at 4 KB pages).
  2. E = 10⁵ erases/block (3D FeFET / V-RRAM's endurance class, Table 1).
  3. T = 5-year deployment ≈ 1.577×10⁸ seconds.
  4. r = (2.5×10⁸ × 10⁵) / 1.577×10⁸ ≈ 1.58×10⁵ tokens/sec — about 158,500 page migrations/sec sustained, before banking.
⚠
Caveat: N, E, T here are my illustrative choices to exercise the formula — the paper does not instantiate specific numbers. Treat the mechanism, not this rate, as the paper's claim.

④ Checking the STT-MRAM "9% faster than DRAM" claim

  1. Interconnect: 40 ns (held constant across all technologies, §5.3).
  2. Controller (reclocked): 13 ns — 36 NOR-measured cycles at 200 MHz, reprojected to a 2.8 GHz DDR5-5600 clock.
  3. Media: 20 ns, STT-MRAM's Table 1 read latency.
  4. Total: 40 + 13 + 20 = 73 ns.
  5. Vs. DRAM's 80 ns: (80 − 73)/80 = 8.75% ≈ 9% faster — matches the paper exactly.
🍯
The same arithmetic applied to 3D FeFET (200 ns media) gives 253 ns total, 3.16× DRAM — the source of the paper's "~220% slower" endpoint.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Each answer names the misconception it's checking.

Problem 1 · easy
A page has been read-only for months, then a single application thread writes to it once. What happens, mechanically?
Show answer
The page is write-protected, so the store triggers a page fault. The kernel allocates a DRAM page, copies the LtRAM page's contents, retargets the PTE, and only then lets the store execute. The old LtRAM page is freed and its block scheduled for erase when the device is idle — the application's write never touches LtRAM media at all.Misconception: that AROM lets the application write LtRAM directly, just more slowly. It never writes LtRAM — the write always lands on a fresh DRAM copy.
Problem 2 · medium
Is AROM a form of CXL-style memory tiering? Name the one architectural difference the paper insists on.
Show answer
Not quite — §6 is explicit: TPP, Memtis, Colloid, and Alto treat the second tier as slower memory below DRAM in the hierarchy, where misplacement only costs extra read latency. AROM treats LtRAM as a co-equal main-memory tier where misplacement costs a synchronous CoW copy on every write — a critical-path penalty tiering schemes don't pay.Misconception: that "OS manages a second memory tier" is a single idea. The cost model differs fundamentally: read-side slowdown (tiering) vs. write-side copy cost (AROM).
Problem 3 · medium
Why does removing the AIT also remove the 60 μs wear-leveling tail-latency spikes, and not just the 76–200 ns lookup cost?
Show answer
The AIT and hardware wear-leveling are coupled: the controller migrates data off worn cells by rewriting the AIT's mapping, and that migration is what stalls a concurrent read for ~60 μs. With no on-module AIT, there's no hardware-triggered remap event to stall behind — wear-leveling becomes an OS-scheduled, page-granularity operation (the token allocator) that the OS can time during low read pressure, not an opaque event the CPU discovers mid-access.Misconception: that the AIT lookup cost and the wear-leveling tail latency are two independent problems. They're two symptoms of the same hidden-remapping mechanism.
Problem 4 · hard
A 2 MB transparent huge page has 511 read-mostly 4 KB sub-pages and one write-heavy one. Under the current design, what's the LtRAM outcome, and why is splitting not a free fix?
Show answer
If the huge page is the migration unit, that one write-heavy sub-page keeps the entire 2 MB off LtRAM — or, once resident, the first write to it forces a 2 MB CoW migration, moving 511 unnecessary sub-pages back to DRAM just to fix one. That single migration lands the whole huge page in DRAM, so it doesn't keep re-faulting on every subsequent write — but the entire 2 MB now sits in DRAM until the OS decides to remigrate it. Splitting into 4 KB sub-pages restores fine-grained migration, but costs 512× more PTEs and higher TLB pressure — exactly the cost huge pages exist to avoid. khugepaged coalescing can also silently re-merge a split page and reintroduce the problem. This is left open in §7.Misconception: that finer granularity is strictly better. It trades away the TLB benefit that huge pages exist for — there's no free lunch, only an open tradeoff.
Problem 5 · medium
Why is NOR flash's measured 540 ns read latency not evidence that AROM's interface is slower than Optane?
Show answer
NOR was chosen purely for prototyping convenience (availability, no research tape-out needed), not as an LtRAM candidate — it shares the asymmetry shape the interface targets but isn't in Table 1's technology list. The 500 ns measured controller+media latency (180 ns controller + 320 ns media) includes 16 of 36 controller cycles that are NOR-specific command-pipeline wait states a purpose-built controller wouldn't incur; adding the paper's 40 ns projected interconnect estimate gives the 540 ns end-to-end figure. NOR's 320 ns media latency is simply slower than the real candidates (FeFET, V-RRAM, STT-MRAM). The paper's actual latency claim is about those candidates, validated structurally — not numerically — by the NOR prototype.Misconception: that the prototype's absolute numbers are the paper's performance claim. The prototype validates that the *interface* works; the *projections* (Fig. 4) are the performance claim.
Problem 6 · hard
The token allocator bounds total wear via r = N·E/T but the paper admits it doesn't guarantee spatial uniformity. Construct a scenario where a device could still fail early despite staying within its token budget.
Show answer
Suppose read-only code pages and pinned kernel pages occupy a fixed subset of LtRAM blocks for the device's entire lifetime, while the token-gated migration traffic happens to always land on the same remaining "hot" blocks (e.g., a workload with a small, stable set of frequently-recycled read-mostly pages). The aggregate token rate never exceeds N·E/T, but those specific blocks individually exceed their E erase-cycle rating long before T elapses, while the pinned blocks sit at zero wear — the average is fine, the distribution isn't. The paper flags exactly this in §7 as future work: an eviction-when-wear-gap-exceeds-threshold policy is proposed but left unresolved (when to trigger, what threshold, how to pick candidates cheaply).Misconception: that bounding total wear is the same as bounding per-cell wear. Pacing the sum says nothing about its distribution across physical blocks.
Problem 7 · easy
Does an application need to call madvise() or otherwise be rewritten to benefit from LtRAM placement?
Show answer
No — AROM is transparent by default (§5.2.4). The kernel infers read-mostly status via dirty-bit scanning and migrates pages without application involvement; a process only ever sees its normal virtual address space. madvise-style hints are proposed as an optional, advisory optimization (§7) to speed up initial placement, not a requirement.Misconception: that transparent placement and hint-based placement are mutually exclusive. The paper's default is fully transparent; hints are an additive, optional fast-path.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Tap any to expand.

Q1What exactly is the AROM invariant?▾
LtRAM pages are read-only from an application's perspective. The kernel writes to LtRAM only when migrating a page from DRAM into LtRAM; a copy-on-write fault runs the other direction — an application write to an LtRAM page migrates it back to DRAM before the store is applied. It's enforced in software (write-protected PTEs), not by hardware.
Q2Why didn't Optane just do this?▾
Optane targeted drop-in DRAM compatibility — random read/write at cache-line granularity, transparent to unmodified software. That goal required hiding wear-leveling and remapping inside the controller. AROM abandons DRAM-compatibility as a goal and instead redesigns the OS/hardware boundary, which only works because the OS is willing to change (a new zone, a new fault trigger, a token allocator).
Q3What does the controller still do, if not wear-leveling or remapping?▾
Three things: serve cache-line-granularity reads, accept 4 KB page writes, and report per-block health metadata to the OS. The prototype also buffers writes in SRAM for immediate ack, and exposes (rather than auto-triggers) the erase command so the OS can schedule it.
Q4What happens to a write-heavy workload under this design?▾
Fewer pages qualify as read-mostly, so LtRAM utilization drops and the system behaves like a conventional DRAM-only machine — colder pages get swapped to a lower storage tier to free DRAM, same as today. AROM degrades gracefully rather than penalizing write-heavy workloads directly, but back-pressure (admission control, DRAM-occupancy watermarks) is needed to stop a sudden phase change from exhausting the DRAM free list via a flood of CoW migrations.
Q5Why NOR flash for the prototype if it's not a real LtRAM candidate?▾
Availability and cost — a 256 MB Micron Serial NOR part drops onto the Enzian FPGA board directly, whereas an MRAM or RRAM prototype would need a research-specific tape-out. NOR shares the target asymmetry shape (byte-addressable reads, coarser writes/erases, bounded endurance), enough to validate the interface, even though its absolute latencies are prototype artifacts.
Q6How does this compare to Google's zswap or CXL tiering (TPP, Memtis, HeMem, Colloid, Alto)?▾
Structurally closest to zswap: OS manages a second tier with a cost deferred to access time. But zswap and the CXL-tiering line (TPP, Memtis, Colloid, Alto) all place the second tier below DRAM and pay only a read-side latency tax on misplacement. AROM places LtRAM as a co-equal main-memory tier and pays a write-side CoW copy cost on misplacement — a different, sharper cost model that none of those systems' policies were designed around.
Q7Does this need application changes?▾
No — placement, migration, and wear-leveling are all kernel-managed and transparent. An optional richer hint interface (e.g., database-engine-supplied access-pattern hints, in the spirit of AIFM) is floated as future work for applications that want to do better than the kernel's dirty-bit heuristic.
Q8What's explicitly left open?▾
Three things per §7: (1) achieving uniform wear across read-only/unmovable/page-cache regions, not just bounding total wear; (2) the huge-page/CoW-granularity tradeoff; (3) a richer application hint interface. Plus, per §5.3, application-level and mixed-workload benchmark evaluation — the current numbers are per-access, isolated projections.
Q9Is the 9%-faster-than-DRAM STT-MRAM number a measured result?▾
No — it's a projection built from Table 1's device-level read latency (20 ns) plus a constant 40 ns interconnect and a 13 ns reclocked controller estimate derived from the NOR prototype's measured cycle count. No STT-MRAM device was built or tested for this paper.
