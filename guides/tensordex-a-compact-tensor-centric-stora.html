SOSP 2026 · A Field Guide

Pairing whoever's closest, not whoever's declared kin

Model hubs store hundreds of petabytes of near-duplicate fine-tuned LLMs. The obvious fix — delta-compress each model against its "parent" — breaks down because parentage is usually unlabeled, and even when it isn't, a model's own tensors don't all share one best relative. This is the plain-English story of TensorDex, a storage system that stops thinking in models and starts thinking in tensors.

📄 Lan, Wang, Zheng, Su, Yang, Cheng · SOSP '26 🎓 Some hashing/ANN-search basics assumed — primers cover them ⏱ ~25 min read
🧩

Compress every TENSOR against whichever other tensor it's actually bit-similar to — never assume the whole model shares one declared parent.

1What delta compression is▾

Instead of storing a fine-tuned model in full, store only its difference from a similar reference ("base") — typically a bitwise XOR or arithmetic subtraction. If the two are close, the difference is mostly zeros or small numbers: low-entropy data that a normal compressor shrinks dramatically.

Now you're ready: TensorDex is entirely about choosing good bases and doing this differencing at the right granularity.
2Why model hubs are exploding▾

Hugging Face's storage went from 0.28 PB (2023) toward 1 EB (2026), driven almost entirely by fine-tuned derivatives, not base models — 99.1% of storage, 99.6% of model count by 2025. Users specialize the same base for coding, safety, multilingual, reasoning tasks, producing huge families of near-identical variants.

Now you're ready: this is the redundancy TensorDex is trying to exploit — a mostly-untapped goldmine, since most existing tools compress each model standalone.
3Deduplication vs. delta compression▾

Deduplication stores one copy of each identical block and points duplicates at it — it only helps for byte-for-byte identical content. Delta compression handles the far more common case where two things are similar but not identical.

Now you're ready: TensorDex applies exact dedup first (via content hashing) to remove true duplicates, then delta-compresses everything that remains but is still similar.
4Hamming distance and bit-level similarity▾

The Hamming distance between two equal-length bit strings is the number of positions where they differ. Normalized (divided by total bits), it's a value between 0 (identical) and ~0.5 (random). It's the natural yardstick for delta compressibility, since delta compression works bit-by-bit, not value-by-value.

Now you're ready: TensorSketch's entire job is estimating this quantity cheaply, without reading full tensors.
5Sketching — summarizing huge data compactly▾

A sketch (e.g., CountSketch) is a small, fixed-size structure that hashes elements of a huge input into a handful of counters, letting you estimate properties of the original data (like distances between two datasets) without keeping the whole thing.

Now you're ready: TensorSketch is a bit-level CountSketch: 8 KB summarizes a tensor that might be hundreds of megabytes.
6ANN search and HNSW, briefly▾

Finding the single closest match among millions of items exactly requires comparing against all of them. Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search trades a little accuracy for huge speed, often via a navigable graph structure like HNSW, which routes queries toward nearby points in roughly logarithmic time.

Now you're ready: TensorDex builds an HNSW index over TensorSketch fingerprints (not raw tensors) to find candidate bases fast.
7The facility-location problem▾

Given many "clients" that each need to be served by some "facility" at a cost, decide which points open as facilities and which clients they serve, minimizing total cost. It's a classic NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem — exact solutions (ILP) don't scale past a few hundred items.

Now you're ready: "which tensors are bases, which are deltas" is exactly this problem — that's why TensorDex needs a fast heuristic (FlexSplit), not an exact solver.
8Binary entropy, briefly▾

The binary entropy function S(p) = −p·log₂p − (1−p)·log₂(1−p) measures the uncertainty of a coin that lands "different" with probability p. It's 0 at p=0 or p=1 (fully predictable) and maximal (1 bit) at p=0.5 (a fair coin).

Now you're ready: TensorPred uses S(p̂) — scaled to 8·S(p̂) — as a feature capturing how hard the differing bits will be for a compressor to encode.
The whole story in one place

TensorDex in a nutshell

The problem, the fix, and how the pieces connect.

Fine-tuned LLMs dominate model-hub storage, and delta compression is the obvious way to shrink them — store the difference from a similar base, not the full weights. But real hubs break the naive version of this idea in three ways: lineage metadata is usually missing (only 25.8% of HF models carry it, and cold models miss it >88% of the time); compression is extremely sensitive to which base you pick (a fixed pre-trained base gives 29% reduction on five Qwen2.5 variants, the empirical best-base gives 47%); and the best base varies tensor-by-tensor within a single model — an embedding layer, an attention projection, and a layernorm from the same model may each have their best match scattered across four entirely different fine-tunes.

TensorDex responds by making the tensor, not the model, the unit of storage. Every tensor is tagged with its name, dtype, and shape, which TensorDex uses to filter out structurally incompatible candidates before any comparison happens; a compact bit-level fingerprint (TensorSketch) then lets it estimate compressibility between two compatible tensors without reading either in full; a lightweight regressor (TensorPred) turns that estimate into a predicted reduction ratio; and an incremental clustering algorithm (FlexSplit) assigns each tensor to whichever matching base — the first tensor with an unseen shape and name seeds a new cluster — maximizes savings, revisiting assignments as new models arrive.

The payoff: across 2,890 randomly sampled Hugging Face models (40.11 TB after dedup), TensorDex reduces storage footprint by 70.5% — 37% lower than the state-of-the-art model-level system ZipLLM (53.4%) — while compressing at 22.9 GB/s (3.86× faster) and decompressing at 28.4 GB/s (1.49× faster) than the next-best system.

🧭 Key takeaways

  • Redundancy in model hubs lives at the tensor level, not the model or lineage level.
  • TensorSketch estimates bit-level Hamming distance from an 8 KB fingerprint, not the full tensor.
  • TensorPred converts that distance into a predicted reduction ratio using bit-divergence + binary entropy, no trial compression needed.
  • FlexSplit greedily attaches tensors to the best available base, then periodically splits overgrown, poorly-matched clusters.
  • Base selection is an NP-hard facility-location problem; in the paper's solver-scaling experiment, FlexSplit reaches near-optimal reduction while its solving time stays near-constant as the corpus grows.
  • Result: 70.5% storage reduction, 22.9 GB/s compression, 28.4 GB/s decompression, <0.02% metadata overhead.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about TensorDex

Each stands alone. Every card names where the picture stops being true.

The core idea · Similarity vs. lineage

A sibling by birth vs. a friend who "gets it"

Your declared family tells you almost nothing about who understands you best — that's often a friend, not a sibling. Model lineage metadata is the "declared family"; it's frequently missing, and even when present, doesn't guarantee the closest match. TensorDex ignores who's "related" and measures who's actually alike, bit by bit.

Where it breaks: real friendships involve judgment beyond similarity (shared values, trust); TensorDex's notion of "alike" is purely a bit-level statistic — it has no concept of semantic intent.
TensorSketch · Sketching

Fingerprinting a fingerprint, not the finger

Police don't compare whole hands to solve a case — a compact fingerprint pattern is enough to match identity. TensorSketch does the same for tensors: it hashes every bit position of every element into a small, fixed 8 KB table, capturing enough structure to estimate Hamming distance without ever re-reading the full multi-gigabyte tensor.

Where it breaks: a real fingerprint captures one unchanging pattern; a tensor's "print" is a lossy statistical summary — collisions in the hash can blur two genuinely different tensors together, which is why TensorDex averages over multiple hash rows for robustness.
TensorPred · Entropy as a proxy

Guessing how hard a crossword will be from its grid

You can estimate a crossword's difficulty just by looking at the grid density, without solving it — dense grids with short words are usually easy, sparse ones hard. TensorPred does something similar: from the estimated bit-divergence (p̂) and its entropy cost, it predicts the reduction ratio a compressor would achieve, without ever running the compressor.

Where it breaks: a grid glance can be fooled by an unusually clued puzzle; TensorPred's median error is small (0.79%) but grows at the tail (P99 = 6.03%) — it's a fast estimate, not a guarantee.
FlexSplit Phase I · Greedy attach

Seating a new guest at whichever table they'll enjoy most

At a big party, you seat a newcomer at the table where they'll click best right now — not by rigidly assigning tables by last name. FlexSplit does this per tensor: each arriving tensor joins whichever existing base cluster gives the highest predicted reduction, evaluated in O(log k) time via the fingerprint index.

Where it breaks: a party seating is final; FlexSplit's assignment isn't — new arrivals can later reveal that an existing guest would have been happier elsewhere, which is exactly what Phase II fixes.
FlexSplit Phase II · Splitting

A book club that outgrows one shared opinion

A club that started around one novel eventually has members who really came for a different genre — you don't kick anyone out, you spin off a second discussion group around whichever member best represents that other taste, and everyone's happier. FlexSplit promotes a poorly-matched member to a new base and reassigns whoever benefits, lifting a cluster's average reduction ratio (e.g., 0.45 → 0.68 in the paper's example) without touching other clusters.

Where it breaks: book clubs split by vibe; FlexSplit's trigger is a hard cost test — split only if predicted savings exceed the cost of storing a new tensor in full.
HNSW vs. brute force · ANN search

Looking up a name vs. reading the phone book cover to cover

An index lets you jump straight to the right page; without one, you'd read every entry. Bit-distance brute force reads every candidate tensor pair (up to 35 MB of I/O per query); TensorSketch + HNSW routes through a compact graph in ~8 KB of I/O, over 25,000 queries per second — a >4,000× reduction in I/O for a near-identical answer.

Where it breaks: an index has to be built and kept updated as new entries arrive; HNSW insertion has its own (amortized, still cheap) cost that a static phone book doesn't.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Component by component, following the four-stage pipeline.

1 · TensorSketch — a bit-level fingerprint

A tensor's raw bytes are a flat sequence of p·n bits (n elements of p bits each — 16 for BF16). TensorSketch builds a 2D sketch table F ∈ ℝ^(d×w). For every set bit at position k in element i, a per-row hash pair (h_r, s_r) maps (i, k) to a bucket and a sign (±1), and that sign is added into F[r][bucket]. Two tensors' Hamming distance is then estimated from the squared ℓ2 norm of the difference between their sketches, median-aggregated over d rows for robustness.

2 · TensorPred — from distance to reduction ratio

Let p̂ be the normalized Hamming distance estimated above. Define τ = 8·S(p̂), where S is binary entropy — the per-byte bit uncertainty a compressor must resolve. The predicted reduction ratio is a small regression over three terms:

R(p̂) = α·p̂ + β·τ + γ·(p̂·τ) + ε      # fit offline on real tensor pairs; clipped to [0,1]

p̂ captures raw divergence, τ its entropy cost, and p̂·τ the nonlinear coupling between them (small p̂ causes a disproportionate jump in hard-to-compress residuals). Validated on 5.7M held-out pairs: Pearson r = 0.993, median absolute error 0.79%.

3 · FlexSplit — incremental base selection

Choosing which tensors act as bases is an NP-hard facility-location problem, so FlexSplit uses a two-phase online heuristic operating purely on fingerprints:

PhaseWhat it doesCost
I — Greedy attachEach new tensor joins whichever existing base gives the highest predicted reduction ratioO(log k) via HNSW, k = # bases
II — Multi-center splitPeriodically, poorly-matched cluster members are identified and promoted to new bases if it raises the cluster's reduction ratioCluster-local, no cascading
💡
Why splitting is cluster-local: unlike an ANN index rebalance, the storage-cost objective decomposes per cluster — promoting a base only re-scores that cluster's own members, with no ripple effect on neighboring clusters.

4 · Codecs — TensorX and FM++

Once pairs are decided, either codec materializes the plan. TensorX extends BitX with chunk-level parallelism and byte-plane separation (grouping, e.g., the first byte of many BF16 floats together) to exploit the near-zero skew of fine-tuned deltas — tuned for throughput (22.9 GB/s compression, 28.4 GB/s decompression). FM++ extends FM-Delta with native BF16 support and parallel chunks, raising its throughput from ~100 MB/s to 9,821 MB/s (~98×) while keeping its higher reduction ratio.

🧭
The codec is pluggable and fully decoupled from planning — swapping TensorX for FM++ over the same pairs adds 5.4 points of reduction (65.1% → 70.5%) from stronger entropy coding alone, isolating the codec's contribution from the planner's.
Follow the numbers

Worked examples

Four traces using the paper's own figures.

① From raw corpus to final stored bytes

  1. Raw corpus: 961,509 tensors, 44.86 TB.
  2. Exact dedup (TensorID): drops to 741,689 unique tensors, 40.11 TB. This is the baseline every reduction ratio in the paper is measured against.
  3. Apply TensorDex-FM++'s 70.5% reduction: final stored size = 40.11 TB × (1 − 0.705) = ~11.8 TB.
  4. Compare to ZipLLM's 53.4%: its final size = 40.11 × 0.466 = ~18.7 TB — TensorDex stores roughly 37% less than ZipLLM on the same corpus (matching the paper's headline claim).

② Metadata overhead arithmetic

  1. TensorID: 16 B (xxHash128) × 741,689 tensors ≈ 11.97 MB (paper's Table 2; our own recomputation gives ≈11.87 MB, a rounding-level gap).
  2. TensorSketch: d=2 rows × w=1024 32-bit counters = 8 KB/tensor × 741,689 ≈ 6.08 GB.
  3. Total metadata: ≈ 6.09 GB, against 40.11 TB of deduplicated tensor data.
  4. Overhead fraction: 6.09 GB ÷ 40,110 GB ≈ 0.015% — consistent with Table 2.
🧭
Fingerprinting every tensor in the entire evaluated corpus costs roughly six gigabytes — cheaper than a single uncompressed 7B-parameter checkpoint.

③ The entropy feature, by hand

Suppose TensorSketch estimates a normalized Hamming distance p̂ = 0.10 between two candidate tensors. What is τ, the entropy feature TensorPred uses?

  1. Binary entropy: S(0.10) = −0.10·log₂(0.10) − 0.90·log₂(0.90) = 0.332 + 0.137 ≈ 0.469 bits.
  2. Scale by 8 (per-byte normalization): τ = 8 × 0.469 ≈ 3.75.
  3. Compare to p̂ = 0.40 (much noisier pair): S(0.40) ≈ 0.971 bits, τ ≈ 7.77 — more than double, even though p̂ only quadrupled, showing the steep, non-linear cost of divergence near p̂ = 0.
💡
This non-linearity is exactly why TensorPred needs the entropy term, not just p̂ alone — a compressor's job gets disproportionately harder as bits stop agreeing, well before p̂ reaches 0.5.

④ Throughput speedups, verified

  1. Compression: TensorDex-TX = 22,916 MB/s; ZipLLM = 5,942 MB/s. Ratio = 22,916 / 5,942 ≈ 3.86× — matches the paper's headline.
  2. Decompression: TensorDex-TX = 28,393 MB/s; next-best (OpenZL) = 19,068 MB/s. Ratio = 28,393 / 19,068 ≈ 1.49×.
  3. Search I/O: Bit Distance reads ≈35 MB/query; TensorSketch reads 8.0 KB/query. 35,840 KB ÷ 8.0 KB ≈ 4,480×, consistent with the paper's reported 4,517× (small gap from rounding of the "~35 MB" figure).
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer.

Problem 1 · easy
A model's base_model metadata field is missing. Can TensorDex still find it a good delta-compression partner?
Show answer
Yes — TensorDex never depends on lineage metadata at all. It fingerprints every tensor with TensorSketch and searches the HNSW index over structurally compatible tensor candidates (matching shape and dtype) in the hub for the best bit-level match, regardless of what any model card claims.Misconception: that missing metadata is a special-case fallback. It's the default, unconditional path — metadata is simply never consulted.
Problem 2 · medium
Why doesn't TensorDex just run ANN search directly on raw tensor bytes instead of building TensorSketch first?
Show answer
Two reasons. First, the curse of dimensionality: tensors have millions of elements, and standard ANN search assumes vectors of thousands of dimensions at most — at tensor scale, distance concentration makes raw-vector search barely better than random (Recall@1 ≈ 0.60–0.77 for Raw Tensor HNSW vs. 1.00 for TensorSketch). Second, cost: querying on raw tensors means moving ~35 MB per query versus ~8 KB for a sketch.Misconception: that ANN search "just works" on any high-dimensional data. Off-the-shelf ANN is built for small embeddings; tensor-scale vectors need a dedicated, compression-aware fingerprint first.
Problem 3 · medium
Two tensor pairs have the same normalized Hamming distance p̂, but TensorPred predicts different reduction ratios for them. How is that possible if R depends only on p̂ and τ (which is a function of p̂)?
Show answer
It isn't possible under TensorPred's formulation — τ = 8·S(p̂) is a deterministic function of p̂, so equal p̂ implies equal τ and hence equal predicted R. If two real tensor pairs with equal p̂ compress differently in practice, that's exactly the ~1% median error the regression accepts: p̂ and its entropy are a strong but not perfect proxy for true compressibility.Misconception: that TensorPred is exact. It's a fast, small-error approximation (P50 = 0.79%, P99 = 6.03%) — good enough for relative ranking, not a guarantee of the true ratio.
Problem 4 · hard
Why is exact ILP infeasible for base selection at hub scale, and why doesn't the classical primal-dual approximation solve the problem either?
Show answer
Base selection is a facility-location instance, NP-hard in general; ILP solvers like Gurobi find the true optimum but scale super-linearly, exceeding 70s past 800 tensors — hopeless for millions. Primal-dual gives a provable 3-approximation but requires materializing the full pairwise cost matrix and assumes a static input, which doesn't fit a hub where models arrive continuously and previously optimal bases can become stale.Misconception: that "provably approximate" solvers are automatically practical. Guarantees don't imply scalability or support for streaming/incremental updates — FlexSplit trades the guarantee for near-constant time and true incrementality.
Problem 5 · medium
FlexSplit's Phase II only splits 41.1% of clusters, leaving 58.9% untouched. Is that a shortcoming?
Show answer
No — it's by design. Phase II checks a dual criterion (cluster large enough and inefficient enough) before committing to a split. Clusters that are already small or already compress well near 0.75 gain nothing from splitting, so leaving most clusters untouched means the refinement is surgical, targeting only the clusters that actually mix well-matched and poorly-matched tensors.Misconception: that more splitting is always better. Splitting a cluster that's already well-fit adds a new base (stored in full) for no compression gain — a pure loss.
Problem 6 · easy
A LoRA adapter and a full-weight fine-tune are both uploaded. Does TensorDex need separate logic for each?
Show answer
No — both are just tensor sets with a shared dtype, and TensorDex's pipeline (fingerprint → predict → cluster → compress) needs no pipeline change for either, beyond that shared-dtype requirement between delta and base. Full-weight fine-tunes dominate hub storage (99.1%) and are the paper's focus, but the mechanism itself is format-agnostic.Misconception: that TensorDex is LLM-architecture-specific. It operates on raw tensors and their bit patterns, independent of what kind of model or adapter they come from.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Tap any to expand.

Q1Is TensorDex lossy?▾
No — it's fully lossless. Every tensor can be reconstructed exactly by fetching its base and applying the stored delta; only quantization/pruning methods (ELF, DeltaZip, QStore) trade exactness for extra reduction, which TensorDex explicitly avoids.
Q2What exactly does "tensor-centric" mean, concretely?▾
Prior systems (ZipLLM, FM-Delta) pick one base model and delta-compress the whole target model against it. TensorDex instead decomposes every model into its individual tensors first, and lets each tensor independently find its own best-matching tensor among structurally compatible candidates in the hub — potentially from a different model than its neighbors.
Q3How does TensorDex avoid comparing every tensor pair (which would be quadratic)?▾
It never computes pairwise distances directly at scale. Each tensor gets an 8 KB TensorSketch fingerprint, indexed in an HNSW graph; a query for the best base is an O(log n) approximate nearest-neighbor lookup over compatible candidates, not an O(n) or O(n²) scan.
Q4Does TensorDex require reading full tensor contents at query time?▾
Only once, when the tensor is first ingested (to build its fingerprint and later, its actual delta). All subsequent similarity queries operate purely on the compact fingerprint — this is the whole point of the sketch.
Q5What happens when a base tensor is deleted?▾
Deletion is deferred: the tensor is tombstoned and reclaimed in a later offline maintenance pass. If it was a base with live dependents, that pass promotes another cluster member to base and recompresses dependents against it, atomically swapping the metadata pointer — and the rewrite stays within that one cluster.
Q6Why two codecs (TensorX and FM++) instead of one?▾
They serve different priorities. TensorX maximizes throughput (22.9/28.4 GB/s compress/decompress) via lightweight byte-plane separation; FM++ maximizes reduction ratio (70.5% vs. TensorX's 65.1%) via a stronger entropy stage, at somewhat lower throughput. The pluggable codec interface lets an operator pick the tradeoff.
Q7Does retrieval speed depend on how many hops a delta chain has?▾
TensorDex keeps reconstruction to one hop by design — every delta references exactly one base directly, so restoring a tensor reads only that base plus its own delta. Measured read amplification stays at 1.2–1.4× (median 1.27×) across the corpus.
Q8Is decompression ever the retrieval bottleneck?▾
Not in the paper's tested configurations. Under real storage I/O (S3 or NVMe-backed EBS) with TensorDex-TX, end-to-end retrieval measures 1.96–4.26 GB/s — 3–4.5× below the same decoder's DRAM-resident throughput (28.6 GB/s) — meaning retrieval is I/O-bound, not decompression-bound, for those setups. A much faster storage tier could shift that balance.
Q9How is the reduction-ratio prediction model validated against overfitting to specific model families?▾
A cross-family holdout: trained on Qwen+Llama pairs only (451K pairs), evaluated on unseen Gemma+Mistral pairs (83K pairs). MAE rises only marginally (0.0230 → 0.0265) and Pearson r is essentially unchanged (0.978 vs 0.977) — the predictor's inputs (p̂ and its entropy) are family-independent numerical properties.
Q10Does TensorDex work for multithreaded / non-LLM workloads?▾
The paper evaluates it specifically on LLM checkpoints (Qwen, Llama, Mistral, Gemma families) from Hugging Face; the mechanism is architecture-agnostic in principle (it operates on raw tensor bytes), but the evaluation and tuning target LLM model hubs specifically.
