HPCA 2025 · A Field Guide

Counting every hammer blow without making memory wait for the count

DDR5's new defense against RowHammer, PRAC, gives every DRAM row its own activation counter. But updating that counter slows every access down — and its safety valve can be starved by a clever attacker. This is the plain-English story of Chronus, the fix.

⏱️

Count every row's hammering WHILE serving accesses, not instead of serving them — and never let the safety valve close until it's actually safe.

1DRAM organization: chips, banks, rows, subarrays▾

A memory channel connects to modules, which hold ranks, which hold DRAM chips, which hold banks. Each bank is a 2-D grid of cells organized into rows, and each bank is itself split into smaller subarrays. To read or write, the controller sends ACT (open a row into the row buffer), then RD/WR, then PRE (close it) before a different row in that bank can open.

Now you're ready: Chronus's whole trick is adding one more small subarray per bank — a "counter subarray" — that works alongside the regular ones.
2RowHammer and RowPress — read disturbance▾

DRAM cells sit so close together that repeatedly activating one row (an aggressor) can flip bits in physically nearby rows (victims) — this is RowHammer. RowPress makes it worse by simply keeping the aggressor row open longer. The threshold at which bitflips start is called NRH, and it keeps shrinking as manufacturing scales down (from ~69K activations in 2013 chips to ~4.8K in 2020 chips).

Now you're ready: everything in this paper is about detecting "a row is approaching NRH" cheaply and reliably, then refreshing its neighbors before it gets there.
3Preventive refresh, and why it's expensive▾

The fix for an about-to-tip aggressor is simple: refresh its neighbors early. The catch is that a refresh operation blocks the entire bank — thousands of unrelated rows — for a real chunk of time (an RFM command takes ~350 ns). Refresh too often and you tank performance; too rarely and you risk bitflips.

Now you're ready: the whole paper is a balancing act between "refresh enough to be secure" and "refresh little enough to be fast."
4Critical timing parameters: tRC, tRP, tRAS, tRTP, tWR▾

tRAS: minimum time an ACT must hold before a PRE. tRP: minimum time after a PRE before the next ACT (closing overhead). tRC = roughly tRAS + tRP: the minimum time between two ACTs to the same bank. tRTP/tWR bound how soon after a read/write a PRE may follow.

Now you're ready: PRAC increases tRP and tRC specifically, because that's exactly when it does its counter bookkeeping — the very thing Chronus's CCU removes.
5The RFM command and the back-off signal▾

DDR5 added RFM, a command that hands the DRAM chip a time window (~195–350 ns) explicitly for preventive refreshes — instead of letting the controller guess when to refresh. A back-off signal (asserted on the alert_n pin) is how the DRAM chip tells the controller "I need that window now."

Now you're ready: PRAC and Chronus differ mainly in how they use this back-off signal — how long it stays asserted and how many RFMs it triggers.
6PRAC in one paragraph▾

PRAC (Per Row Activation Counting), added to DDR5 in April 2024, gives every row its own activation counter. When a row's count reaches a threshold (NBO), the chip asserts back-off. Then three phases follow: a short window of normal traffic (activations still allowed), a recovery period (a fixed number of RFMs, NRef), and a delay period during which the chip cannot re-assert back-off until it sees NDelay more activations.

Now you're ready: those three phases — and especially the fixed NRef and the delay period — are exactly what this paper interrogates.
7The wave attack (a.k.a. feinting attack)▾

An attacker who knows how a mitigation triggers can spread hammering across many "decoy" rows so that each round of preventive refreshes only ever catches a few of them — letting one row's count creep arbitrarily close to NRH before its turn comes. This adversarial pattern is the wave attack.

Now you're ready: a mitigation's real security threshold isn't "NRH under normal hammering" — it's "NRH under the wave attack," which is much harder to satisfy.
8Weighted speedup — the performance yardstick▾

Weighted speedup sums each core's IPC under a mitigation, divided by its IPC with no mitigation at all: Σ(IPCwith/IPCbaseline). A value near 1.0 (normalized) means "as fast as an idealized system with no read-disturbance defense at all."

Now you're ready: every bar chart in this paper reports normalized weighted speedup — read a bar at 0.9 as "10% slower than a world with no RowHammer to worry about."
9Subarray-level parallelism (SALP)▾

A DRAM bank's subarrays share I/O but have mostly independent internal circuitry, so under the right conditions two different subarrays can be worked on concurrently — one served, one refreshed or updated — without either waiting on the other.

Now you're ready: Chronus's Concurrent Counter Update leans entirely on this — the counter lives in its own subarray, updated in parallel with the data access, invisible to timing.
The whole story in one place

Chronus in a nutshell

PRAC's promise, its two costs, and the two fixes that remove them.

DDR5's PRAC gives every DRAM row an activation counter, so the chip can tell exactly when a row is approaching the RowHammer threshold (NRH) and trigger a preventive refresh via a back-off signal. It's the most precise on-DRAM mitigation ever standardized — but no prior work had rigorously measured what it costs.

This paper finds two costs. First, PRAC updates a row's counter while the row is closing, which stretches two critical timing parameters (tRP, tRC) — a tax paid on every access that has to close and reopen a row (i.e., anything beyond a pure row-buffer hit), even when no bitflip is anywhere close. Second, PRAC's back-off only triggers a fixed number of refreshes (NRef, e.g. 4) and then enforces a mandatory delay period before it can trigger again — a gap an attacker can exploit with the wave attack to keep some row's count dangerously high, forcing PRAC to be configured for very low, expensive NRH values (as low as 20) to stay secure.

Chronus removes both costs without leaving the DDR5 back-off interface. Concurrent Counter Update (CCU) physically separates counters into their own small subarray (0.05% of a bank) so a custom circuit can decrement them in parallel with the access — no timing-parameter penalty. Chronus Back-Off keeps the back-off signal asserted and refreshes as many rows as actually need it, with no artificial delay period — closing the wave attack's opening entirely.

The payoff: across 60 four-core workloads, Chronus keeps system performance overhead under 0.1% at today's NRH (~1K), versus PRAC's 5.8% — and at a hypothetical future NRH of 20, Chronus holds overhead to 8.3% where PRAC-4 collapses to 78.5%.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • PRAC's per-row counters are the most accurate RowHammer tracking ever standardized — but updating them during row-close stretches tRP and tRC on every access.
  • PRAC's fixed-NRef, delay-period back-off is exploitable by the wave attack, forcing PRAC to secure itself only down to NRH ≈ 20 and at a steep performance cost.
  • Chronus's CCU hides counter updates in a separate, concurrently-accessed subarray — near-zero timing overhead.
  • Chronus's Back-Off stays asserted and refreshes exactly as many rows as needed, with no delay period — provably closes the wave attack.
  • Result: <0.1% overhead at NRH=1K (vs PRAC's 5.8%); 8.3% at NRH=20 (vs PRAC's 78.5%) — with lower energy than the other mechanisms at low, future NRH values (this is not a universal claim at every NRH). Chronus isn't lowest on every axis, either: its DRAM storage is on par with PRAC's, PRFM needs the least CPU-side storage, and ABACuS's storage is significantly lower than Chronus's (Appendix C).
  • Chronus outperforms Graphene, Hydra, PARA, and all three PRAC/PRFM variants at every tested NRH.
Seven everyday pictures

How to think about Chronus

Each picture stands alone — read them in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the picture stops being true.

PRAC's timing cost · CCU's fix

Signing the guestbook on the way out

A clerk who only writes each guest's visit in the ledger as they're leaving makes every departure a little slower — the next guest has to wait for the pen to finish. That's PRAC: the counter update happens while the row is closing, stretching tRP and tRC on every access that has to close and reopen a row, whether or not any row is anywhere near dangerous.

Where it breaks: a real clerk can't be in two places; DRAM can. Chronus's fix isn't a faster pen — it's a second clerk in a separate room (see the next card).
Concurrent Counter Update

Two clerks, two rooms

Put the ledger in its own room with its own clerk, and guests checking in never wait for bookkeeping — both happen at once. Chronus stores each bank's activation counters in a small separate counter subarray (just 64 extra rows — 0.05% of a bank), updated in parallel with the regular access via subarray-level parallelism. The counting simply disappears from the critical path.

Where it breaks: the counter room is still made of the same leaky material — it's still DRAM, still vulnerable to read disturbance, and needs its own (cheap) bitflip protection.
The wave attack

Musical chairs with a slow referee

If the referee only ever pulls one chair at a time and then must wait before pulling another, a crowd that keeps circling can always leave a few players standing right at the edge of elimination. That's the wave attack: PRAC's fixed NRef refreshes per back-off, plus its mandatory delay period, means an attacker spreading hammers across many rows can keep at least one row's count dangerously close to NRH indefinitely.

Where it breaks: real musical chairs has no adversary engineering the music's timing; here the attacker knows exactly when the referee will look away (a documented threat model, not a fluke).
Chronus Back-Off

The lifeguard who doesn't stop waving until the pool is clear

Instead of blowing the whistle a fixed number of times and then taking a mandated break, a good lifeguard keeps waving people out until everyone dangerous is actually out. Chronus keeps its back-off signal asserted and issues however many RFMs are needed — no fixed count, no delay period — so there's no window left for the crowd to game.

Where it breaks: an unbounded whistle could, in theory, be abused by an attacker to hog the pool itself — which is exactly the "performance degradation attack" the paper analyzes separately (§11) and shows Chronus still bounds tightly (≤32% of bandwidth, in theory).
Deterministic vs approximate tracking

A guest register vs a rough head-count

Writing down every guest's name gives you an exact answer to "who's been here the most?" — a rough headcount by the door only approximates it. PRAC and Chronus keep one counter per row: exact, deterministic. Prior academic schemes like Graphene (Misra-Gries counting) or Hydra (cached DRAM counters) approximate, trading some accuracy for smaller structures.

Where it breaks: "exact" costs real storage (one 8-bit counter × every row in the chip); the paper's whole point is that this cost is manageable (0.05% capacity) if you place it cleverly.
The decrementer circuit

A stopwatch built into the finish line

You don't need a person to click a stopwatch if the finish line itself detects the runner and logs the time — no extra step, no delay. Chronus's decrementer is a tiny 21-gate, 96-transistor circuit wired directly into the counter subarray: it completes in 0.627 ns, a small fraction of the 47 ns row-cycle time, so it never becomes the bottleneck.

Where it breaks: the stopwatch still needs the finish line (the counter subarray) to be reliable and protected from its own kind of interference — see "two clerks, two rooms" above.
Reducing NRH → shrinking the problem

Testing a smoke alarm at a lower threshold

An alarm that must trigger reliably even at faint smoke (low NRH) is harder to build cheaply than one that only needs to catch a raging fire (high NRH). As chips scale down, NRH keeps dropping — meaning future mitigations must stay cheap even at very sensitive thresholds. The paper stress-tests both PRAC and Chronus all the way down to NRH = 20 for exactly this reason.

Where it breaks: NRH isn't a single universal dial — it varies by data pattern, temperature, voltage, and even physical row location, which the paper flags as an open problem, not something either PRAC or Chronus fully solves.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Section by section, with the paper's key mechanisms rebuilt as diagrams you can read at a glance.

1 · PRAC's mechanism — per-row counters and a three-phase back-off

PRAC gives every row an activation counter, incremented at precharge time. When a row's count reaches the back-off threshold (NBO — JEDEC defines it as a configurable fraction of NRH, e.g. 70/80/90/100%, but functionally it is just an absolute activation-count threshold, and that's how the paper's security analysis treats it), the chip asserts back-off. Three phases follow: a window of normal traffic (~180 ns, activations still served), a recovery period where the controller issues NRef RFM commands (each refreshing the victims of the highest-count row tracked in a small Aggressor Tracking Table), then a delay period during which back-off cannot re-trigger until NDelay more activations occur.

2 · Weakness 1 — the timing tax

Because the counter update happens while the row is closing, PRAC changes five DRAM timing parameters (DDR5-3200AN speed bin):

ParameterWithout PRACWith PRACChange
tRP (PRE→ACT)15 ns36 ns+140% — counter write-back
tRC (ACT→ACT)47 ns52 ns+11% — same cause
tRAS (ACT→PRE)32 ns16 ns−50% (compensating)
tRTP (RD→PRE)7.5 ns5 ns−33% (compensating)
tWR (WR→PRE)30 ns10 ns−67% (compensating)
⚠
At NRH = 1K, PRAC's back-off fires only ~6 times per million cycles — vanishingly rare — yet PRAC-4 still costs an average 5.8% system performance. The overhead isn't from refreshing; it's from these stretched timings, paid on every access that requires closing and reopening a row.

3 · Weakness 2 — the wave attack

Because PRAC only performs NRef refreshes per back-off and then enforces a delay, an attacker who hammers a balanced set of rows can ensure the mitigation only ever catches a fraction of them each round. Equation-driven analysis (assuming a blast radius of 2, tRC = 52 ns, tRFM = 350 ns) shows: PRAC is secure only when configured aggressively — NBO = 1 (back off as often as possible) and PRAC-4 (four RFMs per recovery) — and even then, the lowest secure NRH is 20, where an aggressor can still reach 19 activations before its victims are refreshed.

4 · Fix 1 — Concurrent Counter Update (CCU)

Chronus stores every row's 8-bit activation counter in a dedicated counter subarray — just 64 extra rows per bank (128 KB of counters for a 256 MB bank, 0.05% capacity overhead). Because this subarray is physically separate, it can be accessed concurrently with a regular subarray via subarray-level parallelism, hiding the counter update entirely behind the ongoing access.

5 · Fix 2 — Chronus Back-Off

Chronus keeps the back-off signal asserted until every row that reached NBO has had its victims refreshed — dynamically issuing as many RFMs as needed, not a fixed count — and imposes no delay period afterward. The paper proves this bounds every row's activation count to at most NBO + Anormal at all times (where Anormal is the handful of activations possible during the short normal-traffic window), which is secure whenever NBO < NRH − Anormal.

// Chronus back-off (conceptual)
on row.count == NBO:
    assert_backoff()
    while any row.count >= NBO in bank:
        issue_RFM()              // refresh its victims, reset its count
    deassert_backoff()           // no delay period — can re-trigger immediately
💡
Why no delay period is safe: PRAC's delay existed to keep the interface simple, at the cost of a guaranteed attacker window. Because Chronus refreshes completely every time it triggers, there's nothing left to protect against by delaying — the next back-off can only mean a new row has crossed the threshold.
Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four calculations, straight from the paper's own configuration numbers.

① Sizing the counter subarray

A bank has 128K rows, each 16 Kbit wide, and Chronus uses an 8-bit counter per row. How big is the counter subarray, and what fraction of the bank is that?

  1. Bank capacity = 128K rows × 16 Kbit/row = 256 MB.
  2. Counter metadata = 128K rows × 8 bits = 1,048,576 bits = 128 KB.
  3. Rows needed to hold it: each row holds 16 Kbit = 2 KB, so 128 KB ÷ 2 KB = 64 rows.
  4. As a fraction of the bank: 64 rows ÷ 128K rows = 0.05% capacity overhead.
🍯
Insight: exact, deterministic, per-row tracking — the most precise kind — costs a bank just 64 extra rows out of 128K. The expensive part was never the storage; it was the timing.

② Finding PRAC's lowest secure NRH

Security analysis sweeps back-off threshold NBO and refresh count NRef. Why does the paper land on NBO=1, PRAC-4 (NRef=4), NRH=20?

  1. Back off as often as possible: a larger NBO means an attacker gets more free activations before the first back-off — so the most secure choice is the smallest, NBO = 1.
  2. Refresh as fast as possible per trigger: more RFMs per back-off (PRAC-4 over PRAC-1 or PRAC-2) clears more of the wave attack's decoy rows each round.
  3. Even optimally configured, the wave attack still lets one row reach 19 activations before its victims are refreshed (Fig. 3b).
  4. Security requires NRH > that worst case, so the lowest value PRAC can be safely configured for is NRH = 20 — any lower, and the wave attack wins.
💡
This is exactly why §6's overhead sweep stops at 20 — it's not an arbitrary round number, it's PRAC's hard security floor.

③ Is the decrementer circuit actually fast enough?

Chronus's counter-update circuit must finish well within a row cycle for CCU's "hidden behind the access" claim to hold. Check the margin.

  1. Circuit critical path (Synopsys Design Compiler, 22nm GF process, DRAM-process latency penalty applied): 0.627 ns.
  2. Row cycle time (tRC) for the evaluated speed bin: 47 ns.
  3. Margin: 0.627 ⁄ 47 ≈ 1.3% of the row cycle — the circuit finishes with roughly 98.7% of tRC to spare.
🍯
Insight: the whole "no timing overhead" claim rests on this single number. A circuit finishing in under 2% of the window it hides in is comfortably, not marginally, safe.

④ How much DRAM bandwidth can an attacker steal?

§11 derives the theoretical maximum fraction of bank throughput an attacker can consume by repeatedly triggering back-offs. Compute Chronus's bound.

  1. Formula: fraction = tRFM ⁄ (tRFM + NBO × tRC).
  2. Chronus's secure config at NRH=20: NBO=16, tRFM=350 ns, tRC=47 ns.
  3. Denominator: 350 + (16 × 47) = 350 + 752 = 1102 ns.
  4. Fraction: 350 ⁄ 1102 ≈ 0.318 → 32% — matching the paper's reported figure exactly.
  5. Compare to PRAC (NBO=1, NRef=4): one PRAC back-off triggers NRef=4 RFMs rather than just one, so the correct bound is (NRef×tRFM) ⁄ (NRef×tRFM + NBO×tRC) — not the single-RFM formula used for Chronus above. The paper reports this bounds PRAC at 94% of bank throughput — roughly 3× worse than Chronus's bound.
💡
Chronus's larger secure NBO (16, vs PRAC's forced NBO=1) is itself a side benefit of closing the wave attack — it directly shrinks the attacker's leverage over shared DRAM bandwidth.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the common misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
Why does PRAC increase tRP and tRC specifically, rather than, say, tRAS?
Show answer
PRAC increments a row's counter while the row is being closed — during precharge. tRP (the minimum time from PRE to the next ACT) and tRC (the minimum time between two ACTs) both include this closing step, so both stretch to accommodate the read-modify-write of the counter. tRAS, tRTP, and tWR actually shrink slightly to compensate, since the row spends longer open before that closing step begins.Misconception: assuming a security mechanism uniformly "slows everything down." PRAC's cost is surgically located at row-close — which is exactly what Chronus's CCU targets and removes.
Problem 2 · medium
If PRAC used NBO=2 instead of NBO=1 (still PRAC-4), would its lowest secure NRH go up or down? Why?
Show answer
Up (less secure, i.e. a higher NRH would be needed). NBO is the back-off threshold — a larger NBO means every row is allowed to accumulate more activations before the chip even asserts back-off, on top of whatever the wave attack squeezes out during the normal-traffic and delay windows. Since the analysis already found NRH=20 is only just barely secure at the tightest possible NBO=1, any looser NBO pushes the required NRH higher.Misconception: thinking of NBO as a performance-only knob. It directly trades off against security — lower NBO means more (cheaper, more frequent) back-offs but a lower, safer secure NRH floor.
Problem 3 · medium
If Chronus's activation counter were widened from 8 bits to 16 bits (e.g. to support much higher NRH values without wraparound), how many rows would the counter subarray need, and what's the new capacity overhead?
Show answer
Metadata doubles: 128K rows × 16 bits = 2,097,152 bits = 256 KB. At 2 KB per row, that needs 256 KB ÷ 2 KB = 128 rows (double the 8-bit case). As a fraction of the bank: 128 ÷ 131,072 ≈ 0.1% — still tiny, just double the original 0.05%.Misconception: assuming wider counters would meaningfully hurt area. Because the counters live in dense DRAM rows rather than SRAM or CAM, even doubling their width barely moves the needle — this is exactly why the paper stores counters in DRAM at all.
Problem 4 · hard
Chronus's security proof only needs to bound each row's activation count to NBO + Anormal at all times — it never needs to count how many total back-offs occurred. Why is that enough?
Show answer
A read-disturbance bitflip only depends on whether any single row ever reaches NRH activations before its victims are refreshed — not on how many refreshes happened system-wide. So the proof only has to show a per-row ceiling holds at every instant: before back-off, a row has at most NBO−1 activations; during the normal-traffic window, at most Anormal more; and the recovery period (which refreshes and resets every row ≥ NBO) guarantees no row escapes above that ceiling. That's a complete argument without ever tallying total back-offs.Misconception: assuming security proofs for shared/aggregate mechanisms need to reason about global counts. Here the threat is inherently local (one row, one victim set), so a purely local, per-row bound suffices.
Problem 5 · medium
Why must the Aggressor Tracking Table (ATT) hold at least ⌊180ns/tRC⌋ + 1 entries?
Show answer
An attacker can prepare many rows just one activation short of triggering back-off, then trigger one, and during the ~180 ns normal-traffic window that follows, push as many other rows over the threshold as tRC allows: ⌊180/tRC⌋ more rows, plus the one that triggered the back-off = ⌊180/tRC⌋+1 rows simultaneously needing a refresh. If the ATT is smaller than that, some of those rows would be evicted from tracking before they're refreshed.Misconception: sizing the ATT to the "typical" case. It must be sized to the adversarial worst case — the same wave-attack mindset used throughout the paper's security analysis.
Problem 6 · easy
Would it be secure to simply disable preventive refreshes when NRH is high (e.g. 1K) to save the little remaining overhead?
Show answer
No. Even at NRH=1K, a sufficiently hammered row will eventually reach 1K activations and cause a bitflip if nothing ever refreshes its neighbors — NRH isn't a performance parameter, it's a physical property of the chip. The overheads discussed in this paper (5.8% for PRAC, <0.1% for Chronus at NRH=1K) already reflect a fully-enabled, secure mitigation; they aren't "extra caution" that can be switched off.Misconception: treating RowHammer mitigation as an optional performance/security trade-off knob you can just turn down. NRH is a hardware fact about the chip, not a policy choice — the mitigation must always be secure against it.
Problem 7 · hard
Pairing PRAC-4 with periodic RFM (PRAC+PRFM) makes performance worse than PRAC-4 alone (§6.1, observation 6). Why doesn't adding a second layer of protection help?
Show answer
PRAC-4 at its secure configuration (NBO=1) already refreshes the victims of whichever aggressor rows have crossed the threshold — and even in that worst-case-secure setup, the wave-attack analysis shows an aggressor can still reach 19 activations without tipping over NRH=20, which is exactly the tight margin the security proof is built on, not a mechanism that keeps every row "nowhere near" NRH. Layering PRFM on top forces additional periodic RFMs purely based on a raw activation-count threshold (RFMth=75) that fires regardless of whether any row is actually close to dangerous, so it performs refreshes that PRAC's precise per-row tracking has already made unnecessary. The result is pure redundant overhead, not extra safety.Misconception: "more mitigation layers are free extra safety." When the base mechanism is already provably secure, adding a second, cruder mechanism only adds cost, not protection.
Problem 8 · medium
Chronus separates counters from data to avoid interference on the critical path. Does that also make the counter subarray immune to RowHammer bitflips?
Show answer
No. The counter subarray is still built from ordinary DRAM cells, so it is itself vulnerable to read disturbance — activating counter rows repeatedly could flip bits in neighboring counter rows. The paper explicitly recommends protecting it with one of three known techniques (a separate SRAM shadow tracker like Silver Bullet, frequent parallel refresh like REGA, or guard rows like GuardION/ZebRAM), noting the cost is small because it only needs to cover 0.05% of the bank.Misconception: assuming "separated from data" means "separated from the physics of DRAM." Separation solves the timing/interference problem, not the underlying read-disturbance vulnerability, which needs its own (cheap) fix.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Twelve questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What does PRAC actually stand for, and what does it do?▾
Per Row Activation Counting — a feature added to the DDR5 JEDEC standard in April 2024. It gives every DRAM row its own activation counter and adds a back-off signal so the DRAM chip can tell the memory controller "issue a refresh command now" before a row gets hammered into flipping bits.
Q2Is PRAC a real, currently-shipping feature, or a research proposal?▾
It's a real part of the DDR5 specification (JESD79-5C, 2024), meaning DRAM and controller vendors can implement it as specified. This paper is the first to rigorously measure what implementing it actually costs.
Q3Why does the back-off signal need a dedicated pin (alert_n)?▾
The DRAM chip, not the memory controller, is the one that knows a row's exact activation count. Alert_n lets the chip proactively signal urgency rather than making the controller poll or guess — the same pin DDR5 already uses for other error signaling (e.g. Write CRC errors).
Q4What is the wave attack, in one sentence?▾
An attacker hammers many rows in a balanced rotation so that each round of preventive refreshes only ever catches a fraction of them, letting the "last" row's activation count creep arbitrarily close to NRH before its turn to be refreshed finally comes.
Q5If PRFM (periodic refresh) has the same wave-attack problem as PRAC, why bother with PRAC at all?▾
PRFM triggers refreshes based on a raw activation-count threshold with no idea which specific row is closest to danger — it's a blunt instrument. PRAC's per-row counters let the chip target exactly the aggressor closest to NRH, which is strictly more precise, even though its back-off enforcement (fixed NRef, delay period) shares the same class of exploitable gap PRFM has.
Q6What's the difference between PRAC-1, PRAC-2, and PRAC-4?▾
The number is NRef — how many RFM commands the memory controller issues per back-off. PRAC-4 refreshes more aggressively per trigger, which the security analysis shows is necessary to reach the lowest secure NRH (20); PRAC-1 and PRAC-2 need higher, less-aggressive NRH configurations to stay secure.
Q7Does Chronus require changing the DDR5 standard?▾
No — the paper argues Chronus fits within the existing interface. The alert_n pin already supports lower-latency assertions elsewhere in the spec (e.g. Write CRC error handling), and RFM's own duration (350 ns) is already long enough for a controller to notice a de-asserted back-off and stop issuing RFMs, so no new signal or timing category is needed.
Q8Why does Chronus still need its own bitflip protection for the counter subarray?▾
Separating counters from data solves the timing problem, not the physics: the counter subarray is still DRAM and still susceptible to read disturbance. The paper recommends protecting just that small subarray (0.05% of a bank) with an existing technique like Silver Bullet, REGA, or GuardION-style guard rows — cheap because the area needing protection is tiny.
Q9Why 8-bit counters specifically?▾
8 bits (256 max count) comfortably covers NRH values down to 256 without wraparound, and the paper reports Chronus's overhead stays under 0.1% even at NRH=256. For NRH below 256, Chronus compares the counter against (256 − NRH) instead of widening the counter, avoiding extra storage.
Q10Why does NRH keep shrinking across DRAM generations?▾
Smaller manufacturing nodes pack cells closer together, increasing electrical/physical interference between neighboring rows. Measured NRH has fallen from roughly 69K activations (2013-era chips) to about 4.8K (2020-era chips), and the trend is expected to continue — which is why this paper stress-tests mitigations all the way down to NRH=20.
Q11How does Chronus compare to Hydra, Graphene, and PARA in one line each?▾
Graphene approximates frequent-row tracking in the controller (Misra-Gries) — accurate but its storage explodes at low NRH. Hydra caches DRAM-stored counters in the controller — cheaper than Graphene but not fully precise. PARA is stateless and probabilistic — nearly free but needs very aggressive refresh rates at low NRH to stay secure. Chronus tracks every row exactly, like PRAC, but removes PRAC's two costs — and outperforms all three at every tested NRH.
Q12Can Chronus's own back-off be abused as a denial-of-service vector?▾
In theory, yes — any back-off mechanism gives an attacker some leverage over shared bandwidth. The paper's own worst-case analysis bounds Chronus's attacker-controlled bandwidth consumption at ≤32% (vs PRAC's 94%), and simulated performance-degradation attacks show Chronus limits worst-case slowdown to 69.9% vs PRAC-4's 97.5% at NRH=20 — better, but not zero, which the paper flags as a direction for future defenses (e.g. detecting and throttling the offending core).

Glossary & further reading

Every term in one place, then a path to go deeper.

Core terms
ChronusThis paper's mechanism: per-row DRAM counters that update concurrently with accesses (CCU), plus a back-off policy with no fixed refresh count or delay period.
PRACPer Row Activation Counting — the JEDEC DDR5 (2024) standard mechanism this paper analyzes and improves on.
CCUConcurrent Counter Update — Chronus's technique of storing counters in a separate subarray, updated in parallel with the data access.
Chronus Back-OffChronus's back-off policy: stays asserted until all aggressors are refreshed, with no fixed refresh count and no delay period.
Wave attack (feinting attack)An adversarial access pattern that spreads hammering across decoy rows to exploit a mitigation's fixed refresh count and delay period.
NRHMinimum activation count to induce the first RowHammer bitflip in a victim row; shrinks with technology scaling.
NBOBack-off threshold — the activation count at which the chip asserts the back-off signal. JEDEC specifies it as a configurable fraction of NRH (70/80/90/100%), but the paper's security analysis treats it directly as an absolute threshold value (e.g. NBO=1, 2, 16).
ATTAggressor Tracking Table — small per-bank table tracking the highest-activation-count rows to refresh during a back-off.
Supporting terms
RFMRefresh Management — the DDR5 command giving DRAM a fixed time window to perform preventive refreshes.
RowHammer / RowPressRead disturbance mechanisms where hammering (repeated activation) or pressing (prolonged activation) an aggressor row flips bits in nearby victim rows.
tRC / tRP / tRAS / tRTP / tWRDRAM timing parameters governing the minimum spacing between ACT/PRE/RD/WR commands — the ones PRAC stretches.
Blast radiusThe number of physically-adjacent rows a single aggressor can disturb; assumed to be 2 in this paper's security analysis.
Weighted speedupΣ(IPCwith mitigation / IPCno mitigation) across cores — the paper's main performance metric.
Decrementer circuitChronus's small (21-gate) custom circuit that decrements an 8-bit counter in 0.627 ns, well within a row cycle.
Subarray-level parallelism (SALP)The property that different subarrays within a bank can be accessed concurrently, which CCU exploits.
RBMPKIRow-buffer misses per kilo-instruction — used to classify workload memory intensity (High/Medium/Low) in the evaluation.
