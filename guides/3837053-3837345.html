Spending I/O waits on cooling, not just throttling

An edge board can't bolt on a fan mid-training. This is the plain-English story of I/O-Cool: two tiny regression models that predict temperature before you commit to a chunk size and frequency, so loading the next batch of data doubles as a scheduled cooling break.

1Why edge boards overheat during training▾

Servers push heat out with fans and heat pipes (active cooling). Most edge boards — Raspberry Pis, battery-powered sensors — rely on a bare heatsink or nothing at all (passive cooling). Sustained high utilization, like training a model, drives the SoC temperature up until the OS triggers thermal throttling: it caps CPU frequency to protect the chip. Beyond slowing you down, sustained heat also erodes MTTF — the hardware wears out faster.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool's whole premise is to avoid ever reaching the throttling point, instead of reacting to it after the fact.
2DVFS — the standard reactive lever▾

DVFS lowers CPU frequency (and voltage) to cut power and heat, then raises it back once temperature allows. It's the default thermal tool on nearly every device. The catch: slowing the CPU down is the entire mechanism — every degree of cooling it buys comes directly out of execution time.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool treats DVFS as a fallback lever, not the primary one — see Primer 7 and the "Filter-then-Select" strategy.
3Dynamic core mapping — the spatial alternative▾

Instead of slowing everything down, you can migrate hot tasks to cooler, idle cores. Hybrid frameworks like TEEM and DRM do this — but they assume idle cores are available to migrate to. On a compute-intensive, chunk-based training job already using every core, there's nowhere to escape to.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool doesn't need spare cores — its two levers (frequency, data granularity) work even when every core is busy.
4The I/O-then-compute chunking pattern▾

Memory-constrained ML training can't load a whole dataset into RAM, so it splits it into chunks: load a chunk (I/O phase), iterate until a stop criterion (compute phase), store the sub-model, repeat, then aggregate all sub-models (Algorithm 1). Both K-means and GMM training on the edge follow exactly this pattern.

Now you're ready: chunking isn't just a memory trick — I/O-Cool argues it's also a thermal opportunity, since every chunk boundary is already an I/O wait.
5The "sawtooth" thermal observation▾

Watching PIGMMaLIOn (a GMM trainer) run on a Raspberry Pi 4 with a dataset-to-memory ratio (N/M) of 5, temperature rises during each compute phase and consistently drops during each I/O phase — a sawtooth curve instead of a steady climb. Nobody scheduled this cooling; it's a side effect of the CPU idling while waiting on data.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool's core bet — if that drop happens for free, you should be able to make it bigger or more frequent on purpose, by choosing chunk size deliberately.
6Two models, two different training regimes▾

I/O-Cool predicts temperature with two lightweight models. The I/O model is trained online, re-estimating its parameters after every chunk, because cooling mainly depends on elapsed load time and needs to track current conditions. The compute model is trained offline, once per platform, from a characterization sweep over chunk size, cluster count, and frequency, because heating depends on several knobs whose relationship is assumed stable for a given device.

Now you're ready: this online/offline split explains why I/O-Cool needs a short calibration period starting from the first chunk.
7Pareto-front, multi-objective selection▾

When two things both matter (chunk size and CPU frequency) and improving one can cost the other, you don't pick a single "best" option — you find the Pareto front: choices where you can't improve one objective without giving up some of the other. I/O-Cool computes this front among thermally-safe pairs, then breaks the tie by preferring frequency, since that's the lever most directly tied to execution time.

Now you're ready: this is the "Select" half of I/O-Cool's "Filter-then-Select" strategy, covered in How It Works.
8Why three different evaluation metrics▾

No single number captures "did this work?" here. TSat checks whether temperature stayed safe. Execution time checks whether the fix was free performance-wise. ARI checks whether chunking at different granularities quietly hurt the ML result.

Now you're ready: expect every result in this guide reported on at least two of these three axes — a thermal win that tanks execution time wouldn't count as a win.
The whole story in one place

I/O-Cool in a nutshell

The problem, the cheap prediction that fixes it, and how it all connects.

Edge devices run data-intensive ML training with no active cooling, so sustained compute drives temperature up until thermal throttling kicks in — degrading performance and, over time, the hardware's MTTF. Existing fixes are single-lever: DVFS reacts by slowing the CPU down (costing execution time), and dynamic core mapping assumes idle cores exist to migrate to (often untrue when every core is already busy training).

The authors noticed something these approaches ignore: chunk-based edge ML training (K-means, GMM) already alternates between I/O phases (loading the next chunk) and compute phases (iterating on it). Measured on a Raspberry Pi 4 running PIGMMaLIOn, temperature consistently drops during I/O and rises during compute — a sawtooth pattern. That drop is free, unscheduled cooling.

I/O-Cool turns that observation into a controller. Two lightweight regression models predict temperature before you commit: an online I/O model (logarithmic decay vs. load time) and an offline-trained compute model (linear in chunk size, frequency, starting temperature, and workload complexity). Before loading each chunk, I/O-Cool enumerates (chunk size, frequency) pairs, runs both models to predict the resulting temperature, discards any pair that would cross the thermal limit, and — among what's left — picks the pair on the Pareto front with the highest frequency. DVFS only kicks in when I/O-paced cooling alone isn't enough.

The payoff: on a Raspberry Pi 4 running GMM and K-means training, I/O-Cool respects an 80°C limit 90%–100% of measurements, improving thermal satisfaction by up to 60% over zTT and up to 45% over ComBoost, while keeping execution time competitive — and, per Table 3, without hurting clustering quality (ARI).

🍯 Key takeaways

  • DVFS and core-mapping treat compute as the only lever; I/O-Cool treats the I/O wait you already have as a second, free one.
  • I/O-Cool models I/O phases as cooling logarithmically with load time and compute phases as heating linearly in chunk size, frequency, and cluster count — these are modeling/fit choices the authors made, not claimed physical laws of the device.
  • I/O-Cool's rule is "Filter-then-Select": discard thermally-unsafe (chunk, frequency) pairs, then pick the highest frequency survivor — not the smallest chunk.
  • On GMM, I/O-Cool hit 90.58% TSat vs. 19.05% (Original), 30.80% (zTT), 45.56% (ComBoost).
  • On K-means, I/O-Cool hit 100% TSat vs. 12% (Original), 55% (zTT), 65% (ComBoost).
  • ARI differs by ≤0.005 between I/O-Cool and the unconstrained original across all tested separations — clustering quality is preserved.
  • Decision overhead is tiny: O(chunk sizes × frequency options), negligible on a Cortex-A72.
💡
For tiering researchers (guide framing, not in the paper): I/O-Cool's two-lever, Pareto-selected control loop (chunk size ↔ frequency, filtered by a predicted cost) is reminiscent of jointly tuning migration granularity and migration-bandwidth budget in page-tiering systems. Its online re-estimation of the I/O model's α and throughput after every chunk echoes epoch-based hotness-decay counters in access trackers like TPP or Memtis — cheap, continuously refreshed statistics rather than a one-shot profile — though this analogy is this guide's own framing, not a connection the paper draws.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about I/O-Cool

Each stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note on where it breaks.

The core idea · I/O as free cooling

Opening the oven door between bakes

Every time you pull a tray out, the oven cools a little before you slide the next one in — and you were opening that door anyway. An I/O phase is the same: you already need the next chunk, so the "door" is already opening. I/O-Cool's move is to decide how wide to open it (chunk size) on purpose, instead of letting it happen by accident.

Where it breaks: an oven door only cools — it does no other work. A data load is dual-purpose: it's necessary work and a cooling window, which is actually a better deal than the oven suggests.
The strategy · Pacing, not sprinting

Interval training, not a flat-out sprint

Alternating hard bursts (compute) with recovery lulls (I/O) lets you sustain higher intensity overall than pacing yourself evenly the whole time — which is closer to what plain reactive DVFS does, scaling frequency down whenever temperature demands it. I/O-Cool keeps frequency high and instead tunes how long the "rest" between bursts is.

Where it breaks: a runner picks rest length freely; I/O-Cool's rest is set by chunk size and storage throughput, not chosen independently — though tuning chunk size is exactly the knob it has.
Why predict first · Filter-then-select

Checking the forecast before choosing your speed

Instead of driving fast and braking hard when you spot black ice (reactive DVFS), I/O-Cool checks the forecast first: it predicts the temperature for every (chunk, frequency) pair before loading, discards the ones that would be unsafe, and picks the fastest safe route from what's left.

Where it breaks: a one-time forecast can go stale. I/O-Cool's I/O model re-estimates itself after every chunk precisely because the "weather" (cooling rate) drifts — it isn't a single static prediction.
Two independent knobs · Dual-lever control

A thermostat that also controls portion size

Most climate control has one knob — compressor speed (frequency). I/O-Cool also controls how much you "cook" at once (chunk size), giving it two independent ways to manage heat instead of relying on frequency alone.

Where it breaks: portion size doesn't change food taste; chunk size does affect data locality and convergence behavior, so unlike a thermostat's extra knob, this one has side effects I/O-Cool must also check — which is exactly what the ARI results verify.
Breaking ties · Why frequency wins

Taking the highest credit limit, not the smallest bill

Among several safe loan offers, you'd take the one with the highest limit, not the one with the smallest minimum payment — more capacity now is strictly better if it's still safe. I/O-Cool's Pareto front can contain several safe (chunk, frequency) pairs; it always breaks the tie toward the highest frequency, since that's the lever most directly tied to finishing sooner.

Where it breaks: a credit limit has no side effect on anything else; chunk size does (locality, convergence) — so the "size" half of the pair still has to clear the thermal filter first, it isn't free to maximize too.
Adaptive granularity · Stricter limit, finer chunks

Smaller bites for a hotter bowl of soup

The stricter the "don't burn your mouth" rule, the smaller the bites you naturally take. I/O-Cool shrinks chunk size as the thermal limit tightens — more, smaller chunks mean more frequent cooling windows, trading a little extra bookkeeping for staying safely under a lower ceiling.

Where it breaks: you can take an arbitrarily tiny bite; I/O-Cool can't shrink below its 1 MB floor, since chunks smaller than that degrade the prediction models' accuracy.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Component by component — the two predictive models, then the online decision loop that uses them.

1 · Architecture — two models, one decision loop

I/O-Cool has two parts. A Modeling component forecasts temperature: an I/O phase model estimates the drop while loading a chunk, and a compute phase model estimates the rise while processing it. An online decision-making component uses both, before loading chunk l, to enumerate every (frequency, chunk-size) pair, keep only the thermally-safe ones, and select the best.

2 · I/O phase model — why it's logarithmic

The authors observed empirically that the temperature drop during I/O follows a logarithmic decay — cooling is faster when the device is hotter and slows as it nears ambient temperature. For chunk l:

T_IO(l) = T_curr − α · log(t_l + 1)     # t_l = s / TP  (load time = chunk size / I/O throughput)

Both the dissipation coefficient α and the effective I/O throughput TP are re-estimated after every chunk, so the model adapts to real system conditions instead of relying on a one-time calibration.

3 · Compute phase model — why it's linear, and offline

The compute model is a multiple linear regression predicting the post-processing temperature from the just-predicted T_IO(l):

T_proc(l) = β0 + β1·s + β2·F_l + β3·T_IO(l) + β4·k

Here s is chunk size, F_l the candidate frequency, and k a proxy for computational complexity — the number of clusters, for both K-means and GMM. This model is trained offline, once per platform, from a characterization sweep that varies chunk size, cluster count, and frequency and records the resulting temperature. The linear form assumes chunk size and cluster count increase workload (and heat) roughly linearly.

📌
The paper does not publish its fitted β coefficients — Worked Example ① below uses small, clearly-labeled illustrative values to show how the two models chain together.

4 · Online decision making — "Filter-then-Select"

Before loading chunk l (starting from l > 2, once a previous chunk's observations exist), I/O-Cool runs:

for (s, F) in S × F:
    T_IO   = T_curr − α · log(t_l + 1)        # I/O model
    T_proc = β0 + β1·s + β2·F + β3·T_IO + β4·k # compute model
    if T_proc <= T_th:
        feasible.add((s, F))                  # thermally-safe

pareto = paretoFront(feasible, maximize=[F, s])
(s*, F*) = pair in pareto with highest F   # avoid low frequencies when possible

Minimum chunk size is fixed at 1 MB (smaller chunks degrade model accuracy); maximum chunk size is the memory workspace available. Because the decision space is small (a handful of chunk sizes × a handful of frequencies), overhead is negligible — O(chunk sizes × frequency options) — and nothing sits in the critical compute path.

💡
Minimum-chunk floor, for tiering researchers: the 1 MB floor exists for the same reason tiering systems batch page migrations into coarser units rather than migrating single 4 KB pages — below some granularity, per-operation overhead (here, prediction-accuracy cost; there, TLB-shootdown/copy cost) swamps the benefit of finer control.
Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

One mechanics walkthrough, then four examples carried through using the paper's reported results.

① Chaining the two models for one candidate pair

Illustrative coefficients — the paper doesn't publish its fitted β values, so these are chosen just to demonstrate the mechanics of Eq. 1 and Eq. 2 together.

  1. Setup: T_curr = 79°C; candidate chunk s = 20 MB; dissipation α = 1.6; throughput TP such that loading takes t = 9 s.
  2. I/O model: T_IO = 79 − 1.6·ln(9+1) = 79 − 1.6·2.303 = 79 − 3.68 = 75.32°C.
  3. Compute model inputs: candidate F = 1500 MHz, k = 10 clusters; illustrative β = (−5, 0.05, 0.003, 1.0, 0.3).
  4. Compute model: T_proc = −5 + 0.05·20 + 0.003·1500 + 1.0·75.32 + 0.3·10 = −5 + 1 + 4.5 + 75.32 + 3 = 78.8°C.
  5. Check: 78.8°C ≤ 80°C → this pair is thermally feasible and enters the Pareto front.
📌
Note the pipeline: the I/O model's output feeds into the compute model as T_IO — you can't evaluate heating without first predicting cooling.

② Reconstructing the "45%–60% better" headline claim

Table 1 (GMM, Raspberry Pi 4) reports TSat: I/O-Cool 90.58%, zTT 30.80%, ComBoost 45.56%.

  1. vs. zTT: 90.58 − 30.80 = 59.78 percentage points ≈ the abstract's "up to 60% better."
  2. vs. ComBoost: 90.58 − 45.56 = 45.02 percentage points ≈ the abstract's "up to 45% better."
  3. Where these numbers come from: both gaps are computed from the GMM experiment specifically — the K-means gaps (100 − 55 = 45pp, 100 − 65 = 35pp) are different, since I/O-Cool reaches full 100% TSat there.
💡
The abstract's headline numbers are the GMM-specific maximum gaps, not an average across both algorithms.

③ The execution-time vs. thermal-safety trade-off (GMM)

Table 1: I/O-Cool 316.83s vs. Original 280.43s; TSat 90.58% vs. 19.05%; TempAvg 77.38°C vs. 81.75°C.

  1. Time overhead: (316.83 − 280.43) / 280.43 = 36.4 / 280.43 ≈ +13.0%.
  2. TSat gain: 90.58 / 19.05 ≈ 4.75× higher satisfaction rate (or +71.53 percentage points).
  3. Average temperature drop: 81.75 − 77.38 = 4.37°C; max temperature drop: 85.09 − 82.17 = 2.92°C.
  4. Reading it: a 13% time cost buys nearly 5× better thermal compliance and keeps the board several degrees cooler on average — the paper calls this "competitive" execution time rather than "free," which this arithmetic confirms. Note I/O-Cool's own TempMax (82.17°C) still sits above the 80°C limit, consistent with TSat of 90.58% rather than 100%.

④ Turning the memory ratio into a workspace ceiling

Experiment 1 uses a 200 MB dataset with memory constraint ⌈N/M⌉ = 10, meaning the memory workspace can hold roughly 1/10th of the dataset at once.

  1. Workspace scale ≈ 200 MB / 10 = 20 MB — this is the maximum resident-data scale implied by N/M, i.e. the upper bound I/O-Cool's chunk-size search operates under, not a chunk size by itself.
  2. Where I/O-Cool actually picks from: I/O-Cool searches chunk sizes between the 1 MB floor and this ~20 MB workspace ceiling, choosing dynamically per chunk via the filter-then-select rule — it isn't fixed at 20 MB.
  3. Connecting back: Worked Example ① uses s = 20 MB as one illustrative candidate within this range, not necessarily the value I/O-Cool would actually select for a given chunk.

⑤ Does chunking hurt clustering quality?

Table 3 (ARI, GMM, I/O-Cool at 80°C vs. the unconstrained Original) across three separation values.

  1. sepVal = −0.3 (overlapping clusters): I/O-Cool 0.572 vs. Original 0.569 → Δ = +0.003.
  2. sepVal = 0: I/O-Cool 0.898 vs. Original 0.898 → Δ = 0.000.
  3. sepVal = 0.3 (well-separated): I/O-Cool 0.998 vs. Original 0.993 → Δ = +0.005.
  4. Reading it: on a 0–1 scale, the largest gap is just 0.005 — the reported ARI values are very close between I/O-Cool and the unconstrained Original. The paper doesn't report ARI variance/error bars, so this is best read as "very close," not formally "within run-to-run noise."
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
Using the I/O model T_IO = T_curr − α·ln(t+1), if T_curr = 80°C, α = 2.0, and a chunk takes t = 24s to load, what's the predicted post-I/O temperature?
Show answer
T_IO = 80 − 2.0·ln(25) = 80 − 2.0·3.219 = 80 − 6.44 = 73.56°C.Misconception: treating the drop as linear in load time. It's logarithmic — doubling load time doesn't double the cooling, because the model assumes cooling slows as the device nears ambient temperature.
Problem 2 · medium
A 100 MB dataset is trained under a memory constraint of ⌈N/M⌉ = 5. What's the approximate chunk size, and is it above or below I/O-Cool's minimum chunk floor?
Show answer
Chunk ≈ 100 MB / 5 = 20 MB, well above the 1 MB floor — so it's a valid chunk under I/O-Cool's constraints without needing to be clamped up.Misconception: confusing N/M (a ratio of data points) with chunk count directly. Since chunks are equal-sized, the ratio applies the same way to total dataset size.
Problem 3 · medium
Table 2 (K-means): I/O-Cool averages 75.50°C with TSat = 100%; Original averages 81.30°C with TSat = 12%. A colleague argues "since I/O-Cool's average is lower, of course TSat is higher — that's the same fact twice." What's wrong with that claim?
Show answer
A lower average doesn't by itself guarantee every measurement stayed under the limit — TSat depends on the distribution, not just the mean. What actually supports TSat = 100% here is that I/O-Cool's maximum temperature (79.00°C) also stayed under the 80°C threshold, while Original's max (85.05°C) didn't. Average and max are reported separately for exactly this reason.Misconception: that mean temperature alone implies compliance. A workload could have a low average but occasional spikes above the limit — which is why the paper reports TempAvg, TempMax, and TSat as three separate numbers.
Problem 4 · medium
The abstract says "45% to 60% better than past work." A reader checks Table 2 (K-means) and computes I/O-Cool (100%) − zTT (55%) = 45pp and I/O-Cool (100%) − ComBoost (65%) = 35pp — close, but not an exact match to "45–60%." Explain the discrepancy.
Show answer
The headline "45%–60%" figures come from the GMM experiment (Table 1): 90.58 − 30.80 ≈ 60pp and 90.58 − 45.56 ≈ 45pp. K-means (Table 2) shows a different, algorithm-specific pair of gaps (45pp vs. zTT, 35pp vs. ComBoost). Both are real results from the paper — they're just not the same experiment.Misconception: assuming a single headline statistic applies uniformly across every reported workload. Results vary by algorithm; always check which table a quoted number came from.
Problem 5 · hard
Why does I/O-Cool's decision-making only start from chunk l > 2, rather than optimizing chunk 1's configuration too?
Show answer
The decision for chunk l happens after processing chunk l−1, using the I/O model's parameters (α, TP), which are themselves estimated from completed chunks. Before any chunk has been processed, there's no observed cooling behavior yet to calibrate against — so chunk 1 (and the decision feeding into chunk 2) can't be optimized the same way. Chunk 1 effectively bootstraps the online I/O model.Misconception: assuming the whole system can make an informed decision from a cold start. I/O-Cool is explicitly online-adaptive — it needs at least one completed chunk before its I/O model has anything to estimate from.
Problem 6 · easy
Why is the minimum chunk size fixed at 1 MB instead of letting I/O-Cool pick arbitrarily tiny chunks to maximize cooling opportunities?
Show answer
The paper states that chunks below 1 MB degrade the predictive models' accuracy — presumably because very small chunks give the online I/O model too little data per interval to estimate α and throughput reliably. So "more, tinier cooling breaks" isn't free; it trades away prediction quality.Misconception: that smaller chunks are strictly better for cooling. I/O-Cool's benefit depends on the predictions being accurate — below some granularity, the model itself becomes unreliable.
Problem 7 · medium
Experiment 2 reports mean TSat of 98.58%, 99.75%, and 100% for thermal limits of 80°C, 83°C, and 85°C respectively, with chunk count decreasing as the limit relaxes. Explain this trend using the "Filter-then-Select" mechanism.
Show answer
A stricter (lower) T_th shrinks the feasible set of (chunk, frequency) pairs — fewer large-chunk/high-frequency combinations survive the filter, so I/O-Cool is forced toward smaller chunks (and finer-grained, more frequent I/O-cooling windows) to stay compliant, which is harder to hit perfectly (98.58%). A looser (higher) T_th leaves more large-chunk options feasible, so chunk count drops and satisfaction approaches 100% more easily.Misconception: that chunk count and thermal limit are unrelated settings. Chunk granularity is I/O-Cool's main adaptive response to how tight the limit is.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Ten questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What exactly does I/O-Cool control?▾
Two things, jointly, before each chunk: the data chunk size to load next, and the CPU frequency to run it at. It picks the pair that's predicted to stay under the thermal limit while favoring the highest safe frequency.
Q2Is I/O-Cool a replacement for DVFS?▾
No — DVFS is still used, but as a second lever. I/O-Cool's filter-then-select strategy prioritizes finding a thermally-safe chunk size at high frequency first; frequency is only reduced when I/O-paced cooling alone can't keep temperature under the limit.
Q3Why does the I/O model use a logarithm instead of a straight line?▾
Empirically, cooling is faster when the device is hotter and slower as it approaches ambient temperature — a diminishing-returns shape that a logarithm captures. A linear model would over- or under-predict depending on starting temperature.
Q4Why is the compute model trained offline while the I/O model trains online?▾
The compute model depends on several knobs at once (chunk size, frequency, starting temperature, cluster count) whose relationship is assumed stable per platform, so one characterization sweep suffices. The I/O model mainly depends on load time, and its two parameters (dissipation coefficient, effective throughput) can drift with system conditions, so it's cheaper and more accurate to keep re-estimating them online.
Q5What hardware was this tested on?▾
A Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (ARM Cortex-A72, quad-core, 600 MHz–1500 MHz), with temperature and frequency read via Linux's thermal_zone and /cpufreq/scaling_cur_freq interfaces, roughly 1000 measurements per experiment.
Q6Does I/O-Cool always run faster than the baselines?▾
Not always, and that's not the claim. On GMM, I/O-Cool (316.83s) is slower than zTT (299.94s) but faster than ComBoost (358.85s). On K-means, I/O-Cool (266.45s) beats both zTT (278.00s) and ComBoost (295.00s). The headline result is thermal compliance (TSat) at competitive — not necessarily best — execution time.
Q7Does chunking at different granularities hurt model quality?▾
Across three cluster-separation settings, the reported ARI values are very close — I/O-Cool differs from the unconstrained Original by at most 0.005 (Table 3). The paper doesn't report ARI variance/error bars, so this is best read as "very close," not formally "within run-to-run variation."
Q8Why does I/O-Cool only evaluate pairs starting from chunk l > 2?▾
The online I/O model needs at least one completed chunk's observed cooling to estimate its two parameters (α, throughput) before it can predict anything for the next decision — so there's a short calibration period at the very start of training.
Q9Why is overhead described as negligible?▾
The decision space is small — O(number of chunk sizes × number of frequency options) — and both models are lightweight regressions, not learned networks. All of this runs once per chunk boundary, off the compute critical path.
Q10What's left for future work?▾
The authors flag generalizing I/O-Cool beyond K-means/GMM to other ML models and other data-intensive, chunk-based algorithms.

From the paper: all table values (Tables 1–3), Eq. 1 and Eq. 2's structure, the RPi4/Cortex-A72 hardware details, the 80/83/85°C thresholds, the 1 MB minimum chunk, the "45%–60% better" headline claim, the O(chunk sizes × frequencies) overhead claim, Algorithm 1, and the Fig. 1/Fig. 3 sawtooth observation.

This guide's framing (not in the paper): the oven-door / interval-training / weather-forecast / thermostat / credit-limit / soup-bite analogies; the schematic sawtooth SVG (illustrative, not a pixel reproduction of Fig. 1 or Fig. 3); the Pareto-front scatter diagram; the Worked Example ① β-coefficients (explicitly flagged as hypothetical); and the callouts connecting I/O-Cool's dual-lever, online-reestimated control loop to page-tiering concepts (migration granularity/bandwidth tuning, epoch-based hotness decay in TPP/Memtis-style trackers).

Related work cited in the paper

zTT: Learning-Based DVFS with Zero Thermal Throttling for Mobile Devices medium

Kim, Bin, Ha, Lee, Chong · MobiSys · 2021

A learning-based DVFS baseline compared against I/O-Cool; operates at relatively high temperatures while keeping execution time close to the unconstrained baseline.

ComBoost: An Instruction Complexity Aware DTM Technique for Edge Devices medium

Choi, Kong, Chung · ISLPED · 2024

The other DVFS baseline; achieves better thermal compliance than zTT but at a noticeably longer execution time.

TEEM: Online Thermal- and Energy-Efficiency Management on CPU-GPU MPSoCs hard

Isuwa, Dey, Singh, McDonald-Maier · DATE · 2019

Hybrid dynamic-core-mapping framework I/O-Cool contrasts against — assumes idle cores are available to migrate hot tasks to.

Thermal-Cycling-Aware Dynamic Reliability Management in Many-Core SoC hard

Haghbayan et al. · DATE · 2020

Another multi-objective thermal/reliability management framework cited alongside TEEM.

PIGMMaLIOn: A Partial Incremental Gaussian Mixture Model with Low I/O Design medium

Bouzouad, Benhamadi, Slimani, Boukhobza · SAC · 2024

The GMM trainer used to produce the original sawtooth thermal observation (Fig. 1) that motivated I/O-Cool.

K-MLIO: Enabling K-means for Large Data-Sets and Memory-Constrained Embedded Systems medium

Slimani, Rubini, Boukhobza · MASCOTS · 2019

The chunk-based K-means algorithm I/O-Cool's K-means evaluation builds on.

Dynamic Thermal Management for High-Performance Microprocessors easy

Brooks, Martonosi · HPCA · 2001

Foundational DVFS-based thermal-management work I/O-Cool positions itself against as a single-lever baseline.
