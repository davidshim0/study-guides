HotStorage 2026 · A Field Guide

Let the I/Os cool the chip while the compute heats it up

Edge boards train ML models with no fan and no heat sink to speak of. This is the plain-English story of I/O-Cool: a system that noticed data loading already cools the CPU for free, and started scheduling around it.

📄 "I/O-Cool: Using I/Os for Temperature Regulation When Training on the Edge" · Mohammedi, Gana, Bouzouad, Boukhobza 🎓 Some embedded-systems basics assumed — the primers cover them ⏱ ~25 min read
🌡️

Predict the temperature after the next I/O phase and the next compute phase before running them, then pick the chunk size and frequency pair that stays under the limit at the highest frequency.
Free cooling first, frequency cuts only as a last resort.

Before you begin

Quick glossary + ten ideas the paper assumes

Acronyms first, then collapsible primers. Read in order the first time, or jump to whatever's unfamiliar.

Quick glossary

DVFSDynamic Voltage/Frequency Scaling — lower the clock (and voltage) to cut power and heat.
MTTFMean-Time-To-Failure — how long hardware lasts before failing; heat cycling shortens it.
GMMGaussian Mixture Model — a clustering algorithm trained here in memory-sized chunks.
ARIAdjusted Rand Index — scores how well predicted clusters match ground truth (higher = better).
TSatTemperature-limit Satisfaction rate — % of measurements that stayed under the thermal limit.
SoCSystem-on-Chip — the CPU package whose junction temperature is being managed.
zTT / ComBoostTwo prior DVFS-only thermal controllers I/O-Cool is compared against.
Pareto frontThe set of (chunk size, frequency) pairs where you can't improve one without worsening the other.
1Why edge boards overheat at all▾

A Raspberry Pi has no fan, no heat pipe, no active cooling — just a bare SoC dissipating heat passively into the air. Data-center servers offload heat with forced air or liquid; edge boards can't. Sustained high CPU utilization (e.g., ML training) pushes the die temperature up until thermal throttling kicks in.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool exists because "just let it run at max frequency" isn't an option on passively-cooled edge hardware.
2DVFS — the standard fix, and its cost▾

DVFS lowers CPU frequency (and voltage) when temperature rises, since power (and heat) scale roughly with frequency and voltage². It works, but every cycle spent at reduced frequency is a cycle of lost performance — DVFS trades speed for temperature, one-for-one.

Now you're ready: zTT and ComBoost, the paper's baselines, only pull this one lever. I/O-Cool adds a second lever — data granularity — so DVFS is needed less often.
3Memory-constrained, chunk-based training▾

When a dataset doesn't fit in RAM, training algorithms split it into chunks that do fit, and process one chunk fully before loading the next (Algorithm 1 in the paper): load chunk → iterate the model on it → store the sub-model → repeat → aggregate at the end. This avoids swapping and keeps good data locality.

Now you're ready: this load-then-process loop is the raw material I/O-Cool works with — it doesn't add new I/O, it just times and sizes the I/O that's already there.
4Why the "load" step cools the CPU▾

While the CPU waits on storage I/O, it isn't crunching numbers — utilization (and therefore dynamic power draw) drops sharply. Less power drawn means less heat generated, and the chip passively sheds the heat it already has. So the I/O phase isn't just "idle time" — it's a cooling window.

Now you're ready: this is the paper's central empirical observation (Fig. 1) — temperature measurably drops during every I/O phase.
5Logarithmic decay (why cooling curves bend)▾

An object cools fastest when it's far above ambient temperature, and more slowly as it approaches ambient — the temperature gap itself drives the rate of heat loss. A logarithmic function captures exactly this: large early drops, smaller and smaller drops later.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool's I/O-phase model is literally this curve shape: T = T_curr − α·log(t+1).
6Linear regression, briefly▾

A model that predicts an outcome as a weighted sum of inputs: y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + …. Each β tells you how much y changes per unit change in that input, holding the others fixed. It's cheap to evaluate and cheap to fit from characterization data.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool's compute-phase model is exactly this form, with inputs chunk size, frequency, starting temperature, and workload complexity.
7Pareto fronts and multi-objective choices▾

When you're juggling two goals that trade off (here: bigger chunks = fewer I/O interruptions, higher frequency = faster compute — but both raise temperature), a Pareto front is the set of options where improving one goal means sacrificing the other. Everything off the front is strictly worse on both counts, so you only ever choose from the front.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool builds a Pareto front over (chunk size, frequency) pairs that stay under the thermal limit, then picks the point on it with the highest frequency.
8K-means and GMM, just enough▾

Both are unsupervised clustering algorithms: K-means assigns points to the nearest of K centers and updates the centers; GMM does something similar but with soft, probabilistic cluster assignments. Both iterate repeatedly over data until they converge — exactly the kind of workload that benefits from chunk-based, in-memory processing.

Now you're ready: these are I/O-Cool's two evaluation workloads, and clustering quality is measured with ARI.
9ARI — did the clustering still work?▾

Adjusted Rand Index compares the clusters a model produced against known ground-truth labels, adjusted so random guessing scores ~0 and a perfect match scores 1. It's how the paper checks that thermal scheduling (different chunk sizes, different frequencies) doesn't quietly degrade what the model actually learns.

Now you're ready: Table 3's whole point is that ARI barely moves across I/O-Cool's different thermal-limit settings.
10Thermal throttling and hardware lifetime▾

Beyond a temperature threshold, the OS/firmware automatically cuts frequency to protect the chip — that's throttling, and it's reactive and abrupt. Repeated heating/cooling cycles also stress the silicon and packaging over time, shortening MTTF. Staying proactively under a limit avoids both problems.

Now you're ready: I/O-Cool's whole pitch is being proactive — predicting temperature before it happens — rather than reacting after the limit is already crossed.
The whole story in one place

I/O-Cool in a nutshell

The observation, the two cheap models it's built from, and how they combine into a decision.

Edge devices run ML training passively cooled, so sustained compute drives them toward thermal throttling. The standard fix, DVFS, only has one lever: cut frequency, lose performance. But memory-constrained training already alternates I/O phases (loading a chunk) with compute phases (processing it) — and the authors measured that CPU temperature reliably drops during every I/O phase, since the CPU is waiting rather than computing.

I/O-Cool turns that drop into a control lever. It maintains two lightweight predictive models — a logarithmic model for how much an I/O phase of a given chunk size will cool the chip, and a linear model for how much the following compute phase will heat it back up — and re-evaluates them before loading every chunk. It enumerates candidate (chunk size, frequency) pairs, keeps only the ones whose predicted peak temperature stays under the limit, and — from that Pareto-safe set — picks the pair with the highest frequency, so chunk size does most of the thermal work and DVFS is only invoked when it must be.

The payoff: across GMM and K-means on a Raspberry Pi 4, I/O-Cool respects the 80°C limit 90–100% of the time — up to 45–60 percentage points better than ComBoost/zTT — while keeping execution time close to the unconstrained baseline and leaving clustering quality (ARI) essentially untouched.

🔥 Key takeaways

  • I/O phases aren't just "waiting" — dynamic power (and therefore heat) drops measurably while the CPU is I/O-bound.
  • I/O-Cool adds a second lever (chunk size / data granularity) alongside DVFS, instead of relying on frequency cuts alone.
  • Two cheap models: log-decay for I/O cooling, linear regression for compute heating — re-fit / re-used online, once per chunk.
  • Decisions are made proactively, before loading the next chunk, not reactively after the limit is crossed.
  • Selection is Pareto + frequency-first: among all thermally-safe (chunk, frequency) pairs, prefer the highest frequency to protect performance.
  • Result: 90–100% thermal-limit satisfaction, 45–60 pt better than DVFS-only baselines, negligible ARI degradation.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about I/O-Cool

Each stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the picture breaks.

The core idea · Interval breathing

A sprinter who paces themself

Sprint flat-out and you overheat fast. Interval training — sprint, then a deliberate walk, sprint again — lets your body shed heat during the walk so you can keep sprinting hard, longer overall. I/O-Cool treats the chunk load as that walk: a built-in recovery window between bursts of computation, scheduled on purpose rather than left to chance.

Where it breaks: a runner chooses to walk; I/O-Cool doesn't add extra "walks" — it only resizes the ones the algorithm already takes (loading the next chunk), so its cooling budget is limited by how the workload is structured.
The I/O model · Logarithmic decay

A cup of coffee cooling

Fresh coffee loses heat fast at first, then more slowly as it nears room temperature — the bigger the gap to ambient, the faster the drop. I/O-Cool's I/O-phase model uses exactly this shape (T − α·log(t+1)): a hot chip cools quickly at the start of an I/O phase, with diminishing returns the longer the phase runs.

Where it breaks: a coffee cup just sits there; the CPU's cooling rate also depends on how the I/O phase was reached (workload history), so α and the effective throughput are re-estimated after every chunk rather than assumed fixed.
Online decision-making · Pareto selection

Choosing an apartment

You want it big and cheap, but bigger apartments cost more — every option trades one against the other. You'd never pick one that's both smaller and pricier than another (dominated); you only ever compare options on the frontier. I/O-Cool does the same over (chunk size, frequency): first throw out anything thermally unsafe, then only compare what's left on the frontier, and — since it values speed — take the point with the highest frequency.

Where it breaks: apartment hunting is a one-time choice; I/O-Cool re-solves this small search before every chunk, since the "market" (current temperature) shifts each time.
Two-lever design · Frequency-first tie-break

A thermostat with two knobs

One knob is a heavy hammer (turn the furnace way down — effective but you freeze); the other is a light touch (crack a window — subtle, reversible, doesn't hurt comfort much). Given a choice, you'd crack the window first and only reach for the furnace knob if that's not enough. I/O-Cool treats chunk size as the window and frequency (DVFS) as the furnace: it exhausts the granularity lever before ever sacrificing frequency.

Where it breaks: a window has no lower bound on how far it opens; I/O-Cool floors chunk size at 1MB (below that, its predictive models get too noisy to trust), so on very hot runs it still has to fall back to lowering frequency.
Continuous re-fitting · Online adaptation

Recalibrating a kitchen scale

A scale that's slightly off after being bumped can be quietly re-zeroed with each use rather than sent back to the factory. I/O-Cool's dissipation coefficient α and effective I/O throughput are re-estimated after every chunk, so the cooling model keeps tracking the board's actual, current behavior (ambient temperature, storage speed, thermal history) instead of trusting a one-time factory calibration.

Where it breaks: the compute-phase model is not re-fit online — it's trained once offline from a characterization sweep over chunk size, cluster count, and frequency, so it can't adapt to a platform or workload it wasn't characterized on.
Chunking granularity · The dial that stays cheap

Watering-can dosing

Water a plant in a few big dumps and it may flood between waterings; water in many small pours and it stays evenly moist — same total water, different rhythm. Data chunking works the same way: the same dataset can be poured through the CPU in a few large chunks (long compute bursts, long cool-downs) or many small ones (short bursts, frequent cool-downs) — the total work is identical, but the thermal rhythm changes completely.

Where it breaks: pours are free; re-splitting chunks isn't entirely — smaller chunks mean more frequent storage accesses and re-evaluating the decision engine more often, which is why chunk size has a practical floor.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Section by section, following the paper's own architecture (Fig. 2).

1 · The pattern I/O-Cool schedules around

Memory-constrained training already follows a fixed shape (Algorithm 1): split the dataset into chunks that fit in RAM, then for each chunk — load it (I/O phase), iterate the model on it until convergence (compute phase), store the sub-model, move on. I/O-Cool doesn't change this control flow; it only decides how big each chunk is and what frequency to run the following compute phase at.

2 · The I/O phase model — predicting the cool-down

Predicts the temperature right after loading chunk l of size s, given the temperature just before loading, T_curr:

T_I/O = T_curr − α · log(t_l + 1)     # t_l = s / TP (throughput)

Both α (dissipation coefficient) and the effective I/O throughput TP are re-estimated after every chunk — a lightweight online regression, not a fixed offline table.

3 · The compute phase model — predicting the heat-up

Predicts the temperature after processing chunk l at frequency F_l, starting from T_I/O:

T_proc = β0 + β1·s + β2·F_l + β3·T_I/O + β4·k     # k = complexity proxy (e.g. #clusters)

Unlike the I/O model, this one is fit offline, once per platform, from a characterization sweep over chunk size, cluster count, and frequency — it doesn't change at runtime.

4 · Enumerate the feasible set, then select

Before loading chunk l, the decision engine tries every candidate (s, F) pair from a small grid — chunk sizes from 1MB up to the full memory workspace, frequencies from the platform's DVFS table — runs both models in sequence (I/O model → compute model) for each, and drops any pair whose predicted T_proc exceeds the threshold T_th. What's left is the feasible set.

5 · Why this beats single-lever DVFS

zTT keeps frequency relatively high but doesn't scale it down enough to stay under the limit (low TSat, competitive execution time). ComBoost scales frequency down more aggressively, buying better TSat at the cost of a much longer runtime. I/O-Cool sidesteps the trade by handling most of the thermal regulation through chunk size — which barely affects execution time or clustering quality — and reserving frequency cuts for when granularity alone isn't enough.

Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four traces through the paper's own tables — every number below is from Table 1, 2, or 3.

① Verifying the abstract's "45% to 60% better" claim

The abstract says I/O-Cool improves thermal satisfaction "up to 45% to 60% better than past work." Where does that come from?

  1. GMM TSat, from Table 1: I/O-Cool = 90.58%, ComBoost = 45.56%, zTT = 30.80%.
  2. Gap vs ComBoost: 90.58 − 45.56 = 45.02 percentage points.
  3. Gap vs zTT: 90.58 − 30.80 = 59.78 percentage points.
  4. Rounded: ~45 points and ~60 points — exactly the "45% to 60%" range quoted in the abstract, measured on the GMM workload.
🌡️
Insight: the two baselines fail in different ways — zTT stays hotter (lower TSat) while running fast; ComBoost runs much slower without matching I/O-Cool's TSat. I/O-Cool beats both simultaneously.

② The execution-time cost of thermal safety

I/O-Cool isn't free — how much execution time does it trade for its thermal gains, on each workload?

  1. GMM (Table 1): original = 280.43s, I/O-Cool = 316.83s → overhead = 36.4s ≈ 13.0%. In exchange, TSat rises from 19.05% to 90.58% — a ~71.5-point jump.
  2. K-means (Table 2): original = 238.00s, I/O-Cool = 266.45s → overhead = 28.45s ≈ 12.0%. TSat rises from 12.00% to 100% — a full 88-point jump.
  3. Compare to ComBoost: GMM's ComBoost runtime is 358.85s — 42s slower than I/O-Cool — while still landing 45 points behind on TSat.
💡
A ~12–13% time overhead buys a near-total elimination of over-threshold operation. The paper frames this as "competitive" execution time, not free — the honest cost of proactive thermal safety.

③ How much cooler does the chip actually run?

Beyond the pass/fail TSat metric, what's the raw temperature margin I/O-Cool buys?

  1. GMM average temp: 81.75°C (original) → 77.38°C (I/O-Cool) = −4.37°C.
  2. GMM peak temp: 85.09°C → 82.17°C = −2.92°C off the worst moment.
  3. K-means average temp: 81.30°C → 75.50°C = −5.80°C.
  4. K-means peak temp: 85.05°C → 79.00°C = −6.05°C — the peak now sits under the 80°C limit entirely, matching its 100% TSat.
🌡️
Insight: K-means benefits more than GMM here — consistent with K-means being the workload where I/O-Cool reaches a perfect 100% satisfaction rate, versus GMM's 90.58%.

④ Does chunking hurt what the model actually learns?

Using Table 3's ARI values across three cluster-separation levels and three thermal limits, how much does clustering quality move?

  1. sepVal = −0.3 (hardest, overlapping clusters): ARI ranges 0.571–0.572 across 80/83/85°C limits, vs. 0.569 original — a spread of just 0.003.
  2. sepVal = 0: 0.896–0.898 across limits vs. 0.898 original — spread of 0.002.
  3. sepVal = 0.3 (easiest, well-separated): 0.995–0.998 across limits vs. 0.993 original — I/O-Cool is actually marginally higher here.
  4. Context: this holds under a memory constraint of ⌈N/M⌉ = 10 — only 1/10 of the dataset resident in RAM at any moment — so chunking is aggressive, yet ARI barely moves.
💡
Chunk size changes the thermal schedule, not the training math — each chunk still runs to its stopping criterion regardless of size, which is exactly why ARI stays flat across all three thermal limits.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
I/O-Cool takes 13% longer than the unconstrained baseline on GMM. Is that a failure of the design?
Show answer
No — it's the deliberate cost of staying under the thermal limit. The unconstrained baseline is fastest precisely because it ignores temperature, running at 81.75°C average (85.09°C peak) and satisfying the limit only 19.05% of the time — accelerating hardware wear. I/O-Cool trades ~13% time for a TSat of 90.58%.Misconception: that execution time is the only metric that matters. The paper explicitly reports thermal-limit satisfaction and average/peak temperature as first-class metrics precisely because raw speed alone hides the throttling/lifetime cost.
Problem 2 · medium
Why is the I/O-phase cooling model logarithmic rather than linear in time?
Show answer
Because the cooling rate itself depends on how far the chip is above ambient temperature — it sheds heat fastest early in the I/O phase and more slowly as it approaches ambient. A linear model would predict the same cooling rate throughout the whole I/O phase, overestimating cooling for long I/O phases and underestimating it for short ones right after a hot compute burst.Misconception: that "more I/O time" cools proportionally more. Diminishing returns mean doubling an I/O phase's duration does not double its cooling benefit.
Problem 3 · medium
Why does I/O-Cool's decision engine only start acting from chunk l > 2 onward, not on the very first chunk?
Show answer
The I/O-phase model's parameters (α, effective throughput) are estimated online from observed behavior — there's no data yet before the first chunk has actually been loaded and processed. The first chunk runs at a default configuration; only after it completes does the system have a real α/TP estimate (and a starting temperature) to predict from for chunk 2 onward.Misconception: that the whole system is pre-trained and ready from time zero. The I/O model specifically needs a warm-up/calibration chunk; only the offline compute model is ready immediately.
Problem 4 · hard
Why does the selection strategy pick the highest-frequency point on the Pareto front, rather than the largest feasible chunk size?
Show answer
Because frequency has a direct, immediate effect on instruction throughput, while chunk size mainly affects the I/O–compute rhythm (and thus the cooling schedule), not the compute rate itself. Maximizing frequency first keeps the CPU running at its fastest safe clock, minimizing performance loss; chunk size is then the lever absorbing whatever thermal slack remains.Misconception: that any point on the Pareto front is equally good. The front only tells you what's non-dominated — I/O-Cool still needs an explicit tie-breaking preference (frequency first) to pick one point from it.
Problem 5 · medium
zTT keeps execution time close to the unconstrained baseline but has a low TSat (30.8% on GMM). ComBoost has a much better TSat (45.56%) but the longest execution time. Why does I/O-Cool avoid this trade-off?
Show answer
zTT and ComBoost each pull only one lever — frequency — so improving TSat necessarily means cutting frequency more, which directly slows execution; that's a strict trade-off along one axis. I/O-Cool adds a second, largely "free" lever (chunk size), which absorbs most of the thermal regulation without touching frequency, so it isn't forced onto the same one-dimensional trade-off curve.Misconception: that better thermal safety must always cost proportionally more execution time. That's only true when frequency is the sole lever available.
Problem 6 · easy
The paper sets a minimum chunk size of 1MB. Why not let chunk size shrink further for even finer thermal control?
Show answer
The paper states the minimum is set empirically "not to degrade the accuracy of the model" — very small chunks make the I/O-phase and compute-phase temperature predictions noisier and less reliable, undermining the whole proactive approach.Misconception: that finer granularity is always better for thermal control. Below some size, the predictive models themselves become unreliable, so there's a practical floor.
Problem 7 · hard
Table 3 shows ARI is essentially unchanged across thermal limits of 80°C, 83°C, and 85°C. Does this mean the thermal limit doesn't matter for I/O-Cool's behavior at all?
Show answer
No — it means the thermal limit doesn't affect what the model learns, not that it doesn't affect how I/O-Cool runs. Experiment 2 shows the limit strongly affects chunking behavior (finer chunks under stricter limits) and operating frequency (fewer reductions needed under looser limits). ARI is invariant because each chunk still trains to its stopping criterion regardless of how it was scheduled thermally.Misconception: conflating "doesn't affect output quality" with "doesn't affect system behavior." The thermal limit reshapes the schedule; it just doesn't leak into the model's correctness.
Problem 8 · medium
A colleague argues dynamic core mapping (migrating tasks across cores to spread heat) would make I/O-Cool unnecessary. What does the paper say about that class of approach?
Show answer
The paper notes that dynamic core-mapping frameworks like TEEM and DRM typically rely on offline characterization or simulation and, crucially, assume idle cores are available to migrate work onto — an assumption that doesn't hold for compute-intensive workloads like sustained ML training, which tend to keep all cores busy.Misconception: that spatial load-balancing (moving heat around the chip) is a substitute for reducing total heat generated. It doesn't help when there's nowhere cooler to migrate to.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Tap any to expand.

Q1What's the one-sentence difference between I/O-Cool and zTT/ComBoost?▾
zTT and ComBoost only adjust CPU frequency (DVFS). I/O-Cool adjusts frequency and the size of data chunks, using the natural cooling that happens while loading data as a second, largely performance-free thermal lever.
Q2Why can't edge devices just add a fan or heat sink?▾
Cost, size, power budget, and deployment constraints — active cooling infrastructure common in data centers isn't typically viable on small, passively-cooled embedded boards like a Raspberry Pi, which is exactly the class of device this paper targets.
Q3How does I/O-Cool know the temperature effect of a chunk before loading it?▾
It doesn't measure it directly in advance — it predicts it, chaining two lightweight regression models: a logarithmic model estimates the temperature right after loading (I/O phase), then a linear model estimates the temperature after processing that chunk (compute phase), given the chosen frequency.
Q4What happens if no (chunk size, frequency) pair is predicted to stay under the limit?▾
The paper doesn't detail an explicit fallback for this edge case; in practice the smallest chunk size (1MB) at the lowest available frequency represents the most thermally conservative option in the search grid.
Q5Does a smaller chunk always mean a cooler run?▾
Not necessarily in isolation — smaller chunks mean more frequent I/O phases (more cooling opportunities) but also more frequent, shorter compute bursts; the net effect on total execution time and peak temperature is what the decision engine actually evaluates, pair by pair.
Q6Why prioritize frequency over chunk size when both are on the Pareto front?▾
To minimize performance loss. Chunk size mainly reshapes the I/O–compute rhythm; frequency directly sets the compute rate. Keeping frequency as high as thermally possible protects execution time, with chunk size absorbing the remaining thermal adjustment.
Q7Does I/O-Cool hurt the quality of the trained clustering model?▾
No — Table 3 shows ARI stays within about 0.002–0.003 of the unconstrained baseline across all tested thermal limits and dataset separations, because each chunk still trains to its stopping criterion regardless of its size.
Q8Why does I/O-Cool sometimes run slower than zTT?▾
zTT tolerates being hotter more often (lower TSat) in exchange for staying closer to the unconstrained baseline's speed. I/O-Cool accepts a modest overhead specifically to buy a much higher TSat — the two systems are optimizing different points on the safety/speed trade-off.
Q9How much runtime overhead does the decision engine itself add?▾
The paper describes it as negligible — the enumeration runs over a small decision space (candidate chunk sizes × frequency options) using lightweight online computations, once per chunk boundary, not on any hot path.
Q10Is I/O-Cool specific to K-means and GMM?▾
The evaluation covers K-means and GMM, but the mechanism generalizes to any chunk-based, memory-constrained training pattern (Algorithm 1) — the compute-phase model's complexity proxy k is explicitly designed to be swapped for whatever parameter characterizes a different algorithm's workload.
Q11What hardware was this evaluated on?▾
A Raspberry Pi 4 Model B (quad-core ARM Cortex-A72, 600MHz–1500MHz), reading temperature and frequency through the Linux thermal_zone and cpufreq/scaling_cur_freq interfaces — standard, low-cost embedded hardware.
Q12What's left for future work?▾
The authors state they plan to generalize I/O-Cool beyond K-means/GMM to other ML models and other data-intensive algorithms that follow the same I/O–compute alternation pattern.
