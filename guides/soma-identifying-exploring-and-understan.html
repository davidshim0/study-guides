HPCA 2025 · A Field Guide

Deciding when a chip talks to DRAM — not just whether it fuses layers

On-chip buffers have grown so large that tuning a single layer's tiling barely moves DRAM traffic anymore. The opportunity left on the table is timing: exactly when each tensor should be prefetched or written back. This is the plain-English tour of SoMa, the framework that searches that whole space.

📄 "SoMa: Identifying, Exploring, and Understanding the DRAM Communication Scheduling Space for DNN Accelerators" · Cai et al., HPCA 2025 🎓 Some dataflow/accelerator basics assumed — the primers cover them ⏱ ~25 min read
🗓️

Before you begin

Quick glossary, then the concepts that unlock the paper

First the acronym soup, defined once so you never have to guess; then eight primer cards you can open in any order.

Quick glossary of acronyms
SoMaThe paper's end-to-end scheduling framework — the thing this guide is about.
CoccoThe prior SOTA layer-fusion framework SoMa is compared against throughout.
GBUFGlobal Buffer — the large on-chip SRAM shared by all cores, sitting between DRAM and the cores.
LFA / DLSALayer-Fusion-related Attributes / DRAM-Load-and-Store-related Attributes — the two halves of the paper's notation.
FLG / LGFine-grained Layer-fusion Group / (DRAM-cut) Layer-fusion Group — chunks of layers computed together on-chip.
FLC / DRAM CutFine-grained Layer-fusion Cut (splits FLGs) and its subset, the DRAM Cut (splits LGs, forcing a DRAM round-trip).

1The accelerator template: DRAM, GBUF, cores▾

SoMa targets a generic large-scale DNN accelerator: DRAM (off-chip, slow, high-capacity) feeds a shared Global Buffer (GBUF) on-chip, which feeds several cores, each with small private buffers (WL0/AL0/OL0) next to a PE Array (for GEMM/Conv) and a Vector Unit (for pooling, norm, etc.).

Every hardware behavior is abstracted to three instructions: load (DRAM→GBUF), store (GBUF→DRAM), and compute. This is exactly analogous to a CPU/DRAM system's read/write/execute — just with an explicitly-managed buffer instead of a cache.

Now you're ready: everything SoMa schedules is just the timing of load/store instructions relative to compute — nothing more exotic than that.
2Layer fusion — reusing the GBUF instead of round-tripping to DRAM▾

Without fusion, each layer finishes entirely, writes its output feature map (ofmap) to DRAM, and the next layer reads it back in. Layer fusion instead keeps that ofmap in the GBUF and feeds it straight to the next layer's compute — no DRAM round trip.

This only works while both layers' relevant data fit in the buffer at once, so fusing more layers trades DRAM traffic for buffer occupancy.

Now you're ready: "which layers to fuse" is only one knob. SoMa's point is that far more knobs exist and interact.
3Tiles and Tiling Number — the granularity of fused computation▾

Inside a fused group of layers, you don't compute one whole layer before starting the next — you slice each layer into tiles and pipe tiles through layer-by-layer. The Tiling Number is how many tiles each layer is split into.

Finer tiles (more of them) use less buffer at once, but convolution/pooling layers need extra "halo" overlap at tile borders, so more tiles can mean more recomputed/re-fetched data and less on-chip reuse per tile.

Now you're ready: Tiling Number is a buffer-vs-overhead knob, and picking it heuristically (as Cocco does, from core-array parallelism alone) leaves performance on the table.
4Compute-bound vs. DRAM-bound tiles▾

A tile that loads weights (the first tile of a layer) has high DRAM demand relative to its compute; a later tile of the same layer, needing no weights and often no fmap load (thanks to fusion), can have almost zero DRAM demand for a similar amount of compute.

Across a whole fused network these two extremes multiply — you get a scattered mix of very DRAM-heavy and very compute-heavy tiles, with few in between.

Now you're ready: this imbalance (paper's Fig. 3) is the entire motivation for prefetching and delayed storing — idle DRAM time next to a compute-heavy tile can be spent moving data a DRAM-heavy tile will need later.
5Double buffering — the traditional (limited) fix▾

The standard strategy prefetches a tile's inputs while the previous tile computes, and stores a tile's outputs while the next tile computes — one tile of lookahead, always.

When DRAM demand is this imbalanced, one tile of lookahead isn't enough room to hide a big weight load — so compute stalls waiting for DRAM, even while DRAM sat idle several tiles earlier.

Now you're ready: "prefetching and delayed storing" in this paper means loosening that one-tile window into a fully adjustable schedule.
6Tensor-centric Notation, in one sentence▾

SoMa encodes any scheduling scheme as six attributes in two groups: LFA (Computing Order, Fine-grained Layer-fusion Cut, Tiling Number, DRAM Cut) decides what gets computed together and when a DRAM round-trip is forced; DLSA (DRAM Tensor Order, Living Duration) decides the exact order and timing window for every load/store.

Parsing LFA gives you the compute schedule and which tensors touch DRAM; parsing DLSA on top gives you exactly when each of those DRAM accesses happens.

Now you're ready: the whole "DRAM Communication Scheduling Space" is just the product of these six attributes' possible values.
7Simulated annealing, briefly▾

Simulated annealing (SA) searches a huge discrete space by repeatedly making a small random change, keeping it if it's better, and sometimes keeping it even if it's worse — with that "sometimes" probability shrinking over time (cooling), so early exploration gives way to late-stage refinement.

SoMa runs SA twice: once over LFA, once over DLSA — because a single LFA change can invalidate a carefully-tuned DLSA solution, so mixing them in one search is unproductive.

Now you're ready: SoMa's "two-stage" framework is exactly two separate SA runs, connected by a Buffer Allocator that referees how much buffer each stage gets.
8The optimization goal: Energy × Delay▾

SoMa's cost function is Energyn × Delaym for a full batch of samples, with n and m tunable toward energy or latency priorities. The paper's main experiments use n = m = 1, treating both equally.

"Computing Resources Utilization" — used throughout the results — is just actual throughput divided by peak theoretical throughput; it's the paper's stand-in for "how close to ideal is this schedule."

Now you're ready: every result you'll see is ultimately a change in this one cost function, decomposed into energy and utilization/latency terms.
The whole story in one place

SoMa in a nutshell

Why single-layer tuning stopped mattering, what opportunity that leaves behind, and how SoMa captures it.

On-chip buffers have grown to tens or even hundreds of megabytes, large enough to hold most single layers' weights outright. As a direct result, "how do I tile a single layer optimally?" — decades of prior dataflow research — now moves DRAM traffic far less than it used to. The frontier has shifted to a coarser question: how do multiple layers share the buffer, and when exactly does each tensor cross the DRAM boundary?

Layer fusion answers part of that: keep an early layer's output on-chip and feed it directly to the next layer, skipping a store-then-reload round trip. The SOTA framework Cocco explores only which layers to fuse, using heuristic tiling and a classic one-tile-lookahead double buffer for everything else — and even that narrow slice bought 1.89%–50.33% speedups. The paper's core observation is that fusion, by removing the "average" tiles' DRAM demand, makes the remaining demand far more uneven: weight-loading tiles get DRAM-heavy, everything else gets DRAM-free (see the spread in Fig. 3). A fixed one-tile double buffer can't absorb that unevenness — DRAM sits idle at some moments and stalls compute at others.

SoMa's fix: define the full DRAM Communication Scheduling Space with a Tensor-centric Notation (six attributes across the layer-fusion and DRAM-load/store dimensions), then search it with a two-stage simulated-annealing framework governed by a Buffer Allocator that arbitrates buffer budget between the two stages.

The payoff: averaged over 20 workload/hardware combinations, SoMa delivers 2.11× the performance and 37.3% less energy than Cocco — with the second (timing) stage alone adding a further 1.16× on top of the first (fusion) stage's 1.82×, at under 1% off the theoretical-maximum utilization ceiling.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • Bigger buffers make single-layer dataflow tuning matter less; the bottleneck moved to cross-layer fusion + DRAM timing.
  • Cocco explores 2 of the notation's 6 attributes (Computing Order, DRAM Cut); the other 4 — including Tiling Number and exact load/store timing — are fixed by heuristics.
  • After fusion, per-tile DRAM demand becomes highly imbalanced — some tiles all-DRAM, many tiles none — which a fixed one-tile double buffer can't absorb.
  • Tensor-centric Notation: LFA (what/when to compute together) + DLSA (exact DRAM timing) = the whole space.
  • SoMa = Buffer Allocator + two-stage SA (LFA stage, then DLSA stage) + an accurate tile/tensor-level Evaluator.
  • Result: 2.11× performance, 37.3% energy reduction vs. Cocco on average; near-zero gain on LLM decode, which is purely bandwidth-bound.
🧠
For a memory-tiering reader: SoMa's central trade — decide the timing of a data movement based on real-time bandwidth idleness, not a fixed rule — is structurally the same problem OS-managed DRAM/NVM or CXL tiering faces when deciding when to prefetch, promote, or demote a page rather than only which pages are hot. The six-attribute notation plays the role a tiering policy's parameters (hotness threshold, batch size, migration timing) play in systems like TPP or Memtis: it turns "when to move data" from a hand-tuned heuristic into an explicitly searchable space. This connection is our framing, not a claim made by the paper.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about SoMa

Each picture stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the picture breaks.

The core idea · Layer fusion

Passing the baton, not mailing the package

Two relay runners hand the baton directly to each other rather than mailing it to a warehouse and having the next runner pick it up there. Layer fusion keeps a layer's output in the on-chip buffer and hands it straight to the next layer's compute — skipping the "mail it to DRAM and fetch it back" round trip Cocco and SoMa both try to avoid whenever the buffer allows it.

Where it breaks: a relay only has one baton; a fused layer group has many tensors (weights, ifmaps, ofmaps) with different lifetimes, all fighting for the same limited buffer at once — that competition is what the rest of the notation manages.
Why timing matters · Prefetch & delayed storing

A dinner party with one strict rule

Traditional double buffering is a caterer who only ever fetches the next course while the current one is being eaten, and only ever clears a plate for the next diner — one course of lookahead, always. If the next course is a huge roast, that one-course window isn't enough to cook it in time, and diners wait even though the kitchen sat idle two courses earlier. SoMa is the caterer who plans the whole menu's shopping and clearing schedule up front.

Where it breaks: a caterer plans once; SoMa's schedule must also respect strict data dependencies (a tensor can't be fetched before its "Start" tile, or held past its "End" tile) — it isn't free-form, just far less constrained than one-tile lookahead.
The notation · Six attributes

A six-knob mixing board

A sound engineer doesn't master a track with one fader — they balance six independent controls together. SoMa's Tensor-centric Notation is the same: Computing Order, FLC, Tiling Number, DRAM Cut (the LFA "what/when to fuse" knobs) plus DRAM Tensor Order and Living Duration (the DLSA "exact DRAM timing" knobs). Cocco effectively only touches two of the six faders and leaves the rest at a fixed heuristic setting.

Where it breaks: mixing-board faders are independent; these six attributes are coupled — changing Tiling Number changes which DRAM tensors even exist, which is exactly why SoMa searches them in two separate stages rather than all six at once.
The search · Simulated annealing

Slowly cooling metal into shape

A blacksmith shaping metal by annealing lets it flex a lot while hot (accepting rough, even worse-looking changes) and locks in shape as it cools. SoMa's SA does the same over scheduling encodings: early on it accepts many worse-cost moves to escape bad local shapes, and as its "temperature" cools it only accepts improvements — settling into a strong final schedule.

Where it breaks: metal has one shape at the end; SoMa runs two separate annealing processes (LFA, then DLSA) with a Buffer Allocator negotiating between them, so the final "shape" is a joint outcome of two cooling schedules, not one.
Sharing resources · Buffer Allocator

Two chefs sharing one pantry

Two chefs (the LFA and DLSA search stages) share one pantry (the on-chip buffer). Give the first chef the whole pantry and the second has no room to stage ingredients for clever timing tricks. SoMa's Buffer Allocator repeatedly trims the first chef's pantry share by 10% and re-runs both, keeping whichever split produced the best overall meal, stopping once trimming further stops helping.

Where it breaks: real chefs negotiate once; the Buffer Allocator's iterative 10%-step search can take many full two-stage passes, which is exactly why SoMa's runtime (hours, sometimes ~2 days for large batch sizes) is dominated by this outer loop.
The LLM twist · Decode bottleneck

A guest list that eventually needs its own truck

Catering for more guests usually just means bigger pots (better compute utilization per byte moved) — until the guest list itself (the KV cache, growing with batch size) becomes as bulky as the ingredients (the weights). At that point, hauling the guest list starts to dominate the trip, and adding more guests stops paying off the way it used to.

Where it breaks: ingredients are fixed size regardless of guest count; weights genuinely are fixed per model, which is precisely why this analogy captures the KV-cache-vs-weights crossover so directly rather than approximately.
The full explanation

How it actually works

From the notation's six attributes to the two-stage search that fills them in.

1 · Why the traditional double buffer stalls

Under one-tile-lookahead double buffering, a tile can only start once its own prefetch has finished, and a store can only begin once its producing tile is done. When DRAM demand is spread unevenly across tiles (heavy weight-loads for some, none for others), that one-tile window is either wastefully idle or badly insufficient — never both balanced. The paper reports Cocco-scheduled DRAM/compute utilization as low as ~52.7%/62.6% and ~72.5%/45.8% on its example cases: neither resource is close to fully used.

2 · Parsing the Tensor-centric Notation (LFA then DLSA)

Parsing happens in two passes. First, Computing Order fixes a dependency-respecting layer sequence; FLC cuts it into Fine-grained Layer-fusion Groups; each FLG's Tiling Number sets its computing granularity; and DRAM Cut (a subset of FLC) marks which FLG boundaries force an actual DRAM round trip. This pass alone yields the full compute schedule and the complete list of tensors that must touch DRAM (weight loads, boundary-crossing ifmap loads, boundary-crossing ofmap stores).

Second, DRAM Tensor Order fixes the sequence those DRAM tensors are issued in, and each tensor's Living Duration (Start, End) — a tile-ID pair — fixes both its buffer occupancy window and its earliest/latest allowed transfer time. Only after both passes does an encoding become an actual, simulatable hardware timeline.

3 · The SoMa framework: Buffer Allocator + two SA stages + Evaluator

The Buffer Allocator runs the outer loop. On the first pass it lets both stages use the full hardware buffer, records the first stage's peak usage (Buffermax) and the resulting cost. On each subsequent pass it shrinks stage 1's buffer budget by 10% of Buffermax and re-runs both stages; if the new total cost beats the best so far, it becomes the new best. It stops once two consecutive shrinks fail to improve on the best cost.

Within each pass, Stage 1 (LFA search) starts from "no fusion, minimum tiling" and applies four operators — change Computing Order, change Tiling Number (×2 or ÷2), add/delete an FLC, add/delete a DRAM Cut — under a classic double-buffer DLSA, for 100 × #layers iterations. Stage 2 (DLSA search) then fixes that LFA and applies two operators — change DRAM Tensor Order, change a tensor's Living Duration — for 1000 × #DRAM tensors iterations, with tensors chosen proportional to their size.

# SA acceptance rule, both stages
if cost(new) < cost(old): accept
else: accept with probability p = exp((cost(old) - cost(new)) / (cost(old) * T_n))
T_n = T_0 * (1 - n/N) / (1 + α * n/N)   # cools toward 0 as n → N
💡
Why two stages, not one search over all six attributes? An LFA change (adding a DRAM Cut, say) changes which tensors exist and their dependencies, invalidating a tuned DLSA solution outright. Separating the stages lets each search converge on a stable target instead of chasing a moving one — and the paper observes a bonus: minimizing DRAM access first (Stage 1) leaves purer, more schedulable compute time for Stage 2 to exploit.

4 · The Evaluator — turning a schedule into a number

Evaluation is local-to-global. Each compute tile is scored by a classic dataflow scheduler/evaluator (Timeloop/Interstellar-style) for its energy and time. Each DRAM tensor's energy is data volume × unit energy; its time is data volume ÷ bandwidth. Global runtime then enforces dependencies: a DRAM tensor starts only once the previous DRAM tensor in order finishes and its Living-Duration Start has been reached (and, for ofmaps, only once its producing tile is done); a compute tile starts only once its inputs are ready and any DRAM tensor with an End at or before that tile has completed.

Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four calculations straight from the paper's reported results, carried through fully.

① Multiplying the two stages: 1.82× × 1.16× = 2.11×

The paper reports Stage 1 alone gives 1.82× performance over Cocco; Stage 2 then adds a further 1.16× on top of Stage 1. What's the combined speedup, and does it match the paper's headline number?

  1. Stage 1 alone: 1.82× the performance of Cocco (from smarter fusion, tiling, and DRAM-cut choices, still using a plain double buffer).
  2. Stage 2 multiplies on top: 1.82 × 1.16 = 2.1112 ≈ 2.11× — matching the paper's stated overall average exactly.
  3. Energy stays flat across Stage 2: both stages are reported at −37.3% energy vs. Cocco — the same number before and after Stage 2.
  4. Why: Stage 2 only reorders when the same tensors move — it changes zero bytes transferred, so energy (∝ bytes moved) is untouched; only latency (removed stalls) improves.
🍯
Insight: the two-stage split isn't just an implementation convenience — it cleanly separates "reduce how much data moves" (Stage 1, changes energy) from "reduce how long moving it takes" (Stage 2, changes latency only).

② The ResNet-50 case study: 1.57× × 1.25× = 1.96×

For the specific ResNet-50 example the paper walks through (Fig. 8), Stage 1 gives 1.57× performance and −36.1% energy; Stage 2 adds a further average 1.25× over Stage 1. Verify the total.

  1. Stage 1: 1.57× performance, −36.1% energy vs. Cocco — driven by fewer, larger tiles and fewer DRAM cuts (below).
  2. Stage 2 multiplies: 1.57 × 1.25 = 1.9625 ≈ 1.96× — matching the paper's "total performance gain of 1.96× over Cocco" for this example exactly.
  3. Where Stage 1's gain came from: Cocco sets Tiling Number from a fixed kernel-channel parallelism heuristic (e.g. 16 for ResNet-50's later, wide layers); SoMa instead lets Tiling Number fall as low as 1 where the buffer allows, cutting halo-overlap recompute and enabling more reuse per tile.
💡
This is the same 1.82×/1.16× pattern from Example ① — reproduced on one concrete, inspectable network instead of an average across five.

③ Fewer, bigger units: 7962 tiles → 751 tiles, 13.0 LGs → 2.5 LGs

Averaged per network, Cocco produces 7962 computing tiles and 13.0 DRAM-cut Layer-fusion Groups (LGs); SoMa produces 751 tiles and 2.5 LGs (with 3.9 FLGs). What does this ratio mean?

  1. Tile count ratio: 7962 ÷ 751 ≈ 10.6× fewer, larger computing tiles under SoMa.
  2. LG count ratio: 13.0 ÷ 2.5 = 5.2× fewer DRAM round-trip boundaries — i.e., far more layers fused together per DRAM trip.
  3. Why LGs shrink faster than FLGs stay high (3.9): SoMa uses ordinary FLCs (no DRAM access) to free a finished layer's weights and change Tiling Number between groups of layers, reserving actual DRAM Cuts (which do cost a round trip) only when buffer truly runs out.
  4. Net effect: both Core Array Energy (−34.8%) and DRAM Energy (−44.3%) drop in Stage 1 — larger tiles improve on-chip reuse, and fewer DRAM cuts directly cut DRAM traffic.
🍯
Insight: Cocco's conservative, parallelism-only Tiling Number heuristic is the single biggest reason its tile count and LG count are both so much higher than SoMa's jointly-searched values.

④ GPT-2 decode: why batch size gives diminishing returns

After SoMa optimization, GPT-2-Small-Decode utilization at batch sizes 1, 4, 16, 64 is 0.66%, 2.03%, 4.26%, 5.84%. Each step is a 4× batch increase — how does the multiplier on utilization change?

  1. BS 1 → 4 (4× batch): 0.66% → 2.03%, a 3.08× jump in utilization.
  2. BS 4 → 16 (4× batch): 2.03% → 4.26%, only a 2.10× jump.
  3. BS 16 → 64 (4× batch): 4.26% → 5.84%, just a 1.37× jump.
  4. Same 4× batch increase, shrinking payoff each time — because the KV cache grows with batch size and eventually rivals the weights in size, so loading it (not compute) increasingly dominates the DRAM trip, capping how much extra batching can improve compute density.
💡
Even the best DRAM communication scheduler can't fix a workload whose bottleneck is data volume, not data timing — which is exactly why decode's overall speedup (1.14×) is so much smaller than prefill's (2.55×).
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
A team doubles their accelerator's GBUF size, hoping single-layer dataflow retuning will now cut DRAM traffic much further. Should they expect that?
Show answer
Not much. Once the buffer is large enough to hold most single layers' weights, the paper states plainly that single-layer dataflow optimization's impact on DRAM communication "diminishes significantly." The bigger buffer's real value now lies in cross-layer fusion and DRAM-timing flexibility — exactly the space Cocco and SoMa target.Misconception: that more buffer always rewards the same optimization that worked before. The frontier of usefulness shifts as buffer size crosses the "fits one layer" threshold.
Problem 2 · medium
After layer fusion, per-tile DRAM-access-to-compute ratios become more spread out (Fig. 3), not less. Why would removing DRAM access (via fusion) make the remaining demand look less uniform?
Show answer
Fusion selectively removes DRAM access only for fmaps that are consumed on-chip by the very next layer (e.g. Bi's ofmaps feeding directly into Ci). Weight loads and cross-boundary ifmaps/ofmaps still need DRAM. So the tiles that lost DRAM access drop to near-zero demand while the tiles that still need weights stay high — pulling the population apart into two extremes instead of narrowing it.Misconception: that "less total DRAM access" implies "smoother DRAM access." Fusion cuts the middle out of the distribution rather than shrinking it uniformly.
Problem 3 · medium
Suppose a new accelerator's Stage 1 search gives 1.4× performance and −20% energy over Cocco, and Stage 2 adds 1.2× more on top of Stage 1. What's the total performance multiplier, and what energy number would you expect to report alongside it?
Show answer
Total performance = 1.4 × 1.2 = 1.68×. Energy should still be reported as −20% (unchanged from Stage 1), because Stage 2 (DLSA) only reschedules timing of existing tensors — it moves zero additional or fewer bytes, so energy, which scales with bytes moved, doesn't change.Misconception: that a further performance gain from Stage 2 must come with a further energy gain too. Latency and energy are decoupled here precisely because Stage 2 never touches data volume.
Problem 4 · hard
Cocco sets each layer's Tiling Number from core-array kernel-channel (KC) parallelism requirements alone — bigger kernel/channel dimensions get a bigger Tiling Number. Explain concretely why this can hurt performance, using the halo-overlap concept.
Show answer
A larger Tiling Number means each layer is sliced into more, smaller tiles. For convolution/pooling layers, adjacent tiles need overlapping "halo" border pixels; more (smaller) tiles mean this halo overlap is computed and/or fetched repeatedly at more tile boundaries, adding extra work and DRAM/GBUF traffic. It also shrinks each tile's on-chip reuse opportunity for the Core Array Scheduler. SoMa instead adapts Tiling Number to buffer availability and network depth (letting it fall to 1 where possible), reducing both effects.Misconception: that matching Tiling Number to compute-unit parallelism is always "correctly sized." Parallelism-driven sizing ignores halo overlap cost and on-chip reuse entirely — both of which SoMa's search accounts for.
Problem 5 · medium
The Buffer Allocator shrinks Stage 1's buffer budget by 10% of its peak usage each iteration and stops after two consecutive non-improving iterations. Why not just give Stage 1 the smallest buffer that still fits, from the start, to maximize Stage 2's room immediately?
Show answer
Because Stage 1's own performance and energy also improve with more buffer, just with diminishing returns — starving it too early could lose more in Stage 1 quality than it gains in Stage 2 flexibility. The gradual 10% trims let the Buffer Allocator find the sweet spot where the combined cost of both stages is lowest, rather than assuming Stage 2 always benefits more from extra buffer than Stage 1 does.Misconception: that DLSA (timing) always deserves more buffer than LFA (fusion) since it's the "more advanced" optimization. Both stages have their own diminishing-returns curve; the allocator exists precisely because neither should be assumed to win by default.
Problem 6 · easy
The DSE results show that pairing both a very large buffer and very high DRAM bandwidth gives almost no extra benefit over more modest configurations, once SoMa is used. Why?
Show answer
Past a certain point, latency becomes dominated by compute time itself, not by DRAM access — SoMa's efficient scheduling has already hidden essentially all DRAM latency behind compute. Adding more bandwidth or buffer beyond that point has nothing left to hide, so it buys no further speedup, just cost.Misconception: that hardware resources always help "linearly" and stacking more of everything is safe. Once one resource stops being the bottleneck, spending more on it (or its neighbor) is pure waste.
Problem 7 · medium
At batch size 1, increasing buffer size barely reduces GPT-2-Small-Decode latency, but increasing DRAM bandwidth does. At batch size 16, the reverse pattern emerges. Explain both halves.
Show answer
At BS 1, fmaps are tiny (a small buffer already holds them), and there's little compute to hide weight-loading behind — even SoMa can't create prefetch opportunities that don't exist, so bandwidth directly gates latency. At BS 16, fmaps are larger (buffer now matters for holding/reusing them) and there's more compute per tile, giving SoMa more room to hide DRAM transfers behind computation — so buffer size (which enables more fusion and prefetch staging) starts to matter more than raw bandwidth.Misconception: that one hardware resource (buffer or bandwidth) is "the" bottleneck for a workload in general. The dominant resource shifts with batch size, and SoMa's whole DSE contribution is exposing exactly where that shift happens.
Problem 8 · hard
Why does the paper's "Theoretical Maximum Computing Resources Utilization" diamond ignore dependencies, and why is it still a meaningful comparison target for Stage 2's result?
Show answer
The theoretical maximum is defined as Util(MIN(total compute time, total DRAM access time)) — the best possible outcome if DRAM access and compute could be perfectly overlapped with zero stalls, ignoring whether data dependencies would actually allow that overlap in practice. It's an upper bound, not a claim of achievability. It's still meaningful because Stage 2's job is precisely to approach that overlap as closely as dependencies allow — and the paper reports Stage 2's results land within an average of 3.1% of it, showing the DLSA search is nearly exhausting the available scheduling freedom.Misconception: that an idealized upper bound that "ignores dependencies" is an unfair or unusable comparison. As long as it's used only to gauge how much headroom remains — not as a literal achievable target — it's exactly the right yardstick for Stage 2.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Ten questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What exactly is SoMa?▾
An end-to-end DNN accelerator scheduling framework that jointly searches layer-fusion choices and DRAM load/store timing, expressed through a six-attribute Tensor-centric Notation, to minimize an Energy×Delay cost. It has been built into a production compiler for a commercial accelerator and is open-sourced.
Q2How is SoMa different from Cocco?▾
Mapped into SoMa's own notation, Cocco only varies Computing Order and DRAM Cut (with FLC Set forced equal to the DRAM Cut Set); Tiling Number, fine-grained fusion cuts, DRAM tensor order, and living duration are all fixed by heuristics or a classic double buffer. SoMa searches all six jointly.
Q3What's the actual difference between an FLC and a DRAM Cut?▾
Every FLC splits the layer sequence into Fine-grained Layer-fusion Groups (FLGs) — but data can still be reused on-chip across an ordinary FLC boundary, just with weights released and Tiling Number possibly changed. A DRAM Cut is specifically an FLC that also forces a real DRAM round trip; the DRAM Cut Set is always a subset of the FLC Set.
Q4Why split the search into an LFA stage and a DLSA stage instead of one big search?▾
An LFA change (say, adding a DRAM Cut) changes which tensors exist and their dependencies, invalidating whatever DLSA timing was previously tuned — a joint search would keep discarding good DLSA solutions. Splitting also produces a synergy: minimizing DRAM access first (Stage 1) leaves more scheduling freedom for Stage 2.
Q5Why does GPT-2's decode stage see almost no benefit from SoMa?▾
Decode has extremely low compute density — its latency is dominated by loading weights and the KV cache, imposing a nearly pure bandwidth demand with little compute to overlap that loading behind. There's very little stall to eliminate, so even a perfect scheduler barely moves the needle (1.14× vs. 2.55× for prefill).
Q6Why doesn't increasing LLM batch size keep improving utilization?▾
As batch size grows, the KV cache — which scales with batch size — grows to become comparable to or larger than the (fixed-size) weights. Since loading the KV cache doesn't add proportionally more useful compute per byte the way batching normally does, the compute-density benefit of larger batches shrinks with each doubling.
Q7What does the Energy×Delay cost function actually optimize for?▾
Energyn × Delaym for one full batch, where n and m let you bias the search toward energy efficiency or raw speed. The paper's headline experiments use n = m = 1 (equal weight), since both matter for inference accelerators.
Q8Is SoMa specific to one accelerator?▾
No — the underlying template (DRAM + shared GBUF + cores with a PE array and Vector Unit) covers many industry and academic accelerator designs, and SoMa's modular structure lets the Core Array Scheduler/Evaluator and Instruction Generator be swapped per target. The paper's own production deployment is one concrete instance of this portability.
Q9What do "Ours_1" and "Ours_2" mean in the results figures?▾
They're SoMa's Stage 1 (LFA-only, still using a classic double buffer) and Stage 2 (LFA + DLSA, fully re-timed) results, shown side by side with Cocco so you can see how much each stage contributes independently.
Q10What did the authors leave for future work or acknowledge as limitations?▾
The DSE and case studies are the paper's own exploration of SoMa's implications for architecture design (buffer vs. bandwidth trade-offs across batch sizes); the SA-based search's runtime (up to ~2 days for the largest batch-size experiments on a 192-core server) is an explicitly reported cost. The paper positions SoMa as a first comprehensive definition of the DRAM Communication Scheduling Space, implying finer per-layer or hardware-specific extensions remain open.
