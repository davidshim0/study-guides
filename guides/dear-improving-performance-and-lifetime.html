3D NAND Flash Reliability · A Field Guide

Refresh the one wordline that's failing — not the whole block, just in case

Every SSD read softly disturbs its neighbors. NAND flash's fix — read refresh (RR) — has always fired on one fixed, worst-case threshold sized for the weakest wordline in the weakest block. DEAR instead watches each wordline's real, run-time error status and lets everyone else keep the read budget they already earned.

Before you begin

Nine ideas DEAR builds on

You know memory systems; you may not have lived inside a flash controller. These primers cover just the NAND-specific mechanics DEAR leans on — cell/WL/block structure, why reads themselves damage data, and why the existing fix is so wasteful. Tap any open; read in order the first time.

1The 3D flash hierarchy: cells, wordlines, blocks, h-layers▾

A flash cell stores charge; cells in a vertical string share a bitline (BL), and many BLs (e.g., 16 KB) make a block. Cells at the same height across the block share a wordline (WL) — they're read together. In 3D NAND, a block is stacked in h-layers (horizontal layers) along the height of the chip; each h-layer bundles a small group of WLs (four, in the paper's running example: WL1–WL4) separated by select-line transistors.

Why this geometry matters: the etching process that drills the vertical channel holes proceeds top-to-bottom, so channel-hole diameter and shape vary by h-layer — cells in the same h-layer share almost identical physical characteristics; cells in different h-layers don't.

Now you're ready: "block-level" vs "WL-level" management is a real granularity choice — a block bundles thousands of WLs across dozens to hundreds of h-layers, and DEAR's whole argument is that treating them as identical is the bug.
2Multi-level cells and Vth states▾

A cell encodes bits as its threshold voltage (Vth). m-bit multi-level cells (MLC=2, TLC=3, QLC=4) use 2m distinct Vth states to pack more bits per cell. TLC has 8 states: ER (erased, lowest Vth) through P1–P7. Reading a page compares each cell's Vth against reference voltages (V1REF…V7REF) that sit between adjacent states.

Higher m packs the states closer together — smaller margins between neighboring states, so a given amount of Vth drift is far more likely to push a cell across a reference voltage and flip a bit.

Now you're ready: this is why QLC is so much more read-disturb-fragile than TLC or MLC — smaller state margins, not a different physical failure mode.
3The read-disturb mechanism▾

To read one WL, flash applies a high pass-through voltage (Vpass, >6 V) to every other WL in the block so their cells act as pass-transistors. Vpass is far below the program voltage (>20 V), but it still "softly programs" those non-target cells — nudging Vth upward. One read's nudge is negligible; millions of reads to the same WL accumulate into real bit errors on its neighbors — read-disturb error.

The paper quantifies this with the Fowler–Nordheim tunneling relation: ErrorRD ∝ Vpass² · exp(−1/Vpass) · Tread · Nread — error grows with pass-voltage stress, how long each read holds that stress (Tread), and how many reads happen (Nread).

Now you're ready: read-disturb is a side effect of reading itself, not writing — which is why "just refresh before it's too late" (RR) is the only lever, and why bigger blocks / slower reads (Tread) make it worse.
4Read Refresh (RR) — the existing fix, and its price▾

Before accumulated read-disturb errors exceed what the ECC engine can correct, the SSD performs RR: read every valid page in the threatened block and rewrite ("migrate") it to a fresh block. That's extra internal reads and writes competing with host I/O, and extra program/erase cycles that raise WAF (Write Amplification Factor) — directly shortening the drive's usable lifetime.

The paper's own 11-SSD study makes the price concrete: a 1-TB 3D QLC SSD (Q-176-A) accumulates ~8.2 TiB of RR-induced writes after 30 TiB of reads, enough to cut its projected lifetime from a 5-year warranty to roughly 77 days under sustained read-heavy load.

Now you're ready: RR is non-optional for data integrity — the entire paper is about firing it only when actually needed, not about avoiding it.
5The conventional trigger: one fixed, worst-case Cmax▾

Existing schemes pick a single constant Cmax — the minimum, over every block B, of CBmax (the most reads that block can tolerate) — and fire RR for a block the instant its read count hits that number. It is, by construction, tuned to the single weakest block's weakest wordline in the whole SSD.

That single number then governs every block and every WL, regardless of how much healthier most of them actually are.

Now you're ready: "block-level, fixed-threshold" is the entire target DEAR is replacing with "WL-level, measured."
6Process variation: why blocks and WLs aren't identical▾

3D NAND manufacturing (the channel-hole etch, from primer 1) is imprecise enough that tolerance to read-disturb varies substantially — both between blocks and within a single block, between WLs. The paper's motivational characterization (11 commercial SSDs) found the worst block's Cmax up to 69.7% lower than the best block's, and within one block, the best WL tolerated 2.23× more reads than the worst WL.

A deeper 160-chip characterization (3,686,400 WLs) sharpens this further: at a fresh 0K P/E cycles, the best-vs-worst WL gap in one block averages 1.42×; by 2K P/E cycles (an aged drive), that gap widens to 4.14×.

Now you're ready: "worst-case" isn't a small safety margin here — it's throwing away up to 4× of most WLs' real capability, and the problem gets worse, not better, as the SSD ages.
7ECC and the error budget (ECCCAP)▾

An ECC engine can correct up to some number of bit errors per unit of data — call that ceiling ECCCAP (the paper uses 60 bits per 1 KiB in its characterization). RR must fire before a WL's accumulated read-disturb errors reach ECCCAP, or the SSD loses data outright — this is the hard reliability floor everything else in the paper has to respect.

Read-disturb errors aren't spread evenly across a WL's 8 Vth states either: they concentrate heavily in the lowest states — the erased (ER) state alone accounts for 67% of total read-disturb errors after 2000K reads, with P1 a distant second and the higher states barely moving.

Now you're ready: this concentration in the ER state is exactly what DEAR's error estimator (SEE) will exploit — you don't need to inspect every state to know a WL is in trouble.
8The FTL side: migration and garbage collection▾

RR's "rewrite to a new block" step goes through the normal Flash Translation Layer (FTL) path: read the valid pages, write them elsewhere, invalidate the old locations, and update the logical-to-physical mapping table. Blocks left partially invalidated by RR aren't a special case — they're reclaimed by ordinary garbage collection (GC), which already selects victim blocks and compacts valid data independently of why those blocks became fragmented.

Now you're ready: anything that changes when or at what granularity RR fires doesn't need to touch GC at all — a useful fact for judging how invasive DEAR really is.
9Why "just check every WL after every read" doesn't work▾

The obvious fix — continuously measure each WL's real error status — collides with latency. Conventional error checking means reading a WL and running it through the ECC decoder, which for a TLC page means multiple sensing operations per page (LSB, CSB, MSB) plus decode time. Scaled up to a modern 120-MiB block, one full round of "read everything and check" can take up to 337 ms — utterly incompatible with servicing host I/O.

Now you're ready: DEAR's real engineering contribution isn't "monitor per WL" (the idea is simple) — it's making that monitoring cheap enough to run online. That's the job of SEE, covered in How It Works.
The whole story in one place

DEAR in a nutshell

The mismatch it fixes, the mechanism that makes fixing it cheap, and the guardrail that keeps it safe.

Modern high-capacity 3D NAND has a structural problem: as blocks grow (page count per block is up roughly 20× in the last decade, and block size up roughly 40×) and cells get denser (TLC → QLC), read-disturb error gets both more frequent (more reads land in each block) and more dangerous (narrower margins between Vth states). Every existing defense manages this with one number: a fixed Cmax, set to whatever the single worst wordline in the single worst block can tolerate, applied uniformly to every block. Because 3D flash manufacturing has real process variation, that worst-case number throws away most of the fleet's actual reliability headroom — the paper measures the best wordline in a block tolerating up to 4.14× more reads than the worst, in the very same block, on aged flash.

DEAR (Dynamic Error-Aware Refresh) replaces the single fixed threshold with a per-wordline one, CWLmax — the actual number of reads this WL can tolerate before its real, measured error rate would exceed the ECC engine's correction capacity. Refresh (RR) then fires at wordline granularity, only for the WLs whose measured error has actually crossed the line — not for an entire block just because one of its many WLs is weak.

The catch: measuring "real, current error status" for every WL, continuously, is exactly the expensive thing primer 9 ruled out. DEAR's answer is SEE (Swift Error Estimator), three stacked optimizations — check only a representative WL per h-layer (SAM, ×4 fewer checks), estimate error from a single fast sensing instead of a full ECC read (FEM, ~7× cheaper per check), and check only periodically rather than after every read (BPM, with a provably safe interval). Combined, DEAR turns a per-WL check from a multi-hundred-millisecond ECC operation into a ~28 µs sensing op, cheap enough to interleave with normal host reads at well under 1.3% overhead.

The result is not a new reliability risk dressed up as an optimization: DEAR proactively sets its trigger threshold (ETH) below ECCCAP by a margin sized so that, given the measurement interval, no WL can silently cross ECCCAP between checks. Reported system-level gains (Section 7, against the paper's Baseline and prior state-of-the-art Cocktail scheme): RR-induced writes down 64.2% on average (up to 86.8%), 99.9th-percentile read latency down by tens of percent, and SSD lifetime (via lower WAF) extended by 36.7%.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • The mismatch: one fixed, worst-case Cmax is applied to every block and WL, even though real per-WL tolerance (CWLmax) can differ by over 4× within a single block.
  • DEAR's fix: move refresh from block granularity to wordline granularity, gated on each WL's own measured error, not a shared worst-case number.
  • SEE makes per-WL monitoring cheap via three stacked tricks: SAM (check 1-in-4 WLs, exploiting h-layer similarity), FEM (1 sensing instead of ~7 plus ECC decode, using CFBC(ER) as a proxy), and BPM (check every CINT reads, not every read).
  • Safety isn't traded away: DEAR sets its trigger (ETH) below ECCCAP with a margin engineered to survive a full monitoring interval — the paper proves this, it isn't a hand-wave.
  • Headline results: 64.2% average fewer RR-induced writes, up to 54.6% lower average p99.9 read latency (at 2K P/E cycles vs Baseline), and 36.7% longer SSD lifetime — for well under 1.3% monitoring overhead.
  • DEAR is complementary to prior data-placement defenses (e.g., Cocktail): combined as DEAR+, RR-invocation reduction rises to 75.6% on average.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about DEAR

Each picture stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note on where the analogy stops being accurate.

The old policy · fixed C_max

One evacuation order for the whole building

A fire marshal sets one evacuation rule sized for the single weakest stairwell in the single weakest wing of the building, then applies it to every floor. Most floors have far sturdier stairwells and could safely wait — but the rule doesn't know that, so everyone evacuates together, every time, at the pace of the worst case.

Where it breaks: a fire marshal's rule is static by necessity (no time to inspect during an actual fire); DEAR's whole point is that flash can be cheaply "inspected" in real time, so the worst-case shortcut is no longer necessary.
Optimization 1 · SAM

Sampling one plant from the same greenhouse tray

Four seedlings grown in the same tray, same soil, same light get watered and fertilized identically — check one for wilting and you've effectively checked all four. SAM does this for the four WLs in an h-layer: because the manufacturing etch gives them near-identical physical characteristics, measuring the leading WL's error tells you the other three's, for free.

Where it breaks: the tray analogy assumes perfect uniformity; SAM's real guarantee is empirical (Δ<0.85% variation across 921,600 tested h-layers) rather than architectural — it's a very strong correlation, not a law of physics.
Optimization 2 · FEM

Taking a pulse instead of running a full blood panel

A full diagnostic workup (an ECC-based error check — 3 page reads, ~7 sensing operations, plus decode) tells you everything, but it's slow. A single pulse reading — CFBC(ER), one sensing at V1REF — is a fast, well-correlated proxy: on its own it doesn't diagnose everything, but it reliably tells you whether this WL needs the full workup (a refresh) right now.

Where it breaks: a pulse can miss something a blood panel would catch; FEM's proxy is deliberately conservative (it maps to the worst observed error for a given CFBC reading) specifically so it never under-reacts, at the cost of occasionally refreshing slightly early.
Optimization 3 · BPM

Factory QC sampling every Nth unit, not every unit

Inspecting every single unit off a production line is thorough but far too slow to keep up with the line speed. Quality control instead samples every Nth unit, with N chosen conservatively enough that a defect can't slip through undetected between samples. BPM checks a block's WLs every CINT reads, with CINT derived so error can't cross ECCCAP between two consecutive checks.

Where it breaks: a factory's defect rate is roughly constant over time; flash's error accumulation is cumulative and read-count-driven, so BPM's "safe N" isn't a fixed constant — it shrinks as the drive ages (more P/E cycles → smaller safety margin → shorter CINT).
The guardrail · E_TH margin

The smoke detector, not the fire alarm's legal limit

Building code defines a smoke-density threshold that constitutes a genuine hazard. A smoke detector is calibrated to trip well before that legal limit, leaving enough buffer that people can react before the real danger arrives. DEAR's ETH plays that role against ECCCAP: it's not the failure point, it's a deliberately-earlier alarm sized to survive one full monitoring interval.

Where it breaks: a smoke detector's margin is a fixed safety convention; DEAR's margin (ECCCAP − ETH) is explicitly computed per configuration — tighter ETH (e.g., 5%) buys more usable WL lifetime but demands more frequent checks (shorter CINT); the trade-off is tunable, not fixed by convention.
The mechanism · block-wide vs WL-level RR

Recalling one bad batch, not the whole product line

A manufacturer that finds one contaminated batch of a product recalls that batch, not every unit of that product ever made. DEAR's WL-level RR is the same instinct applied to flash: refresh the specific wordline (and its h-layer siblings, per SAM) that measured unsafe, and leave the rest of the block's healthy WLs alone.

Where it breaks: a partial recall doesn't fragment the warehouse; a partial (WL-level) refresh does leave the block partially invalidated. The paper's answer is that this fragmentation is harmless — ordinary garbage collection reclaims it exactly as it would any other partially-invalid block.
Section by section

How DEAR actually works

The mismatch, precisely → SEE's three stacked optimizations → the RR-invocation flow in DearSSD → what it costs. Numbers are the paper's unless flagged as illustrative.

1 · The mismatch, precisely

Every prior read-disturb defense (data reallocation, Vpass scaling, buffer management) still gates RR on the same primitive: a single, fixed Cmax, defined as the minimum CBmax over every block B — itself the worst wordline in that block. Applied uniformly, it is by definition sized for the single weakest wordline on the entire drive. The paper's 160-chip characterization (3,686,400 WLs across 19,200 blocks) shows just how much this throws away: at 0K P/E cycles the average gap between the best and worst block is ~250K reads, and even within one block, the best WL tolerates 1.42× more reads than the worst — widening to 4.14× at 2K P/E cycles as the drive ages and margins shrink further.

◆
The governing move (memorize this). Replace the shared, worst-case threshold Cmax with a per-wordline, measured one, CWLmax — the actual number of reads this WL can take before its real error rate would exceed ECCCAP. Everything else in DEAR (SEE's three optimizations, the DearSSD integration) exists to make measuring CWLmax at runtime cheap enough to be worth doing.

2 · SEE optimization 1 — Similarity-Aware Monitoring (SAM)

A 3D block's h-layers each bundle a small group of WLs (four, in the paper's setup) that share near-identical physical characteristics because of how the vertical channel hole was etched and because they share a common WL gate during reads. Real-device measurement confirms this: across 921,600 tested h-layers, the maximum error-count variation among the four WLs in an h-layer is under 0.85%, regardless of aging (P/E cycles). SAM exploits this directly — measure only the leading WL of each h-layer, and infer the same error status for its siblings, cutting the number of WLs that need checking by 4× (and further, to 1/m, if future flash packs more WLs per h-layer).

3 · SEE optimization 2 — Fail-bit-assisted Error Monitoring (FEM)

Most read-disturb error concentrates in the erased (ER) Vth state (67% of total, per primer 7), so FEM doesn't bother reading the whole WL — it measures only the fail-bit count in the ER state, CFBC(ER), via a single sensing at V1REF. A full conventional check reads the WL's LSB (2 sensings), CSB (3 sensings), and MSB pages — 7 sensings total, plus ECC decode — so FEM's one sensing is a ~7× reduction on the sensing side alone.

To turn a raw CFBC(ER) count into a trustworthy trigger, DEAR uses an offline-built correlation model, MRBER(CFBC) — the maximum observed bit-error count ever seen for a given CFBC(ER) reading — and conservatively maps: CTHFBC = min{ CFBC | MRBER(CFBC) ≥ ETH } (Eq. 2). A WL refreshes once its CFBC(ER) reaches CTHFBC. Because the mapping uses the worst case observed for each CFBC value, some WLs may refresh a little before their true error hits ETH — a deliberate, safe over-estimate, not an accuracy failure.

4 · SEE optimization 3 — Block-wise Periodic Monitoring (BPM)

Checking after every single read is still too frequent to be free, so DEAR checks a block's WLs only every CINT reads. The size of CINT isn't arbitrary: it's set to the minimum number of reads any WL could take to accumulate error from ETH up to ECCCAP, guaranteeing that if a WL was safely under ETH at one check, it cannot silently exceed ECCCAP before the next one. A tighter ETH (smaller safety margin, e.g. 5%) buys much higher RR efficiency — the paper reports the average CWLmax reachable under ETH(5) is 2.8× the fixed Cmax at 2K P/E cycles, versus only 1.2× under a looser ETH(20) — but it demands a shorter, more frequent CINT.

Even amortized, monitoring an entire large block at once is slow (up to 6.42 ms for a 321-h-layer, 120-MiB block, even with SAM+FEM). DearSSD therefore interleaves the work: rather than checking every h-layer at once every CINT reads, it checks exactly one h-layer every CWLINT = CINT / (number of h-layers) reads — e.g., 19.3K / 321 ≈ 60 reads — spreading the monitoring cost thinly across ordinary host reads instead of bursting it.

5 · System integration: cheap enough to be free

DearSSD measures CFBC(ER) using two existing NAND features rather than new hardware: because data is randomized before programming, each Vth state is uniformly populated (e.g., 16K cells in TLC), so CFBC(ER) is just "16K minus the number of '1' bits" after a V1REF sensing — and an on-chip 1s-counter returns that count directly, bypassing the ECC engine entirely. Total: 28 µs (20 µs sensing + 8 µs counting, via existing GET/SET FEATURE commands). New state is minimal: a 2-byte per-block WL Pointer (WP) and a small offline-characterized reference table of (CWLINT, CTHFBC) pairs, one row per P/E-cycle range. Partial, WL-level RR fragments blocks somewhat, but that fragmentation is reclaimed by ordinary GC — no GC changes are needed.

◇
What DearSSD is compared against. Baseline: priority-aware I/O scheduling + a fixed, block-level Cmax. Cocktail: Baseline plus a prior state-of-the-art technique that spreads read-hot pages across blocks to delay future RR. DEAR: WL-level, error-measured RR (this paper). DEAR+: DEAR combined with Cocktail's data placement — the two are complementary (one changes where data lives, the other changes when/at what granularity RR fires).
Put real numbers on it

Worked examples

Five calculations recomputed from the paper's own numbers, then an interactive widget for the core trigger comparison. Anything I derived rather than quoted directly is labeled.

Example 1 — the naive monitoring cost, and why it's a non-starter

A modern 3D block can exceed 120 MiB (Section 6). Suppose checking one WL the conventional way (read + ECC decode) takes on the order of tens of microseconds per page across a WL's LSB/CSB/MSB pages, scaled across every WL in the block.

  1. The paper states this directly: "one round of error monitoring for all WLs in a block may take up to 337 ms" using the conventional (full ECC-based) approach.
  2. Compare to a single 4-KiB random read at, e.g., a 40 µs base read latency (Table 3's tR): 337 ms is over 8,000× a single read's latency (337,000 µs / 40 µs ≈ 8,425×) — utterly incompatible with servicing host I/O between checks.
▲
Takeaway: "just measure every WL's real error" is the right idea and the wrong plan — it's SEE's job (SAM+FEM+BPM) to make the same idea cheap.

Example 2 — SAM × FEM: the paper's own before/after, sanity-checked

With SAM and FEM applied, the paper reports monitoring a single 120-MiB, 321-h-layer block drops from 337 ms to 6.42 ms.

  1. Measured reduction: 337 ms / 6.42 ms ≈ 52.5×.
  2. Naive back-of-envelope estimate (my calculation, not stated in the paper): SAM cuts the number of WLs checked by 4× (checking 321 h-layer leaders instead of 1,284 physical WLs); FEM cuts each check's sensing cost by ~7× (1 sensing vs. 7). Combined: 4 × 7 = 28×.
  3. 28× falls well short of the observed 52.5×. The remaining ~1.9× gap is consistent with — but not explicitly quantified by — the paper's description of conventional monitoring as needing full ECC decoding on top of the 7 sensings; decode latency is not free, and its removal (FEM bypasses the ECC engine entirely) plausibly accounts for more of the saving than the sensing-count ratio alone suggests.
▲
Misconception it kills: "the paper's reduction factors should multiply out exactly." Real systems have overheads (like ECC decode latency) that don't show up as a clean, separately-stated ratio — a healthy reminder to sanity-check reported numbers rather than assume every headline figure decomposes cleanly.

Example 3 — the interleaved monitoring interval, CWLINT

The paper's own worked case (Section 6): a block with 321 h-layers and a block-wide monitoring interval CINT of 19.3K reads (the value the paper derives for ETH(5) at 2K P/E cycles, Figure 16(c)).

  1. CWLINT = CINT / (number of h-layers) = 19,300 / 321 ≈ 60.1, matching the paper's stated "checks only one WL every 60 reads."
  2. My extrapolation, not stated in the paper: Table 3's evaluation SSD instead reports CWLINT directly — 261, 212, and 108 reads at 0.5K, 1K, and 2K P/E cycles. That SSD has 176 stacked WLs ÷ 4 WLs/h-layer = 44 h-layers. Multiplying back out gives an implied CINT of 44×261=11,484, 44×212=9,328, and 44×108=4,752 reads at those three P/E points — a different block geometry from the 321-h-layer example above, so these numbers shouldn't be compared directly to the 19.3K figure, but the trend (CINT shrinking as P/E cycles rise) matches Figure 16(c)'s described behavior.
◆
Takeaway: as flash ages, the safety margin shrinks, so DEAR must check more often — that's expected and accounted for, not a flaw. The reference table (primer/How-It-Works) is exactly the mechanism that adapts CWLINT as P/E cycles climb.

Example 4 — monitoring overhead vs. a plain read

Table 3: base read latency tR = 40 µs; the paper's CFBC(ER) check costs 28 µs (20 µs sensing + 8 µs counting).

  1. At CWLINT = 60 reads, the "budget" of ordinary reads between checks is 60 × 40 µs = 2,400 µs.
  2. Overhead fraction = 28 / 2,400 ≈ 1.17% — matching the paper's own statement of "less than 1.2% overhead."
  3. Even pushed to a much more aggressive CWLINT = 15 reads (15×40 µs = 600 µs budget; 28/600 ≈ 4.7% by this simple ratio), the paper's actual measured system-level impact is only 0.7% IOPS drop and 1.3% latency increase — lower than the naive ratio suggests, because interleaving spreads the 28 µs check across many in-flight requests rather than blocking one read outright.
◆
Takeaway: a simple "check cost ÷ interval" ratio is a useful upper bound, but real queued/pipelined I/O absorbs some of it — measured overhead came in below the naive estimate at the tight interval.

Example 5 — memory footprint of DEAR's new state

Two new structures: a 2-byte per-block WL Pointer (WP), and an 8-byte-per-entry reference table.

  1. WP overhead: for a 1-TiB SSD built from 88-MiB blocks (matching the paper's own example — and Table 1's 2022 QLC row: 176 layers, 5,632 pages/block, 88 MiB): 1 TiB / 88 MiB ≈ 11,915 blocks × 2 bytes ≈ 23.3 KiB — matching the paper's stated "about 24-KiB."
  2. Reference table: one 8-byte entry per 100 P/E-cycle range, up to a 2,000-cycle limit → 2,000/100 = 20 entries × 8 bytes = 160 bytes total, exactly as the paper states.
●
Takeaway: DEAR's entire new metadata footprint for a 1-TiB drive is on the order of 24 KiB — vanishingly small next to the terabytes of writes it avoids.

Check yourself

Practice

Work the answer before opening it. Each answer ends with the misconception the question is designed to catch.

Warm-up
Table 3 gives tR = 40 µs and DEAR's CFBC(ER) check costs 28 µs. If CWLINT is set to 60 reads, what is the monitoring overhead as a fraction of the reads served in one interval?
Show answer
Budget per interval = 60 × 40 µs = 2,400 µs. Overhead = 28 / 2,400 ≈ 1.17%, matching the paper's "less than 1.2%." Catches: assuming any "runtime monitoring" must be expensive by nature. Here it's under 1.2% precisely because SEE was engineered (SAM + FEM) to make each individual check nearly free.
Diagnose
Why can't DEAR simply check every WL's ECC status after every single read, instead of building SAM, FEM, and BPM?
Show answer
Because a full, ECC-based check of every WL in a modern (~120-MiB) block can take up to 337 ms — over 8,000× a single 40 µs read. Doing that after every read would dwarf host I/O latency entirely. SAM (4× fewer WLs), FEM (~7× cheaper per check, no ECC decode), and BPM (check every CINT reads, not every read) are three independent, stacked ways of collapsing that cost to something interleavable with normal reads. Catches: treating "per-wordline monitoring" as a design decision with no cost. The paper's real engineering contribution is making that idea's cost negligible — the idea itself (check individually, not worst-case) is simple.
Diagnose
DEAR reduces RR-induced writes by 64.2% on average — but the paper reports only about 3–5% for YCSB-A and YCSB-F. What's different about those two workloads?
Show answer
YCSB-A and YCSB-F are read/write-mixed workloads (roughly 50% writes) rather than read-intensive. When writes are frequent, garbage collection reclaims blocks (because they accumulate invalid pages from overwrites) before those blocks' read counts ever get close to needing RR — so there's little RR-induced writing left for DEAR to eliminate in the first place. DEAR's benefit scales with how much of a workload's internal writes actually come from RR versus GC. Catches: assuming DEAR's percentage improvement is a fixed workload-independent property of the technique. It's conditional on RR actually being the dominant source of internal writes — true for read-heavy workloads, not for write-heavy ones.
Design
The paper shows the average CWLmax reachable is 2.8× the fixed Cmax under ETH(5) but only 1.2× under ETH(20), at 2K P/E cycles. Does choosing ETH(5) mean DEAR is running closer to a reliability edge than ETH(20)?
Show answer
No — both are equally safe by construction. ETH only sets where the trigger sits relative to ECCCAP; BPM independently derives CINT (the check frequency) so that no WL can cross ECCCAP between two checks, whatever ETH is chosen. A tighter margin (ETH(5)) just means DEAR must check more often (a shorter CINT) to keep that same guarantee — it trades monitoring frequency for RR efficiency, not safety for efficiency. Catches: conflating "closer to the failure threshold" with "less safe." The paper's BPM analysis is exactly the mechanism that decouples those two — safety is guaranteed at any ETH by adjusting CINT accordingly.
Design
DEAR and Cocktail attack the RR problem from different angles. Cocktail spreads read-hot pages across blocks to delay future RR; DEAR refreshes precisely at WL granularity when needed. Why does combining them (DEAR+) outperform either alone (75.6% average RR-invocation reduction vs. 64.2% for DEAR and 16.3% for Cocktail)?
Show answer
They act on different variables. Cocktail changes the data placement — reducing how quickly any given block's read count grows by not concentrating hot pages together — which pushes RR further into the future but still triggers it on a fixed, worst-case threshold once it does fire. DEAR changes the trigger condition itself — letting each WL use its real, measured capacity rather than the worst-case one — regardless of how reads are distributed. Because one shifts when pressure builds and the other raises the ceiling before RR is needed, their gains compound rather than overlap. Catches: assuming two RR-reduction techniques must be redundant with each other. They're complementary exactly because they intervene on different parts of the pipeline (placement vs. trigger), which is also why the paper frames DEAR as something existing techniques can adopt, not a replacement for them.
Loose ends

Frequently asked

The questions that come up once the mechanism clicks.

QDoes DEAR need new flash chip hardware?▾
No. DearSSD's CFBC(ER) measurement uses two features that already exist in commercial NAND — data randomization (which makes each Vth state's population predictable) and an on-chip 1s-counter — both exposed through existing GET/SET FEATURE commands. The paper is explicit that this requires "only minor changes to conventional SSD [firmware]," not chip redesign.
QDoes refreshing individual wordlines instead of whole blocks break garbage collection?▾
No. Partial, WL-level RR does leave some blocks more fragmented (more invalid pages scattered through them) than whole-block RR would, but GC already reclaims blocks based on invalid-page ratios using its existing victim-selection policy — it doesn't care why a block became fragmented. The paper states DEAR "does not require any modifications to standard GC operations."
QIf DEAR delays refresh compared to the old fixed threshold, how do we know it never loses data?▾
Because the trigger threshold ETH is set strictly below ECCCAP, and BPM's monitoring interval CINT is derived (not guessed) as the minimum number of reads any WL could take to go from ETH to ECCCAP. So even in the worst case — a WL just under ETH right after a check, accumulating error as fast as physically observed — it cannot reach ECCCAP before the next scheduled check catches it. This is a designed invariant, not an empirical hope.
QWhat's the actual difference between DEAR and Cocktail (the paper's prior state-of-the-art baseline)?▾
Cocktail changes where data lives — it spreads frequently-read ("read-hot") pages across more blocks so no single block's read count climbs as fast, delaying when a fixed-threshold RR would fire. DEAR changes when and at what granularity RR fires — it doesn't touch data placement at all, but replaces the fixed worst-case trigger with a per-WL, measured one. They're independent levers; combined (DEAR+), their effects compound (see Practice).
QWhy does DEAR help so much less on write-heavy workloads (YCSB-A, YCSB-F)?▾
Because DEAR only reduces the writes that come from RR. Write-heavy workloads generate a lot of internal writes from garbage collection regardless (blocks fill with invalidated overwritten pages and get reclaimed well before RR would ever trigger), so RR was never the dominant cost there. DEAR's ~3–5% improvement on those workloads is real but small precisely because there wasn't much RR-induced writing to remove in the first place.
QDoes the ETH/ECCCAP margin or CINT change as the SSD ages?▾
Yes — this is central to the design, not an afterthought. DEAR's reference table stores a distinct (CWLINT, CTHFBC) pair for each P/E-cycle range (characterized offline, e.g. every 100 cycles up to a 2,000-cycle limit), and DearSSD loads the row matching the drive's current aging status at runtime. As P/E cycles climb and the flash's error margin narrows, the safety margin shrinks and monitoring must happen more often — the table is exactly what lets DEAR track that automatically.
QWould DEAR still work as flash moves beyond QLC (e.g., PLC)?▾
Conceptual — extrapolated beyond what the paper directly tested. The paper argues DEAR should scale: it measured negligible overhead (0.7% IOPS, 1.3% latency) even at an aggressive CWLINT of 15 reads, and explicitly frames this as evidence DEAR should remain practical for "emerging QLC NAND flash" (~3× lower Cmax than TLC) and for even larger future block sizes. It does not present PLC-specific data, so treat the further extrapolation to PLC as a reasonable expectation stated by the authors, not a measured result.

Glossary, a dated summary card, further reading, and the honest provenance notes.

Core terms
Read-disturb errorBit errors on non-target wordlines caused by the pass-through voltage (Vpass) applied during reads to a neighboring WL, accumulating with read count (Nread).
Read Refresh (RR)The recovery procedure: read all valid pages in a threatened block and migrate them to a new block before errors exceed ECCCAP.
CmaxThe conventional, fixed, block-level trigger threshold — the minimum CBmax over every block, i.e. sized for the worst wordline in the worst block.
CWLmaxDEAR's per-wordline analogue: the actual number of reads this specific WL can tolerate before its real error would exceed ECCCAP.
DEARDynamic Error-Aware Refresh — the paper's scheme: trigger RR at WL granularity, based on each WL's measured error status.
SEESwift Error Estimator — DEAR's low-latency, per-WL error estimation mechanism (SAM + FEM + BPM).
SAMSimilarity-Aware Monitoring — check only the leading WL of each h-layer; infer the other ~4 WLs' status from it.
FEMFail-bit-assisted Error Monitoring — estimate error from a single CFBC(ER) sensing instead of a full ECC-based read.
BPMBlock-wise Periodic Monitoring — check every CINT reads (not every read), with CINT sized to guarantee safety between checks.
Supporting terms
ECCCAPThe hard reliability ceiling: the maximum bit errors the ECC engine can correct (60 bits/1 KiB in the paper's characterization).
ETHDEAR's trigger threshold, set below ECCCAP with a margin sized to survive one full monitoring interval.
CFBC / CFBC(ER)Fail-bit count; CFBC(ER) is the fail-bit count specifically in the erased state, FEM's low-cost error proxy.
CTHFBCThe CFBC(ER) value at which DEAR refreshes a WL, derived conservatively from the worst-case MRBER(CFBC) correlation (Eq. 2).
CINT / CWLINTThe block-wide monitoring interval, and its per-h-layer, interleaved equivalent (CINT ÷ number of h-layers).
h-layer / v-layerA 3D block's horizontal layer (a small bundle of WLs, e.g. 4, sharing similar physical characteristics) vs. its vertical layer (stacked WLs in one string).
WP (WL Pointer)A 2-byte per-block data structure recording which WL/h-layer should be checked next.
WAFWrite Amplification Factor — extra physical writes per logical write; RR raises it, DEAR lowers it by cutting RR-induced writes.
Baseline / Cocktail / DEAR+The paper's comparison points: fixed-Cmax scheme; prior state-of-the-art data-placement scheme; and DEAR combined with Cocktail.
DearSSD / NVMeVirtThe DEAR-enabled SSD design, implemented by extending NVMeVirt, a software-defined NVMe SSD emulator, with heterogeneous read-disturb modeling.

📌 Summary card · as of 2026-08-30 · MICRO'25

  • What: DEAR triggers SSD read refresh (RR) per wordline, based on each WL's real measured error, instead of one fixed, block-wide, worst-case threshold.
  • Why: process variation means a block's worst WL can need refresh up to 4.14× sooner than its best WL (2K P/E cycles) — the fixed threshold wastes most WLs' real headroom.
  • How it's made cheap: SEE stacks three tricks — SAM (check 1-in-4 WLs via h-layer similarity), FEM (1 sensing + on-chip counter instead of a full ECC read, ~7×), BPM (check every CINT reads, not every read).
  • The guardrail: ETH sits below ECCCAP with a margin sized so CINT provably can't let any WL cross ECCCAP between checks.
  • Headline results: 64.2% average (up to 86.8%) fewer RR-induced writes; lower p99/p99.9 read latency (tens of percent, growing with P/E cycles); 36.7% longer SSD lifetime; DEAR+ (with Cocktail) reaches 75.6% average RR-invocation reduction.
  • Cost: ~28 µs per WL check; well under 1.3% IOPS/latency overhead even at an aggressive 15-read interval; ~24 KiB metadata for a 1-TiB drive.
  • Sweet spot: read-intensive workloads (RR dominates internal writes). Diminishes for: write-heavy, mixed workloads where GC reclaims blocks before RR would fire anyway.
  • Not a replacement for data-placement defenses like Cocktail — complementary, and shown to compose (DEAR+).
  • Implementation cost: no new flash-chip hardware; reuses existing GET/SET FEATURE commands and on-chip counters; no GC changes.
