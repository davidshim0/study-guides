The fast pipe barely matters where you'd want to run it

RDMA-backed swap is 5–7× lower latency than an NVMe SSD. So why does a $0.50/GiB SSD look "surprisingly competitive"? Because the regime where RDMA wins big is the regime your SLO won't let you operate in.

1Swap and the page fault path▾

When DRAM is full, the kernel evicts cold pages to a backend device. Touch an evicted page again and the MMU raises a page fault; the kernel blocks the thread, fetches the page, and resumes it. Classic swap uses local disk; "far memory" swaps to remote DRAM instead.

Now you're ready: this paper is entirely about what backend sits at the far end of that fault-handling path — remote DRAM over RDMA, or a local NVMe SSD.
2Why RDMA is used for "far memory"▾

RDMA lets a NIC place data directly into remote memory without involving the remote CPU, at microsecond-scale latency (the paper measures 4.05 µs RDMA reads) — far faster than disk, though still orders of magnitude slower than local DRAM. Systems like Infiniswap, Hermit, and Mage route swap traffic over RDMA instead of disk, hoping to make remote DRAM feel closer to local.

Now you're ready: the paper's Table 2 measures exactly how much faster RDMA is than SSD in raw microbenchmarks — the starting point for everything else.
3Memory pressure and offload fraction▾

Shrinking a program's local DRAM budget below its footprint forces some of its data out to swap. "% memory offloaded" is how much of the footprint no longer fits locally — the paper's x-axis for every experiment.

Now you're ready: more offloaded memory ⇒ more swap traffic ⇒ more exposure to backend latency. The question is how much offload an app can tolerate before it matters.
4Reclaim vs. fault-in (asymmetric costs)▾

Evicting a page (reclaim) can usually happen asynchronously in the background — it costs CPU cycles but rarely stalls the app directly. Fetching a page back on a fault (swap-in) is synchronous — the faulting thread typically waits. That asymmetry is why the paper's model treats λin and λout differently.

Now you're ready: the backend's read latency (DB) matters on the swap-in path; reclaim mostly just eats CPU (Kreclaim).
5Working sets, hot vs. cold pages▾

Not all of a program's footprint is equally used. "Hot" pages are touched repeatedly; "cold" pages sit idle. Good offloading pushes cold pages out — they're rarely faulted back — and keeps hot pages resident.

Now you're ready: this is the crux of Table 4 — cold-data offloading barely exercises the swap backend at all; hot-data offloading exercises it constantly.
6Interrupt-driven vs. polling completion▾

Waiting for I/O to finish, a CPU can either sleep and get interrupted when done (cheap on CPU, adds wake-up latency) or spin/poll on a flag (near-zero latency, but burns a full core while waiting). SSD swap typically sleeps; latency-optimized RDMA swap often polls.

Now you're ready: RDMA's lower elapsed latency can come with a hidden CPU tax — the model's QB term is where such effects would show up.
7Cost per GiB, DRAM vs. Flash▾

DRAM's component price per gigabyte is far higher than NVMe flash's — and the paper notes that DRAM's scaling and cost improvements have slowed in recent years. RDMA-backed far memory means provisioning more remote DRAM; SSD swap only needs cheap flash.

Now you're ready: raw speed isn't the only axis — the paper explicitly frames SSD swap as the cost-effective baseline RDMA has to beat.
8Why "peak swap traffic" benchmarks can mislead▾

Push memory pressure high enough and any backend becomes the bottleneck — that's exactly where a faster backend shows the biggest relative win. But if the app is already 10× slower than baseline at that pressure level, nobody would deploy it there.

Now you're ready: this is the paper's central methodological move — evaluate backends only in the range where the app still meets its SLO, not wherever the gap looks most dramatic.
The whole story in one place

RDMA vs. SSD swap, in a nutshell

The raw gap, the SLO constraint that shrinks it, and why that matters for tiering systems.

Raw microbenchmarks are unambiguous: RDMA-backed remote DRAM has 5–7× lower latency and 5–9× higher bandwidth than a local NVMe SSD for 4 KiB swap-sized I/O (Table 2). If you evaluate swap backends purely as storage devices, RDMA wins comfortably — and by a wider margin as swap traffic increases.

But applications don't care about backend speed in isolation — they care about the fraction of their no-offload performance they retain. The paper defines this as θ-performance-preserving offloading: backend B under local-memory budget M is θ-preserving if the app still achieves ≥θ of its baseline throughput. Across four applications (PMBench, XSBench, PageRank, Spark), the two backends mostly track each other closely while performance stays near baseline, and pull apart only once offload traffic is high enough that performance has already collapsed well below any reasonable SLO (θ=0.8 or 0.9) — except Spark, the one workload where RDMA extends the SLO-preserving offload range to 40% vs. SSD's 20% (Table 5).

A simple model explains why: application-visible stall time is roughly λin·(Kfault + DB) + α·λout·Kreclaim. The SSD-vs-RDMA gap is proportional to λin, the swap-in rate. Performance-preserving offloading, by construction, keeps λin low — which is exactly the condition under which the backend's latency advantage stops mattering, unless (as with Spark's GC) λin stays elevated even at moderate offload.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • Raw speed: RDMA is 5–7× lower latency, 5–9× higher bandwidth than NVMe SSD (Table 2).
  • The gap that matters is application-level, and it scales with the swap-in rate λin, not raw device speed alone.
  • At the 80%-of-baseline SLO line, RDMA extends offloadable memory by ≤1 percentage point for 3 of 4 apps — only Spark gets a real 20-point boost (Table 5).
  • DRAM costs ~25× more per GiB than NVMe flash — a real premium RDMA far memory has to justify.
  • Implication for tiering: classify pages by expected reuse, not just "give the hottest tier to whoever has the fastest interconnect."
Four everyday pictures

How to think about the RDMA-vs-SSD gap

Each stands alone. Every card ends with where the picture stops being true.

The core tension · Cost vs. speed

The tollway and the free road

RDMA is a tollway: pay a steep premium (~25×/GiB) and traffic moves 5–7× faster. SSD is the free road: cheap, slower. If you rarely drive (low swap-in rate), the tollway's time savings are trivial — take the free road. Only a commuter who drives constantly (high λin) gets real value from the toll.

Where it breaks: tollway pricing is per-trip; RDMA's DRAM cost is provisioned up front regardless of how often you actually swap in.
Design philosophy · Prevention over speed

A faster ambulance vs. avoiding the accident

RDMA is a faster ambulance — it shortens DB, the time each fetch takes. But performance-preserving offloading tries to avoid needing the ambulance at all, by keeping hot pages resident so faults stay rare. If λin is near zero, ambulance speed is nearly irrelevant.

Where it breaks: Spark's GC-driven memory pressure does call the ambulance often — that's exactly the case (Table 5) where RDMA's speed genuinely extends the safe offload range.
Evaluation bias · Measuring at the wrong point

Judging a commute only during rush hour

If you only measure two commute routes during gridlock, the faster route looks dramatically better — but that's precisely when both are miserable. The honest question is which route is faster during your normal, SLO-compliant commute. Measuring RDMA vs. SSD only under heavy swap traffic makes the same mistake.

Where it breaks: unlike a commute, "rush hour" here (heavy swap traffic) isn't a fixed daily event — it's a knob (offload %) the operator controls, so it should be dialed back rather than tolerated.
The implication · Hybrid tiering

Long-term parking and valet

A smart lot sends most cars to cheap long-term parking (cold pages → SSD) and reserves valet spots (RDMA) for cars that will move again soon (hot/warm pages). Neither tier alone is optimal — the win is routing by expected reuse, which is exactly the paper's hybrid-offloading proposal.

Where it breaks: unlike parking, page "hotness" isn't known in advance — it must be estimated online, and misclassification (a cold page that suddenly gets hot) costs a slow round-trip.
The full explanation

How the study actually works

From raw device numbers to the model that explains why the gap collapses.

1 · Establishing the raw gap

On a testbed with AMD EPYC 7642, 512 GiB DRAM, a 1.92 TB Samsung PM983 NVMe SSD, and 100 Gbps InfiniBand (ConnectX-6), 4 KiB random I/O gives:

BackendOpAvg latp99 latBandwidth
SSDread27.15 µs89.60 µs2231 MiB/s
SSDwrite16.47 µs18.04 µs1367 MiB/s
RDMAread4.05 µs4.11 µs11774 MiB/s
RDMAwrite3.21 µs3.24 µs11728 MiB/s

Add cost: NVMe flash ≈ $0.50/GiB vs. remote DRAM ≈ $12.3/GiB — DRAM ~25× more expensive per GiB.

2 · Defining "performance-preserving" and sweeping offload %

A backend B under memory budget M is θ-performance-preserving if the app retains ≥θ of no-offload throughput. The paper sweeps offload % from 0–20% for PMBench and XSBench, 0–60% for PageRank, and 0–50% for Spark, on four apps — PMBench (uniform/hotspot, 50 GiB), XSBench (60 GiB), PageRank (15.5 GiB), Spark logistic regression (44.4 GiB) — comparing SSD swap, RDMA swap (via Hermit), and a no-offload baseline.

📊
Pattern for most workloads: both backends track closely near 0% offload; RDMA pulls ahead sharply only once offload is high enough that both have already fallen well under 80% of baseline — PMBench-uniform falls to 15%/26% of baseline at just 1% offloaded. Spark is the exception: RDMA keeps it ≥80% of baseline up to 40% offloaded, vs. SSD's 20% (Table 5) — see §3 below.

3 · Reading Table 4's three regimes

RegimeOffloaded dataSwap trafficApp perf.RDMA vs. SSD gap
Cold-dataMostly cold pagesLowNear baselineSmall
Warm-dataSome reused pagesModerateNoticeable slowdownVisible, workload-dependent

Spark is the outlier: its JVM garbage collector touches large swaths of heap during collections, pushing it into the warm/hot regime even at moderate offload — which is why RDMA meaningfully helps Spark specifically (Table 5: SSD sustains 80% up to 20% offloaded, RDMA up to 40%).

4 · The model — why the gap is proportional to λin

L_B ≈ λ_in·(K_fault + D_B) + α·λ_out·K_reclaim + Q_B

# comparing backends, ignoring second-order Q_B:
L_SSD − L_RDMA ≈ λ_in · (D_SSD − D_RDMA)

DB is backend-dependent read latency (RDMA's edge); Kfault and Kreclaim are backend-independent software costs. The gap scales with λin — the swap-in rate — so performance-preserving offloading (which by definition keeps λin low) is exactly the condition under which RDMA's latency edge stops mattering at the application level.

💡
One subtlety: optimized RDMA swap often polls for completion (keeping the CPU busy to cut latency) while SSD swap typically sleeps on an interrupt. So DRDMA's elapsed-time advantage can partly trade off against extra CPU usage — a cost the simple model folds into QB.

5 · The implication: hybrid tiering by expected reuse

Because the backend gap is driven by reuse frequency, not raw device speed alone, the natural design is a hybrid offload tier: route pages predicted to be cold to cheap local (or pooled) SSD, and reserve RDMA-backed remote DRAM selectively — for applications where it measurably extends the performance-preserving region, or for short-lived pressure on warmer data.

🍯
For DRAM/NVM tiering work: this is the swap-side mirror of hot/cold page classification in TPP/HeMem/Memtis — the paper does not draw this connection itself, but its λin-driven argument echoes, from the swap-backend side, why access-frequency-aware placement beats "always promote to the fastest tier."
Follow the numbers

Worked examples

Four calculations straight from the paper's own tables.

① The raw latency and bandwidth ratios (Table 2)

  1. Read latency ratio: 27.15 µs (SSD) ÷ 4.05 µs (RDMA) = 6.70×.
  2. Read bandwidth ratio: 11774 MiB/s ÷ 2231 MiB/s = 5.28×.
  3. p99 read latency ratio: 89.60 µs ÷ 4.11 µs ≈ 21.8× — the tail gap is far larger than the average (both figures measured under light load, per the paper's methodology; the paper doesn't attribute this specifically to queuing).
🍯
These are the numbers that make RDMA look unambiguously superior as a raw device — and exactly the numbers that stop mattering once λin is small (Example ③).

② The 25× DRAM cost premium

  1. SSD: $0.50/GiB. DRAM: $12.3/GiB.
  2. Ratio: 12.3 ÷ 0.50 = 24.6× ≈ the paper's stated "roughly 25×."
  3. So what: for RDMA far memory to be worth deploying, its performance-preserving benefit must justify paying ~25× more per GiB of offload capacity.

③ Reading Table 5's "useful RDMA gain"

Table 5 gives, for each workload, the max % offloaded while retaining ≥80% of baseline.

  1. PMBench: N/A for both — neither backend retains 80% even at 1% offloaded. Δ < 1%.
  2. XSBench: SSD 3%, RDMA 4%. Δ = 1 percentage point.
  3. PageRank: SSD 46%, RDMA 46%. Δ < 1 percentage point — essentially tied.
  4. Spark: SSD 20%, RDMA 40%. Δ = 20 percentage points — by far the largest useful gain.
  5. Conclusion: for 3 of 4 workloads, RDMA's real-world SLO-preserving advantage is at most 1 percentage point of offloadable memory (PageRank <1 pp, XSBench exactly 1 pp), despite its 5–7× raw speed edge.

④ Applying the model's first-order gap

Using L_SSD − L_RDMA ≈ λin·(DSSD − DRDMA), with DSSD − DRDMA ≈ 27.15 − 4.05 = 23.1 µs/page:

  1. At λin = 10 pages/s (a rare-swap-in regime): gap ≈ 10 × 23.1 µs = 231 µs/s of saved stall time — negligible against a 1-second window.
  2. At λin = 10,000 pages/s (heavy swap traffic): gap ≈ 10,000 × 23.1 µs = 231 ms/s — a substantial fraction of wall-clock time.
  3. Insight: the same 23.1 µs/page backend edge is invisible at low λin and dominant at high λin — precisely the pattern in Figure 1.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer.

Problem 1 · easy
A vendor claims their far-memory system is "6× faster than SSD swap" using a benchmark at 20% memory offloaded. What's missing from that claim?
Show answer
Whether the application is still within an acceptable SLO at 20% offloaded. The paper shows that at high offload fractions, both backends may already be far below 80% of baseline — a "6× faster" backend can still mean "6× less catastrophically slow," not "fast enough to deploy."Misconception: that a bigger relative speedup at high pressure implies a bigger practical benefit. The benefit should be measured within the SLO-preserving region.
Problem 2 · medium
Using Table 2, compute the write latency ratio (SSD ÷ RDMA) and compare it to the read latency ratio. Which is bigger, and does that match "RDMA writes are typically posted asynchronously"?
Show answer
Write ratio: 16.47 ÷ 3.21 ≈ 5.13×. Read ratio: 27.15 ÷ 4.05 ≈ 6.70×. The read ratio is larger. This is consistent with reads sitting squarely on the synchronous swap-in path (D_B in the model), while writes/reclaim are more often overlapped with other work, so their absolute gap matters less even though the ratio itself is still large.Misconception: that latency ratios alone determine application impact — the model says impact depends on which path (sync fault-in vs. async reclaim) the latency sits on.
Problem 3 · medium
Why does Spark behave differently from PMBench, XSBench, and PageRank in Table 5?
Show answer
Spark runs on the JVM; garbage collection phases touch large portions of the heap, generating swap-in traffic even when the application's "useful" working set is small. This pushes Spark into the warm/hot-data regime (Table 4) more readily than the other apps, so RDMA's lower fault latency meaningfully extends how much memory can be offloaded while staying above 80%.Misconception: that all "normal" applications behave like PMBench/XSBench. Runtime-level effects like GC can create swap-in patterns very different from the application's own logical memory-access pattern.
Problem 4 · hard
Explain, from the model L_B ≈ λ_in(K_fault + D_B) + αλ_out K_reclaim + Q_B, why increasing offload % increases both λ_in and λ_out, and why that means the absolute performance loss keeps rising even in the regime where the RDMA-SSD gap is small.
Show answer
More memory offloaded means more of the working set no longer fits locally, so both the rate of pages being faulted back in (λ_in) and the rate of pages being reclaimed to make room (λ_out) rise. Even when D_SSD − D_RDMA is small relative to K_fault (so the two backends are close), the λ_in·K_fault and αλ_out·K_reclaim terms — which are backend-independent — still grow with offload %, meaning total loss L_B rises for both backends even as their difference stays small.Misconception: that a small RDMA-vs-SSD gap means offloading is cheap. It means the two backends are similarly priced; the offloading itself can still be expensive via K_fault and K_reclaim.
Problem 5 · medium
A tiering system for CXL-attached memory promotes any page with ≥2 accesses/interval to the fastest tier, regardless of frequency. Using this paper's argument, what's the flaw?
Show answer
This is the swap-side mistake in reverse: it ignores that a tier's speed advantage only pays off in proportion to how often a page is actually re-referenced (the λ_in analogue). A page accessed exactly twice, then never again, gains little from residing in the fastest tier — the same logic that makes RDMA's edge vanish for rarely-swapped-in pages here.Misconception: that any nonzero reuse justifies the fastest available tier. The benefit must be weighed against reuse frequency and the cost premium of that tier.
Problem 6 · easy
Under what condition does the paper's own model predict RDMA and SSD would perform identically (L_SSD = L_RDMA)?
Show answer
When λ_in = 0 — i.e., no pages are ever faulted back in. Then L_SSD − L_RDMA ≈ λ_in·(D_SSD − D_RDMA) = 0, regardless of how large the backend latency gap D_SSD − D_RDMA is.Misconception: that backend choice always matters once any offloading occurs. If offloaded pages are never revisited, the backend is never actually exercised on the critical path.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Q1Is the paper claiming RDMA is bad for swap?▾
No. It claims RDMA's advantage is real but concentrated in a regime (high swap traffic) that usually violates application SLOs. In low-swap regimes, and for workloads like Spark with GC-driven pressure, RDMA still has genuine, measurable value.
Q2What exactly is θ-performance-preserving offloading?▾
A backend B and local-memory budget M are θ-preserving for an app if running under that budget with that backend retains at least fraction θ (e.g. 0.8) of the app's no-offload throughput. It reframes evaluation around "how much can I offload while staying acceptable," not "how fast is the backend."
Q3Why do PMBench results drop so sharply from just 1% offloaded?▾
PMBench is close to a pure memory-touching workload with almost no computation to hide fault latency, so nearly every swapped-out page immediately becomes visible as stall time — there's no "free" overlap to absorb the cost.
Q4Why does PageRank tolerate offloading up to ~46% before collapsing?▾
A large fraction of PageRank's footprint appears to be cold, idle, or rarely revisited during the run, so offloading it generates little swap-in traffic — consistent with the "cold-data offloading" row of Table 4.
Q5What's the asymmetry between swap-in and swap-out (reclaim) costs?▾
Swap-in (fault-in) typically blocks the faulting thread synchronously, so its latency (D_B) directly stalls the app. Reclaim/swap-out is usually asynchronous background work — it costs CPU cycles (K_reclaim) but doesn't stall the app the same way, unless the CPU is already saturated (captured by α).
Q6Does the polling-vs-interrupt detail matter a lot?▾
It's a caveat, not a headline result: optimized RDMA swap implementations often poll for completion, trading CPU occupancy for lower elapsed latency, while SSD swap typically sleeps on interrupt. The paper folds this into the model's Q_B term rather than measuring it directly.
Q7Why does the paper single out Spark as an exception?▾
Spark performs substantial computation between memory-intensive phases (tolerating some paging), but its JVM garbage collector periodically touches large heap regions, generating swap-in traffic that RDMA's lower latency measurably absorbs — hence the 20-percentage-point gap in Table 5.
Q8What's the paper's actual recommendation for system builders?▾
Treat modern SSD-backed swap as a serious baseline; report any new far-memory system's benefit at a fixed SLO target (not just under heavy pressure); and consider hybrid designs that route cold pages to cheap SSD (possibly pooled across nodes) and reserve RDMA for pages/workloads where it demonstrably extends the SLO-preserving region.
Q9Is this result specific to this exact SSD/NIC hardware?▾
The qualitative claim isn't: the first-order model says the benefit of any faster backend scales with λ_in times the backend latency gap. Newer hardware shifts the absolute numbers (D_B, bandwidth ceilings) but not the underlying structure of the argument.
Q10How does this relate to production systems like TMO or Gswap?▾
Both TMO (Meta) and Gswap (Google) already target cold memory and regulate reclaim by application-visible impact (e.g. PSI stalls) rather than raw memory savings. This paper applies the same "measure at acceptable impact, not maximum offload" lens specifically to the RDMA-vs-SSD backend choice.
