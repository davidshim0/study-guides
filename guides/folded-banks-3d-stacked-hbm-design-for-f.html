3D-Stacked HBM Architecture · ISCA'25 · A Field Guide

Fold the bank, not the row

Random 8B–64B accesses to HBM waste most of a stack's advertised bandwidth — not because the wires are slow, but because a power-safety limit (tFAW) caps how many rows can activate at once. Folded Banks (FB-HBM) turns each 2-D bank into an 8-way 3-D stack spanning the DRAM dies, moves control logic to a new base die, and lets hybrid-bonded TSVs carry the now-short wires — buying far more simultaneous activations inside the same power budget.

🧱 Adhinarayanan et al., AMD Research — ISCA'25 🎓 Assumes DRAM/HBM basics: banks, rows, sense amps, TSVs ⏱ ~45 min read
🧱

Don't just shrink the row — shorten the path. Fold the bank vertically across dies and move GSAs/decoders to a base die so more rows can activate inside the same current/power budget (tFAW) — that's what irregular bandwidth was actually starved of.
Row-size shrinking alone plateaus at 1.65× more activations; shortening the data path gets you to 7.2× (Section 3.1) — and a full architecture built on that insight gets 6.7× effective bandwidth.

Before you begin

Nine ideas FB-HBM builds on

You already know DRAM/HBM basics, so these skip the fundamentals and go straight to the pieces this paper actually leans on — the bandwidth model, the current budget behind tFAW, and the two levers (row size vs. data movement) whose unequal payoff is the paper's central finding. Tap any open; read in order the first time.

irregular / random-access bandwidth — the metric being chased row size & atom size — the traditional levers FB-HBM machinery — folds, base die, hybrid bonding, TSVs power/current & area costs — tFAW, IDD7, KOZ
1DRAM's physical hierarchy, in the vocabulary this paper uses▾

A bit lives in a capacitor, read through an access transistor onto a bitline, sensed by a local sense amplifier (LSA). A row of cells sharing a wordline forms one line across a mat (a 2-D grid of cells); a horizontal row of mats is a subarray; multiple subarrays stacked form a bank. The wordline itself is hierarchical: a coarse master wordline (MWL) runs the length of a subarray and feeds local wordline drivers (LWD), which drive the local wordlines (LWL) inside each mat.

To actually read/write, data travels from LSAs down long master data lines (MDL) to global sense amplifiers (GSA) at the bank's edge, gated by column select lines (CSL) from the column decoder. This MDL/GSA path — the distance data must travel inside a bank — is exactly what FB-HBM goes after.

Now you're ready: whenever this guide says "shorten the path," it means shortening the MDL run from LSA to GSA, or the wire from bank to TSV — not making the DRAM cell itself faster.
2How HBM stacks dies: TSVs, pseudo-channels, and the 4-step read▾

HBM stacks 4+ DRAM dies vertically, wired together by through-silicon vias (TSVs). Command/address arrives on CA pins, data leaves on DQ pins, both routed through a central TSV stripe. To spread bandwidth efficiently, HBM splits each channel into pseudo-channels (pCH) that share CA pins but split DQ pins.

A read takes four steps: ① activate a wide row, ② move a 32B atom to the central TSV stripe, ③ move it vertically through the stack via TSVs, ④ route it across the interposer to the host. Step ② — data movement within the die, from cell to TSV — is the most energy-intensive of the four.

Now you're ready: "central TSV stripe" plus "long intra-die wires" is the baseline geometry FB-HBM tears up — it distributes the TSVs instead of centralizing them.
3The three DRAM operations and their timing knobs▾

ACT (activation) asserts the wordline, sensing cell voltages onto bitlines. CAS reads/writes columns once the sense amps have settled — the gap is tRCD. PRE (precharge) resets the bitlines afterward; from ACT to PRE-eligible is tRAS, and PRE itself takes tRP. Together, tRAS + tRP = tRC: the minimum time between successive ACTs to the same bank.

Column commands are throttled by tCCDL (same bank group) or the shorter tCCDS (different bank group). Row commands are throttled by tRRDL/tRRDS (same/different bank group) and, crucially, tFAW: no more than 4 ACT commands per channel within any tFAW window, a hard cap on activation frequency to bound current draw.

Now you're ready: five acronyms matter most going forward — tRC, tRRD, tFAW (row-side, they gate irregular bandwidth) and tCCD, tBURST (column/I/O-side, they gate regular bandwidth).
4Two bandwidth formulas — why "regular" and "irregular" diverge▾

Peak regular bandwidth (sequential accesses to an open row) is simply an I/O-signaling question: Br = min(Satom/tCCDS, Satom/tBURST). Faster pins, faster Br — this is exactly what HBM2→HBM2E→HBM3→HBM4 have scaled well.

Irregular (random-access) bandwidth is a different equation entirely — it never touches signaling speed: Bir = min(n·Satom/(tRAS+tRP), Satom/tRRDS, ACTsInFAW·Satom/tFAW). It's bounded purely by how often new rows can be activated. Faster pins do nothing for Bir if the ACT rate doesn't also rise.

Now you're ready: this is the paper's central diagnosis — HBM generations have scaled Br by racing pin speed while leaving the tRC/tRRD/tFAW terms in Bir almost untouched, so the gap between the two keeps widening.
5Why tFAW exists: the IDD7 current-budget model▾

Activating a row draws a current spike; too many overlapping activations can draw enough current to cause erroneous sensing or exceed the power-delivery budget. JEDEC's IDD7 test measures current under interleaved activate/read/precharge traffic, and vendors use it to set tFAW — capping ACTs per channel to keep current under a fixed budget (this paper uses 500 mA per channel).

The paper's model splits IDD7 current into two additive pieces: IDD7 = (tRC/tRRD)·Iact + bus_util·IDD4R — an activation-current term (Iact, scales with row size) and a data-movement/read-current term (IDD4R, scales with how far/how much data moves). Both terms must shrink to buy more concurrent activations.

Now you're ready: tFAW isn't an arbitrary vendor number — it's a current-budget constraint. Anything that lowers per-activation or per-read current directly buys more ACTs per tFAW, which is exactly what raises Bir.
6Two levers, unequal payoff — the paper's key empirical finding▾

Because IDD7 has two terms, there are two ways to cut it: shrink row size (lowers Iact — the traditional approach, e.g. FGDRAM) or shrink data-movement distance (lowers the read-current term — largely untried before this paper). The paper measures both, halved repeatedly on a reference HBM4 design.

Row-size reduction alone plateaus at 39% of baseline current (only 1.65× more activations) — diminishing returns, because the data-movement term never moves. Data-movement reduction alone is more effective: 59% current reduction, 2.4× more activations. Combined, seven halvings of each drop current to 13.8% of baseline and unlock 7.2× more simultaneous activations — a synergy neither lever gets alone.

Now you're ready: this is why FB-HBM's headline move is architectural (fold the bank to shorten data movement) rather than just another row-shrinking scheme — the paper's own model shows row-shrinking alone runs out of road.
7The Folded Banks idea: one bank, spread across 8 dies▾

FB-HBM adds a new base (logic) die beneath an 8-Hi HBM stack, and instead of keeping a full 2-D bank on one DRAM die, splits its subarrays into 8 folds — one-eighth of a bank's subarrays per die. Each fold can activate independently. The GSAs that used to sit at the (tall) bank's edge move down to the base die instead, shared across all 8 folds.

Because each fold is short, the LSA→GSA distance collapses (Table 5: bank height drops from 918.4 µm in Iso-HBM4 to just 95.8 µm in FB-HBM — see the signature figure in the next tab). The MDL and CSL wires now run vertically through the stack via TSVs instead of horizontally across a tall die.

Now you're ready: "folding" = taking one tall 2-D structure and re-laying it as several short slices distributed across the dies you already have, connected vertically instead of horizontally.
8The two enablers: hybrid bonding and self-timed sensing▾

Folding needs a lot of new vertical wiring — MDL/CSL routes need 128× the wiring density of ordinary DQ pins. The paper conservatively assumes hybrid bonding at a 5 µm pitch: ~40,000 vertical connections per mm², a 363× density jump over microbumps, with much smaller keep-out zones (KOZs) than older TSV technology.

Folding also creates a second problem: a short fold with only 4 subarrays has no room for the "dummy" reference subarrays traditional sensing needs at its edges (25–33% area overhead). The paper's fix is an Imbalance-Tolerant Bit-Line Sense Amplifier (IBT-BLSA) — it senses correctly even with unequal-length bitlines, eliminating dummy subarrays for only ~8% overhead.

Now you're ready: hybrid bonding is what makes folding physically wireable; IBT-BLSA is what makes folding physically affordable at the bank's edges. Neither is FB-HBM's headline idea, but both are load-bearing.
9Grains and FB-G-HBM: shrinking the row on top of folding▾

Folding shortens the path but keeps HBM3's 1024B row. FB-G-HBM adds the other lever back in: it segments each bank's MWL into 4 independent grains (256B rows), the same idea prior work (FGDRAM) tried — but FGDRAM's grain-selection logic doesn't fit cleanly next to the LWD or LSA without ballooning mat area. FB-G-HBM instead puts the grain-selection AND gate on the base die, sending a single pre-computed enable signal (LWDEn) up through a TSV.

FB-G-HBM also adds an optional 8B read/write mode (new SM_RD/SM_WR commands) so GUPS-style workloads, which only need 8 of a 32B atom's bytes, stop paying to move the other 24.

Now you're ready: FB-HBM = shorten the path. FB-G-HBM = shorten the path and shrink the row and shrink the atom — stacking all three of the paper's levers, which is why it, not FB-HBM alone, posts the headline 6.7× number.
The whole story in one place

FB-HBM in a nutshell

The problem, the diagnosis, the fix, and the two-lever result that makes the fix necessary rather than incremental.

HBM has gotten dramatically faster at regular, sequential bandwidth — a 6.4× increase over two generations — while its irregular, random-access bandwidth barely moved. Projected out to HBM4 (16 Gbps pins, 2 TB/s per stack), a random 8B write is estimated to land at just 8.3% of peak bandwidth. Graph analytics and sparse ML workloads live almost entirely in this irregular regime, so the gap is not academic.

The paper's diagnosis: irregular bandwidth is governed by an entirely different equation than regular bandwidth — one bounded by tFAW, a hard cap on activations per channel that exists to keep current draw under a fixed budget (JEDEC's IDD7 test, ~500 mA/channel in this paper). Faster I/O pins do nothing for a bottleneck that's about current, not signaling speed. Modeling that current as two additive pieces — activation current (scales with row size) and data-movement current (scales with wire distance and data volume) — reveals something the field had mostly ignored: shrinking rows alone plateaus (1.65× more activations, then diminishing returns), while shortening data movement is both more effective alone (2.4×) and synergistic combined with row shrinking (7.2×).

Folded Banks (FB-HBM) acts directly on that insight. Rather than another row-shrinking scheme, it re-lays each 2-D bank as an 8-way 3-D "folded" structure spanning the dies of an 8-Hi HBM stack, and adds a new base (logic) die that hosts the command/control logic and the global sense amplifiers every fold now shares. Hybrid-bonded, thin-pitch TSVs carry the short vertical wires this needs; a new sense-amp design (IBT-BLSA) removes the dummy subarrays that would otherwise make a 4-subarray fold too expensive. FB-G-HBM stacks the other two levers on top — smaller rows (grains) with their control logic also relocated to the base die, and an optional 8-byte atom for workloads like GUPS that never needed the full 32 bytes.

The upshot: FB-G-HBM reaches 1350 GB/s of effective 8B random-access bandwidth versus a projected 201 GB/s for a conventionally-scaled HBM4 — a 6.7× improvement — while cutting stack power for streaming workloads from 39 W to 11.3 W. Detailed cycle-level simulation turns that into real speedups: 6.74× on GUPS, 5.02× on graph neural networks, 2.0–4.1× across CORAL-2 HPC kernels, all over an HBM3 baseline.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • Irregular bandwidth is bounded by tFAW / activation frequency, not by I/O signaling speed — faster pins alone can't fix it.
  • Row-size reduction plateaus (1.65× more activations); data-movement reduction is more effective and synergistic (2.4× alone, 7.2× combined).
  • FB-HBM = fold a 2-D bank into 8 vertical "folds" across dies + a new base (logic) die hosting GSAs/decoders — shortens the path without shrinking the row.
  • Hybrid bonding (5 µm pitch, ~40,000 conn/mm²) makes the vertical MDL/CSL wiring feasible; IBT-BLSA removes the dummy-subarray tax (25%→8%) that folding would otherwise incur.
  • FB-G-HBM adds grains (256B rows) with base-die grain logic, and an optional 8B atom for GUPS-class workloads — stacking all three levers.
  • Headline results: 6.7× effective irregular bandwidth at 8B accesses, 6.74× GUPS speedup, 5.02× GNN speedup, stack power for streaming cut from 39 W to 11.3 W — all versus a conventionally-scaled HBM4 baseline (Iso-HBM4).
  • Not yet standardized: small-atom commands (SM_RD/SM_WR) and folded-bank sub-row activation are absent from current JEDEC HBM specs.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about FB-HBM

Each picture stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the analogy stops being true.

The core move · Folding

A tower vs. a low-rise campus

The conventional bank is a single tall tower: every delivery (data access) has to travel the full height to reach the mailroom (GSA) in the basement. FB-HBM instead builds four low-rise buildings connected by a shared basement corridor (the base die) — each building is short, so its own deliveries travel almost no distance, and the corridor carries everything to one shared mailroom.

Where it breaks: real buildings can't be re-poured into shorter ones without demolishing the tower; FB-HBM's "re-layout" is a chip redesign, not a retrofit — it requires a new base die and different DRAM-die masks from day one.
The mechanism · Base die

Moving the switchboard to the basement

Every office floor (DRAM die) used to have its own switchboard operator (GSA/decoder) crammed into the corner. FB-HBM rips the switchboard out of every floor and puts one shared switchboard in the basement (base die) — freeing up floor space and shortening every floor's internal wiring, since nobody has to route across their own floor to reach it anymore.

Where it breaks: the basement switchboard isn't "free" — it's a whole extra die that must be manufactured, bonded, and yielded, and the paper flags thinned-die (<30 µm) manufacturing as an open challenge, not a solved one.
The wiring win · Distributed TSVs

One crowded elevator vs. many local ones

Central TSVs are one elevator bank in the middle of the building — everyone on every floor funnels toward it, jamming the lobby and the shaft. Distributed, hybrid-bonded TSVs are like giving every small office cluster its own short local elevator straight down to the shared lobby — less crowding, shorter individual trips, and the building's overall footprint barely changes because the elevators are so thin (5 µm pitch, 40,000/mm²).

Where it breaks: thin elevators aren't free either — each one needs a keep-out zone (KOZ) where no other circuitry can sit, and while hybrid bonding shrinks that zone a lot, it doesn't erase it; more elevators still costs some floor space.
The trick · IBT-BLSA

Balancing a seesaw electronically instead of with weights

A sense amp needs two balanced inputs to tell "1" from "0." The old way to balance a short edge bitline was to physically add a matching dummy subarray on the other side — like adding counterweights to a seesaw. IBT-BLSA instead electronically compensates for the imbalance (pre-boosting the shorter side's signal), so you never need the counterweight at all — saving the area a matched dummy array would cost.

Where it breaks: electronic compensation isn't free either — IBT-BLSA still costs about 8% area (vs. 25–33% for dummy schemes), and it needs extra, precisely-timed control signals the simple scheme didn't.
The constraint · tFAW / IDD7

The circuit breaker at the panel

tFAW is your house's circuit breaker: it doesn't care how fast an individual appliance runs, only how much total current is drawn at once. Buying smaller appliances (shrinking row size) lets you run a few more before tripping it — but there's a floor, because even small appliances still need power just to move data around the house (data-movement current). Moving appliances closer to the outlets (shortening wires) cuts the "line loss" component of that draw far more effectively.

Where it breaks: a real breaker just trips; DRAM's version is a soft scheduling limit (the memory controller simply won't issue a 5th ACT inside the tFAW window) — nothing burns out, you just don't get the bandwidth.
The waste · Atom size

Buying a 32-pack to get one bottle

HBM moves data in 32-byte "atoms" — like a store that only sells soda in 32-bottle cases. A GUPS-style update only needs 8 bytes (one bottle), but every access still hauls the full case through the store, the truck, and the warehouse — burning energy and internal bandwidth on 24 bytes nobody asked for. FB-G-HBM's 8B mode is the store finally agreeing to sell single bottles.

Where it breaks: selling single bottles needs new registers at checkout — FB-G-HBM's 8B mode needs a new command (SM_RD/SM_WR) and extra address wires, and it only helps when an access genuinely needs less than 32B; regular workloads gain nothing from it.
Section by section

How FB-HBM actually works

Baseline bottleneck → why data movement dominates → the 3-D fold → solving the two folding challenges → grains in the base die → the small-atom finish. Numbers are as reported in the paper (HBM3-calibrated, projected to HBM4/FB-HBM).

1 · The baseline bottleneck: two equations, one growing gap

Regular bandwidth (Eq. 1) is an I/O question — Br = min(Satom/tCCDS, Satom/tBURST) — and it scales cleanly with pin speed: 25.6 GB/s per channel for HBM3, 32 GB/s for the paper's projected Iso-HBM4. Irregular bandwidth (Eq. 2) is an activation-frequency question — Bir = min(n·Satom/tRC, Satom/tRRDS, ACTsInFAW·Satom/tFAW) — and for HBM3 it's bounded by tRC at 11.38 GB/s, a 2.25× gap versus regular. Because Iso-HBM4 leaves tRC, tRRD, and tFAW essentially unchanged while doubling Br, that gap widens to 2×. Nothing about faster signaling closes it.

2 · Why data movement, not just row size, drives the current

The paper's IDD7 current model (Eq. 3) splits current into an activation term and a data-movement term. Measuring HBM3 and projecting to HBM4, it finds four design factors that inflate irregular-bandwidth-limiting current: wide rows (16 mats, high per-ACT current), a central TSV array (49% of energy from long-distance LSA→TSV movement), tall banks (long LSA→GSA MDL runs), and the 32B atom (wasted movement for sub-32B accesses like GUPS). Reducing row size only attacks the first factor; the other three are all forms of data-movement distance or volume — which is why row-size reduction alone plateaus (Primer 6) and why the paper's fix is architectural.

3 · The 3-D fold: one bank, eight dies, one base die

FB-HBM assumes an 8-Hi stack and splits each bank's subarrays into 8 folds, one per DRAM die, each capable of activating independently. The GSAs that would normally sit at a (tall) bank's edge move to a new base die beneath the stack, shared by all 8 folds of a bank. MDL and CSL wires — previously routed horizontally across a whole die to reach the GSA — now route vertically, through TSV strips, straight down to the base die.

4 · Solving folding's two challenges

A 4-subarray fold is too short for the traditional way of giving edge arrays a reference bitline voltage — dummy subarrays cost 25% area, closed-bitline schemes 33%, half-length mats 25%; only Bitline-Emulated CMOS Capacitors got to 5%, but need per-chip fine-tuning that doesn't scale to production. FB-HBM instead uses IBT-BLSA: an amplifier design that senses correctly even when one bitline (BLB) is much shorter/lower-capacitance than the other (BLT), by pre-boosting the shorter side's signal. Its height is only 0.32× a normal subarray, for roughly 8% area overhead — down from 25%.

The second challenge is keeping multiple rows activated at once within a bank without extra latch circuitry. FB-HBM's answer is a split address-decoding scheme: a global decoder on the base die pre-decodes addresses and routes them, by fold ID, to small sub-row decoders on each DRAM die — each sub-row decoder holds one MWL assertion per fold. Because addresses reach different folds over distinct TSVs, commands to different folds can even beat tRRDS/tCCDS.

5 · Grains and FB-G-HBM: where the selection logic lives matters

The grain architecture (borrowed from prior FGDRAM work) segments the MWL to shrink the effective row — but where you put the extra "is this grain selected?" AND logic determines whether it's practical. Embedding it inside every LWD needs 4–6 transistors per LWL (nearly doubling LWD transistor count) and roughly doubles mat size — impractical. Pre-combining signals to replace one AND with a NAND cuts that to 3 transistors/LWL but still costs 24% pitch-mismatch overhead. Placing the extra gate in the LSA–LWD corner (as FGDRAM proposed) collides with I/O-switch circuitry already there and disrupts the mat grid, inflating subarray area 3.5% for 4 grains.

6 · The last mile: base-die data movement and the 8B atom

Even after folding and grains, activation parallelism caps around 4.4× because data still has to move across the base die — a distance that folding, unlike everything else, cannot shrink (it's 2.5D, not 3D, at that layer). FB-G-HBM's answer is to reduce the volume instead: an optional 8B read/write mode reusing the grain architecture's ability to fetch fewer bits per column select. It costs a new SM_RD/SM_WR command, two extra address wires, and a modest PHY-area increase — offset by reassigning some of HBM3's over-provisioned column-address pins to row addresses, so the final pin count only grows from 72 to 76 per channel.

◆
The pattern across all three fixes. Folding shortens the wire. Base-die grain logic avoids adding transistors where none fit. The 8B atom shrinks what has to move at all. Every FB-(G-)HBM mechanism is a variation on the same lever the IDD7 model pointed at: cut data-movement current, don't just cut row size.
Put real numbers on it

Worked examples

Four calculations redone from the paper's own tables and equations, then an interactive widget reproducing Section 3.1's central finding. All arithmetic below is shown and independently recomputed from the paper's stated inputs.

Example 1 — the regular/irregular bandwidth gap (Eq. 1 & 2, HBM3)

HBM3: Satom=32B, tCCDS=tBURST=1.25 ns, n=16 banks, tRAS=29 ns, tRP=16 ns (so tRC=45 ns), tRRDS=2 ns, tFAW=16 ns, ACTsInFAW=8.

  1. Regular bandwidth: Br = min(32/1.25, 32/1.25) = 25.6 GB/s/channel — matches the paper exactly.
  2. Irregular, tRC term: n·Satom/tRC = 16×32/45 = 512/45 = 11.38 GB/s.
  3. Irregular, tRRDS term: Satom/tRRDS = 32/2 = 16 GB/s. tFAW term: 8×32/16 = 16 GB/s.
  4. Bir = min(11.38, 16, 16) = 11.38 GB/s — the tRC term binds. Gap vs. regular: 25.6/11.38 ≈ 2.25×, exactly as reported.
◆
Takeaway: nothing about tBURST or tCCDS appears in Bir at all — irregular bandwidth genuinely lives in a different equation, so scaling I/O pins (which is all regular-bandwidth scaling has done) cannot touch it.

Example 2 — distributed-TSV area, from wire count to mm²

FB-G-HBM routes 128 MDLs + 16 CSLs + 16 CAs + 8 LWDEns = 168 vertical connections per bank. At 16 banks/channel × 64 channels/stack:

  1. Total connections: 168 × 16 × 64 = 168 × 1024 = 172,032 — matches the paper's stated total exactly.
  2. Raw area at hybrid bonding's 40,000 connections/mm²: 172,032 ÷ 40,000 = 4.30 mm².
  3. With KOZ overhead: the paper reports 4.92 mm² once keep-out zones are included — about 14% added for KOZ, consistent with hybrid bonding's much-reduced (but nonzero) keep-out cost versus the ~2.5×-pitch KOZ of older TSV technology.
◇
Sanity check: the paper's own comparison points — Iso-HBM4's TSV block at 3.84 mm² and FGDRAM's at 4.65 mm² (Table 7) — are for far fewer per-bank connections (channel/data TSVs only, no distributed MDL/CSL routing), so FB-G-HBM's larger 4.92 mm² buys a capability (per-fold independent activation) those designs don't have, not just "more area for the same thing."

Example 3 — grain-logic wiring overhead, verified two ways

A 4-grain bank needs 4 new selector wires beyond the existing 128 MWL + 4 LWLSel + 16 LDL = 148 wires already routed under a bank.

  1. 4-grain wiring overhead: 4/148 = 2.70% — matches the paper's reported 2.7% for FGDRAM, and FB-G-HBM's net-four-new-wires design (swap 4 LWLSel for 8 LWDEn) lands at the identical 2.7%.
  2. 8-grain wiring overhead: 8/148 = 5.41% — matches the paper's reported 5.4% for VFG-DRAM/8-grain FB-G-HBM.
  3. Subarray-area check (Table 7): FG-DRAM's 4-grain subarray grows from 4042.94 µm² to 4101.14 µm²: (4101.14−4042.94)/4042.94 = 1.44% ≈ the paper's "1.4%." At ~60% of die area being subarrays: 1.44%×0.60 = 0.86% ≈ the paper's "0.8%" die-level figure.
  4. 8-grain (VFG-DRAM) subarray-area check: (4178.74−4042.94)/4042.94 = 3.36% ≈ "3.4%"; die-level: 3.36%×0.60 = 2.02% ≈ "2.0%."
●
Takeaway: FB-G-HBM matches FGDRAM's wiring overhead (2.7%/5.4%) but avoids the extra ~0.6% transistor overhead FGDRAM pays for on-die grain logic, since FB-G-HBM's selection logic sits on the base die instead — a small but real net area win, and a clean illustration of "moving logic off-die costs wiring, not transistors."

Example 4 — GUPS energy per bit, end to end (Table 6)

Table 6 splits total energy for GUPS into a data-movement component and a row-activation component (both scaled ×4 from the STREAM baseline for the non-FB-G-HBM designs, since GUPS accesses only 8 of a 32B atom's bytes but those designs still move the full atom).

  1. Iso-HBM4: data-movement 2.55 pJ/b × 4 = 10.21 (rounding) + row-activation 5.80 = 16.02 pJ/b total (Table 6 confirms exactly).
  2. FB-G-HBM: data-movement 0.58 pJ/b (already 8B-native, no ×4 penalty) + row-activation 1.45 = 2.03 pJ/b total (confirmed).
  3. Improvement: 16.02 ÷ 2.03 ≈ 7.9× less energy per bit for a random GUPS access, FB-G-HBM vs. Iso-HBM4.
▲
Don't conflate this with the abstract's "4×." The paper's abstract separately states FB-HBM (not FB-G-HBM) "improves energy efficiency for GUPS by 4×" versus a different baseline comparison not broken out in Table 6. The 7.9× figure above is Table 6's FB-G-HBM-vs-Iso-HBM4 comparison specifically — a different pair of designs than the abstract's headline number, even though both describe "GUPS energy efficiency."

Check yourself

Practice

Work the answer before opening it. Each answer ends with the misconception the question is designed to catch.

Warm-up
Using Eq. 1, what is Iso-HBM4's peak regular per-channel bandwidth if tCCDS = tBURST = 1 ns and Satom = 32B?
Show answer
Br = min(32B/1ns, 32B/1ns) = 32 GB/s per channel — matches Table 1's "Sub Ch BW" row for Iso-HBM4. Catches: confusing the per-channel model output with the whole-stack "peak provisioned bandwidth" (2048 GB/s = 32 GB/s × 64 channels). They're the same number at two different scopes.
Diagnose
A colleague says "HBM4's 16 Gbps pins should make random accesses ~2× faster than HBM3's, since pin speed roughly doubled." Using the irregular-bandwidth equation, is that right?
Show answer
No. Bir depends on tRC, tRRDS, and tFAW — none of which involve pin speed. Table 1 shows these row-timing parameters are essentially unchanged from HBM3 to Iso-HBM4 (tRC=45ns both, tFAW=16ns both). So irregular bandwidth stays capped near 16 GB/s regardless of how fast the pins run, while regular bandwidth doubles — which is exactly why the gap widens to 2× instead of staying flat. Catches: assuming "faster memory generation" uniformly improves all access patterns. Pin speed is a regular-bandwidth lever only; irregular bandwidth needs a row-timing/current-budget lever instead.
Diagnose
Why does "just add more banks by using a taller stack" only shift the irregular-bandwidth bottleneck from tRC to tFAW (16 GB/s) rather than actually fixing the problem?
Show answer
Because Bir is a minimum over three terms. More banks raises n, which raises the n·Satom/tRC term — but tFAW still hard-caps ACTs per channel at a fixed rate (ACTsInFAW × Satom / tFAW = 8×32/16 = 16 GB/s) regardless of how many banks exist. You've removed one bottleneck only to expose the next one in the same min() — the underlying current budget never moved. Catches: treating "more banks" as a general-purpose fix. It only helps the specific term it targets (tRC); the paper's own point is that lasting improvement needs to attack the current budget itself (Section 3.1), which is what folding does.
Design
FB-G-HBM's IDD7 budget analysis finds room for 71 simultaneous activations per tFAW at the 500 mA current limit, yet the paper caps the design at 64. Why cap below the current-budget-derived maximum?
Show answer
Because ACT concurrency isn't the only ceiling — channel bandwidth is the other one. The paper states 64 ACTs are already enough to fully utilize the channel's 32 GB/s bandwidth at FB-G-HBM's 8B atom size; pushing to 71 would only draw more current for no additional deliverable bandwidth, since the DQ pins would already be saturated. The binding constraint at that point shifts from IDD7/current to I/O bandwidth. Catches: assuming "more headroom in one constraint = better," without checking whether a second, independent constraint (bandwidth, not current) has already become the real limit.
Design
The paper reports "6.7× higher [bandwidth]" (Figure 13, FB-G-HBM vs. Iso-HBM4 at 8B) and separately "6.74× speedup" (Figure 14, GUPS, FB-G-HBM vs. HBM3). A reader assumes these are the same number stated twice. Are they?
Show answer
No — different metrics and different baselines, despite the near-identical value. The 6.7× is an effective-bandwidth ratio (GB/s at 8B random access) with Iso-HBM4 as the baseline. The 6.74× is an application-level speedup (full GUPS workload runtime, through the GPU cache hierarchy and memory scheduler) with HBM3 as the baseline. That they land within 0.6% of each other for this one workload is very likely coincidental — GUPS happens to be nearly all-irregular-bandwidth-bound, so the app speedup tracks the raw bandwidth ratio unusually closely. Catches: pattern-matching on similar-looking numbers without checking what's actually being measured (bandwidth vs. wall-clock speedup) or against what baseline (Iso-HBM4 vs. HBM3).
Loose ends

Frequently asked

The questions that come up once the mechanism clicks.

QWhat exactly is a "fold"?▾
A fold is the fraction of a single bank's subarrays that lives on one DRAM die of the 8-Hi FB-HBM stack. A conventional bank has all its subarrays on one die; FB-HBM's version of "the same bank" spans all 8 dies, with each die holding one-eighth of the subarrays as an independently-activatable fold. GSAs, previously part of the bank on one die, move down to the shared base die.
QIsn't 3D die-stacking with TSVs already how HBM works — what's actually new?▾
HBM already stacks dies vertically, but for channel data movement through a centralized TSV stripe — banks themselves stay conventional 2-D structures on a single die. FB-HBM's novelty is spanning a single bank's internal structure (subarrays, GSAs, decoders) across the dies via distributed TSVs, going beyond prior 3D-stacked memory architecture proposals that the paper cites (Loh 2008) as a starting point but extends.
QWhy is irregular bandwidth so much worse than regular, physically?▾
Regular (sequential, open-row) accesses reuse an already-activated row repeatedly, so the bottleneck is just how fast data can be clocked out over the I/O pins (tCCD/tBURST). Irregular (random) accesses usually need a new row activation almost every time, and activation is throttled by tRC/tRRD/tFAW — timing parameters that exist to bound current draw, not signaling delay. Two entirely different physical limits govern the two access patterns.
QWhy not just keep shrinking DRAM rows, like FGDRAM does?▾
Because the paper's own IDD7 model shows row-size reduction alone plateaus at 1.65× more simultaneous activations (39% of baseline current) — it only attacks the activation-current term, leaving the data-movement-current term untouched. FGDRAM also runs into a real circuit-layout problem: smaller rows need more local wordline drivers (LWD), especially at bank edges, and existing chip layouts don't have spare area for that without disrupting the pitch-matched LWD/MWL grid.
QWhat is hybrid bonding and why does the pitch (5 µm) matter?▾
Hybrid bonding is a die-to-die (or die-to-wafer) bonding technique using direct Cu-Cu connections instead of solder microbumps, letting connections be packed far more densely. At a conservative 5 µm pitch, the paper estimates ~40,000 vertical connections per mm² — a 363× density improvement over microbumps — which is what makes routing 168 signals per bank vertically (172,032 total per stack) physically fit. Smaller pitch also shrinks the keep-out zone (KOZ) around each connection, further saving area.
QDoes FB-HBM sacrifice DRAM density, or need new fabs?▾
The paper states FB-HBM achieves its gains "without sacrificing DRAM density." It does, however, require several unresolved manufacturing steps: thinned dies below 30 µm (or stronger on-die drivers as an alternative), a wholly custom base die, and — since small-atom commands and folded-bank sub-row activation aren't in any JEDEC HBM spec — industry standardization before it could ship. The paper explicitly frames these as open challenges, not solved problems.
QWhat's the actual difference between FB-HBM and FB-G-HBM?▾
FB-HBM is the base contribution: fold the bank across dies + base-die logic, keeping HBM3's 1024B row and 32B atom. FB-G-HBM layers the other two levers on top: it segments each bank into 4 independent 256B-row grains (with grain-select logic on the base die, avoiding FGDRAM's area problems) and adds an optional 8B atom mode. FB-HBM alone already matches FGDRAM's bandwidth by shortening data movement instead of shrinking rows; FB-G-HBM combines both levers for the paper's headline 6.7×/6.74× numbers.

📌 Summary card · as of 2026-08-30 · ISCA'25 paper

  • What: FB-HBM re-lays each HBM bank as an 8-way 3-D "folded" structure across the dies of an 8-Hi stack, plus a new base logic die holding shared GSAs/decoders.
  • Why: irregular bandwidth is bounded by tFAW/IDD7 current, not I/O speed; the paper's model shows shortening data-movement distance beats shrinking row size alone (2.4× vs. 1.65×, synergistic to 7.2×).
  • Mechanism: hybrid-bonded, thin-pitch (5 µm) TSVs carry short vertical MDL/CSL wires; IBT-BLSA removes dummy-subarray overhead (25%→8%); split global/sub-row decoding gives per-fold parallel activation.
  • FB-G-HBM extension: adds base-die grain-selection logic (4×256B grains) and an optional 8B atom (SM_RD/SM_WR) for GUPS-class accesses.
  • Headline results: 6.7× effective irregular bandwidth at 8B (1350 vs. 201 GB/s vs. Iso-HBM4); 6.74× GUPS and 5.02× GNN application speedup over HBM3; streaming stack power cut 39W→11.3W.
  • Area cost: fold overhead 25%→8% (IBT-BLSA); grain-logic wiring overhead 2.7%/5.4% (4/8 grains); ~4.92 mm² for FB-G-HBM's distributed TSV block per stack.
  • Open challenges (paper's own list): <30 µm die thinning (or stronger drivers) for manufacturing yield; a custom base die; JEDEC standardization of SM_RD/SM_WR and sub-row activation primitives.
  • Not the same as ordinary HBM 3D die-stacking (which stacks channels, not bank internals) — and FB-HBM's "8× activation parallelism" is structural, capped well below 8× in practice by the 500 mA IDD7 current budget.
