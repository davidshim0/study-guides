SOSP 2026 · A Field Guide

Packing a GPU full can quietly make it hungrier, not leaner

Sharing one GPU across several models is supposed to save resources. But chase utilization alone and you can accidentally raise the electric bill. This is the plain-English story of EnerTune, a serving system that treats joules as a first-class citizen.

📄 "Beyond Utilization" · Sinha, Liakopoulos, Lemma, Yadwadkar (UT Austin) 🎓 GPU/inference-serving basics assumed — the primers cover them ⏱ ~30 min read
⚡

Choose placement, GPU allocation, batch size, and frequency by the ENERGY they cost — not by how FULL the GPU looks.
A packed GPU that runs hot and slow can burn more power than two GPUs run gently.

Quick glossary

Acronyms you'll hit immediately

Defined once, up front, so the rest of the guide reads smoothly.

SLOService-Level Objective — the latency bar (usually p99) a deployment must not cross.
MIGNVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU — hardware-isolated slices of one GPU; EnerTune's default multiplexing mechanism.

OLSOrdinary Least Squares — the regression EnerTune uses to fit its per-model power/latency polynomials.

Before you begin

Eight ideas the paper assumes you know

Collapsed by default — open any that's unfamiliar.

energy-aware / good decision tunable knob EnerTune machinery energy-agnostic baseline
1GPU sharing: why multiplex at all?▾

Production GPU fleets sit at only 10–50% utilization, because most inference models (small CNNs, speech, mid-size LLMs) don't need a whole GPU's worth of compute or memory. Running one model per GPU wastes the rest.

Spatial multiplexing splits a single GPU's resources among several models running concurrently, instead of one model owning the whole chip.

Now you're ready: EnerTune's whole job is deciding which models share a GPU, and at what settings — not whether to share at all.
2MIG and MPS — two ways to share a GPU▾

MIG physically partitions a GPU into isolated instances with their own compute and memory — strong isolation, fault tolerance, but coarser-grained slices. MPS instead lets multiple processes share a single GPU context and execute concurrently in software — finer-grained, but with more interference risk between colocated jobs.

Now you're ready: EnerTune uses MIG by default for safety, but its power/energy models generalize to MPS too (§7.4).
3Power vs. energy — and why the difference matters▾

Power (Watts) is how fast a GPU is drawing electricity right now. Energy (Joules) is power integrated over time: E = ∫P dt. A GPU can draw less power but run longer, and end up consuming more energy — or vice versa.

Now you're ready: almost every counterintuitive result in this paper — frequency scaling, sharing at low vs. high load — comes down to this power×time tradeoff.
4GPU allocation size and batch size▾

Allocation size is the fraction of a GPU's compute/memory given to one model (e.g., a 30% MIG slice). Batch size is how many requests a model processes together in one forward pass. Both trade latency against throughput and power.

Now you're ready: EnerTune treats these as two knobs that must be tuned jointly, not one after the other.
5GPU frequency scaling (DVFS)▾

A GPU's compute clock can be pinned below its maximum. Lower frequency draws less power but takes longer per operation — CMOS theory says dynamic power scales roughly with frequency cubed (P ∝ f³), while latency grows as frequency drops.

Now you're ready: because colocated models share one physical clock, frequency is a device-wide setting, not a per-model one — a recurring constraint in this paper.
6Static vs. dynamic power▾

Static power is drawn just by having the GPU powered on, regardless of workload. Dynamic power is the extra draw from actually doing work, and depends on frequency, batch size, and allocation.

Now you're ready: EnerTune further splits dynamic power into a model-specific part and a model-agnostic "shared components" part — the key to estimating multiplexed-GPU power correctly.
7Bin-packing for cluster scheduling▾

Deciding which models go on which GPUs, at what configuration, is a bin-packing problem: GPUs are bins, models are items with a resource "size," and you want to pack tightly while meeting constraints (here, latency SLOs).

Now you're ready: EnerTune's twist is packing by a cost function (energy or power), not just by how much fits — and reasoning about a device-wide frequency shared by everything in a bin.
8Profiling — and why it gets expensive▾

To know how a model behaves under a given allocation/batch/frequency combination, you typically have to run it and measure. With 450+ possible configurations per model and hundreds to thousands of models in a real fleet, brute-force profiling costs real GPU-hours — and real energy.

Now you're ready: EnerTune's whole "Config Characterizer" exists to shrink this cost by measuring only a small sample and predicting the rest.
The whole story in one place

EnerTune in a nutshell

The problem, the fix, and how the pieces connect.

Modern GPU fleets are underutilized (10–50%), so serving systems multiplex several models onto one GPU to use it more fully. The trouble: state-of-the-art multiplexers optimize for utilization, not energy — and the two can pull in opposite directions. Placing two memory-hungry models together can inflate latency so much that the energy saved by sharing hardware is wiped out by running longer; a single bad SDXL colocation costs ~15% more energy than not sharing at all (Fig. 2), and system-wide, energy-agnostic multiplexing can inflate total energy by up to 1.62× over an energy-optimal Oracle (Fig. 1).

EnerTune's fix is to make power and energy first-order objectives, sitting alongside utilization and SLOs. It does this with two analytical models built from a small amount of profiling: one estimates a single model's power/latency/throughput across its ~450 deployment configurations (allocation × batch × frequency) from measuring only 15% of them; the other estimates the power draw of several models sharing one GPU, by correctly separating power that belongs to each model from power that's paid once per GPU regardless of who's running.

An energy-aware bin-packing algorithm then uses these estimates to jointly decide placement, GPU allocation, batch size, and — critically — a shared GPU frequency for each group of colocated models, since frequency is a single device-wide knob. At runtime, a lightweight tuner nudges frequency and batch size as load drifts, escalating to full replanning only for large shifts.

The payoff: across four state-of-the-art multiplexing baselines and real Azure traces, EnerTune cuts energy by 1.4–2.3× and power by 1.3–2.6×, while continuing to meet latency SLOs.

⚡ Key takeaways

  • Higher GPU utilization does not guarantee lower energy — a bad colocation can cost more energy than not sharing at all.
  • Energy is power integrated over time (E=∫Pdt): a lower-power, longer-running config can lose to a higher-power, faster one.
  • An analytical model, fit from just 15% of ~450 configurations per model, predicts power/latency/throughput for the rest.
  • Naively summing per-model power overestimates shared-GPU power draw — some components are paid once per GPU, not once per model.
  • Colocated models share one GPU frequency, so placement and frequency decisions must be made jointly, not layered on top of each other.
  • Result: 1.4–2.3× less energy, 1.3–2.6× less power, SLOs still met, <3.4s of scheduling overhead.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about EnerTune

Each stands alone. Every card ends with where the picture stops being true.

The core idea · Utilization vs. energy

A packed moving truck that burns more gas

Fill a truck to the brim with boxes and it looks maximally "used" — but if two of those boxes are so heavy and awkwardly stacked that the truck has to crawl in low gear the whole trip, you may burn more fuel than making two lighter, faster trips. Cramming a GPU full of the wrong model pair works the same way: high utilization, worse energy.

Where it breaks: a truck's fuel use doesn't depend on which boxes touch which — GPUs have real interaction effects (shared memory controllers), so the "bad pairing" penalty is often larger than simple weight-and-drag intuition suggests.
Frequency sharing · The C4 constraint

Carpooling at one speed

Four coworkers carpool to work. One has a 9am meeting and needs the highway; the others would happily go the scenic, fuel-sipping route. But it's one car, one speed — everyone rides at whatever pace the most time-pressed rider needs. Colocated GPU models share one physical clock the same way: the most latency-sensitive model in the group sets the frequency floor for all of them.

Where it breaks: carpool riders can renegotiate the route daily; on a GPU, EnerTune can also choose who rides with whom in the first place — grouping models with similar frequency needs so nobody is forced to speed unnecessarily.
Colocation power · Not simple addition

One water heater, several apartments

Two studio apartments each with their own water heater use twice the standby energy of one duplex sharing a single heater. Add a second tenant to a shared heater and you don't pay for a whole new heater's idle draw — only for the extra hot water they actually use. GPUs have shared components (memory controllers, cooling) with their own "idle" cost; summing each model's isolated power double-counts that shared cost per extra tenant.

Where it breaks: apartment tenants don't fight over water pressure; colocated models do contend for the shared memory controller's bandwidth, so usage isn't perfectly separable even after removing the double-count.
Profiling · Sample, then predict

Tasting a few dishes to price the whole menu

A caterer doesn't cook and weigh every one of 450 possible meal combinations to know their calorie counts — they measure a well-chosen handful, notice the pattern (protein adds roughly this much, starch that much), and fit a formula that predicts the rest. EnerTune profiles just 15% of a model's configurations and fits a polynomial over frequency, batch size, and allocation to estimate the other 85%.

Where it breaks: the caterer's formula assumes ingredients combine smoothly; if a model's behavior has a sharp discontinuity the sampled 15% missed entirely, the fitted curve can mispredict that region — hence choosing samples carefully (Latin Hypercube Sampling) rather than at random.
Runtime tradeoff · Sharing isn't always cheaper

Splitting a taxi vs. two solo rides

Sharing a taxi with a stranger saves gas per person — unless it makes the ride so roundabout that you're both in the car twice as long, at which point two direct solo rides might use less fuel overall. At low load, sharing a GPU wins (small latency cost, big static-power savings from needing fewer GPUs); at high load, the extra queuing/contention latency can outweigh those savings, and isolated execution wins instead.

Where it breaks: taxi-sharing decisions are made once per ride; GPU load keeps shifting mid-"ride," so EnerTune has to keep re-evaluating whether to stay shared or split apart as demand changes.
Bin-packing · Sort, then merge

Laundry loads by temperature

You don't wash every garment separately, but you also don't throw a delicate silk blouse in with heavy-duty denim on a hot cycle — you sort by temperature first, then combine small loads of the same setting to avoid running half-empty machines. EnerTune first groups models by frequency need, then merges under-full "bins" (GPUs) across frequency tiers — biasing toward lower, cheaper settings — while still meeting each item's care label (SLO).

Where it breaks: laundry loads don't get "faster" if you turn up the heat; some GPU workloads do speed up meaningfully with higher frequency, which is exactly why EnerTune sometimes merges bins upward in frequency too, when it reduces the total machines (GPUs) needed.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Section by section, with the paper's key relationships rebuilt as visuals.

1 · The problem — same GPUs, very different energy

Serving systems that spatially multiplex GPUs (GPULets, ParvaGPU, FGD, Usher) pack models to raise utilization, but treat energy as an afterthought. Colocating models with heavy shared-memory pressure inflates latency, and since E=∫Pdt, that extra time can outweigh the power saved by needing fewer GPUs.

2 · Estimating one model's power (Config Characterizer)

Per-model dynamic power depends on frequency (near-cubic, from CMOS theory: P∝V²f, V∝f ⇒ P∝f³), batch size (sublinear — pipelines saturate), and allocation (model-specific). EnerTune fits a cubic multivariate polynomial over these three knobs plus their interaction terms, using Ordinary Least Squares over just 15% of configurations sampled via Latin Hypercube Sampling:

P_D(f,b,a) = Σᵢ (β_i,f·fⁱ + β_i,b·bⁱ + β_i,a·aⁱ) + Σ β_ijk·fⁱ·bʲ·aᵏ   # i,j,k ≤ 3, cubic terms
⚡
Removing any one term category (frequency, batch, or allocation) from this model spikes per-model power error from 5.2% to 21–43% (§7.3) — each knob's nonlinearity genuinely matters, none can be dropped for simplicity.

3 · Estimating shared-GPU power (Colocation Power Estimator)

GPUs power-gate unused compute units, and some components (L2 cache, memory controllers) draw power in an idle part (paid whenever any model runs) plus an active part (scales with that model's usage). Summing each model's isolated dynamic power (a prior approach) double-counts the idle, model-agnostic part once per model instead of once per GPU.

4 · Deciding placement and frequency (EPA Resource Planner)

Exhaustive search is exponential in the number of models; EnerTune instead reduces this to a two-step bin-packing pass (Algorithm 1) with polynomial complexity, about O(N³(BA)⁴):

Step 1 — Bin by frequency
Give each model its lowest-cost SLO-meeting config; group compatible models sharing a frequency
→
Step 2a — Merge downward
Fold higher-frequency bins into lower-frequency ones when SLOs still hold (lower freq ⇒ usually lower power)
→
Step 2b — Merge upward by cost spread
If GPU budget is tight, merge remaining bins upward, prioritizing the cheapest frequency increases first
💡
Why bin by frequency first? Since colocated models are stuck with one shared clock, grouping by frequency need upfront avoids ever discovering — after placement is already fixed — that two models actually wanted very different speeds.

5 · Adapting at runtime (Runtime GPU Tuner)

As load drifts (±α/β% per model), the Tuner re-evaluates feasible frequency/batch-size combinations within the existing allocation, picking the lowest-cost one that still meets SLOs — with no downtime. From the paper's own trace walkthrough (Fig. 12, §7.2):

  1. Small load increase → raise batch size only, not frequency, to absorb it cheaply.
  2. Noticeable load drop (two points in the trace) → cut frequency by ~600 MHz, saving 50–60 W each time.
  3. Load grows again → raise frequency while trimming batch size back down to meet demand.
  4. Large or infeasible shift → escalate to the EPA Resource Planner for full replanning (triggered at >20% or <40% cluster load deviation, tuned by grid search).
Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four traces using the paper's own reported numbers.

① Checking Stable Diffusion's configuration-space variation

The paper reports: 450+ configurations, latency 0.036–3.25 min, throughput 9.83–166.3 req/min, energy 353–4747 J/request. Do the stated ratios check out?

  1. Latency variation: 3.25 ÷ 0.036 = 90.3× — matches the paper's "90.3× latency variation" exactly.
  2. Throughput variation: 166.3 ÷ 9.83 = 16.92× — matches "16.9× throughput variation."
  3. Energy variation: 4747 ÷ 353 = 13.45× — matches "13.4× variation in energy per request."
  4. Insight: throughput and latency don't move in lockstep with energy — the worst-latency config isn't automatically the worst-energy one, which is exactly why a joint model (not a single knob) is needed.
⚡
This is the scale of the problem Challenge 1 names: hundreds of configurations, each pulling latency/throughput/energy in different directions — brute-force profiling of even one such model is expensive.

② The profiling energy budget for 11 small models

The paper states profiling 11 small models (<6B params) takes >60 GPU-hours (5–6 hrs/model) and consumes >5 MJ (0.1–0.9 MJ per model, 0.53 MJ average). Does the average check out?

  1. Time: 11 models × ~5.5 hrs/model average ≈ 60.5 GPU-hours — matches ">60 GPU-hours."
  2. Energy: 11 models × 0.53 MJ average = 5.83 MJ — matches ">5 MJ" (and sits inside the stated 0.1–0.9 MJ per-model range).
  3. Why it matters: 5.83 MJ ≈ 1.6 kWh just to profile 11 models once. Real fleets serve hundreds to thousands of models and must re-profile after hardware/software changes or fine-tuning — brute force doesn't scale.
💡
EnerTune's 15%-sampling Config Characterizer directly targets this line item: §7.3 reports 7.3× average profiling-time reduction and 7.4× average profiling-energy reduction across the same baselines.

③ Why frequency scaling doesn't save energy cubically

Dynamic power should scale roughly as f³. Dropping GPT-J/Whisper/MobileNetV3's shared GPU from 1.41 GHz to 0.87 GHz should cut dynamic power to (0.87/1.41)³ ≈ 23.5% of its original value — a ~4.3× drop. But the paper reports only a 1.43× energy reduction. Why the gap?

  1. Static power doesn't scale with frequency the same way dynamic power does, so total power drops by less than the cubic dynamic-power law alone suggests.
  2. Latency rises as frequency drops — the GPU takes longer to finish the same work.
  3. Energy = power × time (E=∫Pdt): the power savings and the latency increase partially cancel, leaving net energy reduction far below the naive cubic estimate.
  4. Takeaway: you cannot predict energy savings from the power-frequency curve alone — you need the joint power and latency model EnerTune builds (§4.1, §C), which is exactly why the paper reports 1.43×, not ~4.3×.
⚠
Misreading the cubic power law as a cubic energy law is an easy mistake — the paper is explicit that latency inflation is what tempers the gain (§2.3).

④ Tallying EnerTune's end-to-end scheduling overhead

Is EnerTune's decision-making itself cheap? Sum the pieces reported in §7.6.

  1. Colocation Power Estimator: one shared-GPU power estimate takes 400 µs.
  2. EPA Resource Planner: generates an execution plan in <1.7 s (up to 1.5 min only at the extreme of 1000 models).
  3. Model loading: ranges 0.39 s (MobileNetV3) to 1.53 s (GPT-J-6B).
  4. GPU configuration (allocation, frequency, batch): <0.1 s.
  5. Total: 400µs + 1.7s + 1.53s + 0.1s ≈ 3.33 s, matching the paper's reported 2.24–3.38 s range — comparable to baseline overheads of 1.83–3.98 s.
💡
All of EnerTune's extra modeling machinery (profiling, power estimation, bin-packing) adds essentially no serving-time tax compared to systems that don't do any of this energy accounting.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer.

Problem 1 · easy
A cluster operator sees a GPU running at 95% utilization and concludes it must also be near its most energy-efficient point. Is this necessarily true?
Show answer
No. Utilization measures how "full" the GPU is, not how much power/energy that work costs. A 95%-utilized GPU running two energy-incompatible models at high frequency can consume more energy than the same GPU at slightly lower utilization running compatible models at a tuned frequency (§2.1, Observation #1).Misconception: that "not idle" means "efficient." Utilization and energy efficiency are different axes that can move independently.
Problem 2 · medium
Dynamic GPU power roughly follows P∝f³. If you halve the frequency, should you expect energy per request to also drop by roughly 8×?
Show answer
No. That cubic relationship is for power, not energy. Lower frequency increases per-request latency, and energy = power integrated over that longer time (E=∫Pdt). Static power also doesn't scale down with frequency the same way. The paper's real example (1.41→0.87 GHz, a 38% frequency cut) only achieves 1.43× less energy, not the much larger cubic-power-implied reduction.Misconception: conflating a power law with an energy law. Always fold in the latency (time) side before estimating energy impact.
Problem 3 · medium
Why does summing each colocated model's individually profiled dynamic power overestimate the shared GPU's actual power draw?
Show answer
Some GPU components (idle draw of shared memory controllers/caches, fan/auxiliary circuitry) are paid once per GPU regardless of how many models are running. Summing isolated per-model profiles counts that shared, model-agnostic cost once per model instead of once per GPU, inflating the total (§4.2, Eq. 3–4).Misconception: that GPU power is perfectly additive across concurrent workloads. Some of it is genuinely shared infrastructure, not a per-tenant cost.
Problem 4 · hard
A scheduler picks batch size for a model first (to hit a target throughput), then allocates just enough GPU share to support that batch size. The paper says this is energy-efficient for a single isolated model but can hurt cluster-wide energy. Why?
Show answer
Choosing batch first and then a minimal allocation tends to pick smaller GPU allocations, which reduces packing density — you need more GPUs to fit all models, and more GPUs means more static power drawn across the fleet. The paper's Whisper example: a 45% allocation with batch 2 uses 1.71× less energy than a 30% allocation with batch 4 for the same throughput, because the larger allocation cuts latency 2.18× even though it raises power 1.27× (§2.2, Observation #2) — allocation and batch must be decided jointly, cluster-wide, not per-model in isolation.Misconception: that a locally energy-optimal per-model choice is also cluster-wide optimal. Packing density (GPU count) is a hidden cost that per-model reasoning misses.
Problem 5 · medium
Why does composing an existing utilization-driven placement system (e.g., Usher) with a separate device-level frequency tuner (LithOS) still fall well short of EnerTune's energy savings?
Show answer
The placement system, being frequency-agnostic, may colocate models with very different frequency requirements. Since all colocated models share one clock, the device-level tuner is then forced to run at the frequency the most demanding model needs, even if others could tolerate much lower frequencies. Composing the two systems still leaves this mismatch unresolved — the paper shows EnerTune's jointly optimized placement+frequency still cuts energy 1.5–1.89× over each baseline-plus-LithOS combination (§7.5).Misconception: that layering a good device-level optimizer on top of any placement policy recovers most of the benefit. The placement decision itself determines how much headroom the device-level optimizer even has.
Problem 6 · easy
Is static power the same thing as the "P_O" term EnerTune introduces for shared GPUs?
Show answer
No — they're different. Static power (P_S) is drawn just by having the GPU powered on, independent of any workload, and is measured once with no processes running. P_O is a dynamic-power quantity: the model-agnostic part of the idle/active draw of shared components (memory controllers, fan/auxiliary circuitry) that's incurred whenever any model runs, but isn't specific to which model (§4.2, Eq. 3–4).Misconception: treating "not model-specific" as synonymous with "static." P_O is workload-dependent (present only when something runs) but not workload-specific (doesn't depend on which model).
Problem 7 · hard
The Runtime GPU Tuner sometimes escalates a decision to the EPA Resource Planner instead of adjusting frequency/batch locally. Name two conditions that trigger escalation, and why local tuning can't handle them.
Show answer
(1) Cluster load deviates beyond thresholds γ (up) or θ (down) from the planned load — a shift large enough that the current placement/allocation itself may no longer be a good fit, not just the frequency/batch settings. (2) Local frequency/batch adjustments are insufficient (SLOs still violated) or infeasible (e.g., not enough GPU memory for the needed batch size) — the Tuner is confined to the model's existing fixed allocation and can't grow it. Both cases need the Planner's ability to re-run placement, which the Tuner deliberately doesn't do, to avoid the overhead/disruption of full replanning on every minor fluctuation (§5.2).Misconception: that a local, low-overhead runtime tuner should always be able to fully absorb any load change. It's scoped to frequency and batch within a fixed allocation — bigger shifts genuinely need a new placement.
Problem 8 · medium
At low cluster load, is spatial sharing usually the better choice over giving each model its own dedicated GPU?
Show answer
Usually, yes, but it's a workload-specific trend rather than a universal rule. At low load, sharing tends to win: the paper shows completion time increases only ~5% while GPU power drops 1.54×, avoiding the static power of an extra GPU, netting 1.08–1.56× less energy (§2.4, Fig. 5). But at high load, isolated execution can win instead: it reduces completion time by ~1.33×, and that time savings outweighs the extra GPU's static cost, so sharing there increases energy by 1.14–1.25×.Misconception: that spatial multiplexing is unconditionally energy-better because it "uses fewer GPUs." The right answer flips with load — which is exactly why EnerTune must keep re-evaluating at runtime rather than fixing the decision once.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Tap any to expand.

Q1What's the actual difference between power and energy here?▾
Power (Watts) is an instantaneous rate; energy (Joules) is power integrated over the time a request takes (E=∫Pdt). A configuration can draw less power but take longer, ending up costing more energy than a higher-power, faster one — this tension underlies almost every design decision in the paper.
Q2Why can improving GPU utilization ever make energy worse?▾
Because multiplexing decisions guided only by "does it fit" can still colocate models that contend heavily for shared resources (e.g., memory bandwidth), inflating latency enough that the energy cost of running longer outweighs the energy saved by needing fewer GPUs (§2.1).
Q3What's the difference between MIG and MPS, and which does EnerTune use?▾
MIG hard-partitions a GPU into isolated instances with dedicated compute/memory — strong isolation, fault tolerance. MPS software-multiplexes processes on a shared GPU context — finer granularity, more interference risk. EnerTune uses MIG by default for safety/reliability, but also demonstrates compatibility with MPS (§3, §7.4).
Q4How does EnerTune avoid brute-force profiling every configuration?▾
It samples only 15% of a model's ~450 deployment configurations (via Latin Hypercube Sampling for good coverage), measures power/latency/throughput there, and fits cubic multivariate polynomials (over frequency, batch size, allocation) via OLS regression to estimate the remaining 85% (§4.1).
Q5Why does simply adding up each model's power overestimate shared GPU power?▾
Because some GPU components (idle draw of shared caches/memory controllers, auxiliary circuitry) are paid once per GPU, not once per colocated model. Summing isolated per-model profiles double-counts that shared cost for every extra model added (§4.2).
Q6Why can't each colocated model just run at its own preferred frequency?▾
GPU frequency is a single device-level setting shared by everything running on that chip — there's no per-process clock. So the model with the tightest latency needs effectively sets the floor for everyone sharing that GPU, which is why EnerTune groups models by similar frequency needs before placing them together (§2.4 C4, §5.1).
Q7How does the Runtime GPU Tuner decide when to change frequency vs. batch size?▾
It evaluates feasible (frequency, batch size) combinations within the model's existing GPU allocation using the analytical power/latency models, and picks the lowest-cost one that still meets all colocated models' SLOs — small load upticks often only need a bigger batch; load drops often allow a lower frequency (§5.2, Fig. 12).
Q8Does EnerTune always choose to share GPUs?▾
No. At low load, sharing usually wins on energy (small latency cost, avoids an extra GPU's static power). At high load, dedicating a GPU per model can win instead, since the latency savings from not contending can outweigh the static-power cost of the extra GPU (§2.4 C3, Fig. 5).
Q9Does this work for large language models, not just small CNNs?▾
Yes — §7.4 evaluates an LLM-only workload (six quantized LLMs, 8B–34B parameters) and reports 1.65–2.27× less energy while meeting SLOs, with power estimation error remaining low (4.63% per-model, 9.7% shared-GPU). The paper notes handling dynamically growing KV caches under multiplexing is left as future work.
Q10How much overhead does all this analysis add to serving?▾
Very little: a shared-GPU power estimate takes 400µs, generating a full execution plan takes under 1.7s (up to 1.5 min only for 1000 models), and total per-deployment overhead is 2.24–3.38s — comparable to baseline systems' 1.83–3.98s (§7.6).
Q11Can EnerTune optimize for carbon emissions instead of just energy?▾
The paper argues yes in principle: carbon derives from energy via grid carbon intensity plus a hardware-manufacturing "embodied" term, and extending the Resource Planner to minimize carbon instead of energy/power is framed as a straightforward extension of the same machinery, though not the paper's primary evaluation (§9, Appendix A).
Q12What did the authors leave for future work?▾
Formally decomposing P_O per hardware component as better instrumentation becomes available, controlling GPU memory frequency (currently only compute frequency is tuned), handling dynamically growing KV caches under multiplexed LLM serving, and improving shared-GPU power accuracy under MPS (currently higher error than under MIG) (§4.2, §5.2, §7.4, §D.5).

OracleAn exhaustive-search baseline used in evaluation that finds the energy-minimal, SLO-compliant placement over a short trace window.

Spatial multiplexingRunning multiple models concurrently on one GPU by dividing its resources, vs. time-multiplexing (one at a time).
FragmentationWasted GPU capacity left over when models don't pack tightly into bins/GPUs.
