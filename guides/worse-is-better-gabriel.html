Why the worse design keeps winning

One of Common Lisp's own designers turns a critical eye on his own community. Richard Gabriel names two competing philosophies of software design — the right thing and worse is better — and argues that the one everyone assumes is worse keeps beating the one everyone assumes is better, for reasons that have nothing to do with which is actually the better engineering.

⚡ TL;DR

  • Gabriel names two design philosophies: the right thing (correctness and consistency as near-absolute constraints, then completeness, then simplicity with the interface ranked above the implementation) and worse is better (implementation simplicity above everything, completeness sacrificed first), and argues real engineering cultures split along exactly this line.
  • He claims worse-is-better tends to win in practice: a simple, "basically right" partial solution is easy to port, spreads to weak hardware everywhere, resets user expectations, and then attracts far more collective improvement effort than a better-designed but heavier alternative ever gets.
  • He's an insider making this argument against his own side (he helped design Common Lisp/CLOS) — and his real point is a warning to that community, not a claim that simpler is always better regardless of whether the simple version is actually sound.

1Who is Richard Gabriel, and why is he criticizing his own side?▾

Gabriel was one of the principal designers of Common Lisp and its object system, CLOS — a genuine insider of the design tradition he calls "the right thing." This essay is an excerpt from a longer piece, "Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win Big," written in 1989 while he was at Lucid, Inc.

Now you're ready: that's why his claim that his own community is losing carries more weight than an outsider's complaint — he had a direct stake in the answer.
2The MIT approach vs. the New Jersey approach▾

Gabriel's two nicknames for two design cultures. The "MIT approach" (also "MIT/Stanford style") is his term for the-right-thing thinking. The "New Jersey approach" is his term for worse-is-better thinking, named for where Bell Labs — Unix and C's birthplace — is located.

3What "the right thing" means, precisely▾

Not a vague compliment: Gabriel defines it as a specific ranking of four design qualities (simplicity, correctness, consistency, completeness), where correctness ("Incorrectness is simply not allowed") and consistency ("as important as correctness") are treated as close to non-negotiable, all reasonably expected cases "must be covered", and simplicity, interface before implementation, gives way when it conflicts with those.

4What "worse is better" means, precisely▾

The same four qualities, reordered: implementation simplicity outranks everything else, including interface simplicity, and completeness is the first thing sacrificed whenever it threatens that simplicity. Gabriel says up front he's deliberately making this sound like an obviously bad philosophy — then spends the rest of the essay arguing it wins anyway.

5PC loser-ing, in full — you'll need this in The Story tab▾

Yes. PC here is "program counter," the CPU register that holds the address of the next instruction a program will execute. "Loser" was affectionate MIT-lab slang for a user (not an insult; see the ITS card below). Gabriel's own gloss: the PC "is being coerced into loser mode", that is, into user mode. So "PC loser-ing" means forcing the user program's program counter back to a clean user-mode instruction boundary (in the right-thing version, the system-call instruction itself) so the process can resume correctly.

Here's the concrete problem. A user program calls a system routine to do something that takes a while and touches real state — say, reading from a device into a buffer. Because invoking that system call is just one instruction from the user program's point of view, the program counter alone doesn't capture "how far the operation got" if an interrupt arrives mid-call. The operating system has to decide what happens next: does it quietly unwind everything and reset the user program's PC so that, on resumption, the program transparently re-enters the system call as if it had never been interrupted — or does it let the call simply return (possibly with a failure code) and leave it to the user program to notice and retry?

Now you're ready: The Story tab walks through exactly how MIT's ITS and Unix's answers to this differed — it's the single concrete example the whole essay's argument is built on.

7Common Lisp, CLOS, and ITS — the proper nouns this essay assumes you know▾

Common Lisp is a general-purpose programming language defined in the 1980s to unify several earlier, incompatible Lisp dialects (the first reference, Steele's Common Lisp: the Language, appeared in 1984; the ANSI standard was completed in 1994). Lisp dialects treat code and data uniformly and were the dominant language family in AI research from the 1960s into the 1980s.

CLOS (the Common Lisp Object System) is the object-oriented layer added to Common Lisp — it gives Lisp classes, inheritance, and generic functions that can dispatch on the types of multiple arguments at once, which is more flexible than the single-dispatch model most object-oriented languages use. Gabriel was one of CLOS's principal designers, which is exactly why this essay is an insider's critique, not an outsider's.

ITS (the Incompatible Timesharing System) was the operating system built at MIT's AI Lab starting in the late 1960s, running on PDP-6/PDP-10 hardware. It was famously open — no real security model or passwords by default — reflecting a small research community's culture of mutual trust. That same culture is where "loser" as affectionate slang for "user" came from.

The whole story in one place

Two philosophies, four qualities, one winner Gabriel didn't want

Rank the same four qualities two different ways, and you get two entire design cultures.

Gabriel frames the whole essay around a single move: take four design qualities nearly everyone would call good — simplicity, correctness, consistency, completeness — and show that two real engineering cultures rank them differently, with real consequences. He calls the first ordering the-right-thing and the second worse-is-better.

The-right-thing treats interface simplicity as more important than implementation simplicity, treats correctness as non-negotiable, treats consistency as roughly as important as correctness, and treats completeness as something simplicity is not allowed to "overly reduce". Gabriel's examples of this culture are Common Lisp (with CLOS) and Scheme.

Worse-is-better flips the priority on the very first quality: implementation simplicity outranks interface simplicity, and in fact outranks everything else on the list. Correctness can be traded away slightly for it; consistency can be traded away, especially at the interface; and completeness is the most expendable quality of all — sacrificed first whenever it threatens the implementation's simplicity. Gabriel's examples are early Unix and C.

He's explicit that he built the worse-is-better description to sound bad on purpose — and then argues that even in that deliberately unflattering form, it tends to beat the-right-thing in practice, because early Unix and C turned out to be, in his own words, "the ultimate computer viruses."

The MIT approach — priority order

  1. Correctness: non-negotiable ("simply not allowed" to be wrong)
  2. Consistency: weighed equal to correctness; may cost a little simplicity and completeness
  3. Completeness: protected: simplicity may not "overly reduce" it
  4. Interface simplicity, then implementation simplicity; the implementation may be complex

Order derived from the conflict clauses in each bullet; the essay lists simplicity first but lets the other three win conflicts with it. See Part II, M3, in the Why Worse Wins tab.

The New Jersey approach — priority order

  1. Implementation simplicity — above all else
  2. Interface simplicity — may be sacrificed for #1
  3. Correctness & consistency — may be slightly sacrificed
  4. Completeness — sacrificed first, whenever #1 is at risk

🍯 Key takeaways

  • Same four qualities, two rankings: the-right-thing and worse-is-better differ in what they sacrifice first, not in which qualities they value.
  • The one flip that matters most: which kind of simplicity comes first — the interface's (the-right-thing) or the implementation's (worse-is-better).
  • Gabriel's examples: Common Lisp/CLOS and Scheme for the-right-thing; early Unix and C for worse-is-better.
  • He deliberately caricatures worse-is-better to sound obviously bad, then argues it survives better anyway.
  • His label for the result: early Unix and C as "the ultimate computer viruses."
The anecdote that started it all

The MIT guy, the New Jersey guy, and the PC loser-ing problem

One meeting, one interrupted system call, and the whole essay in miniature.

  1. The setup. Gabriel recounts a meeting between two well-known figures — one from MIT, familiar with the MIT AI Lab's own operating system, ITS; the other from the Unix/Berkeley world. The MIT engineer had been reading Unix's source and wanted to know how it handled PC loser-ing.
  2. The right thing's answer. Fully back out of the interrupted operation and restore the user program's program counter, so that resuming the program transparently re-enters the system call on its own — the user program never has to know anything went wrong. This is the ITS-style answer.
  3. Unix's answer. Let the system routine simply finish (or fail) and return, sometimes with an error code. It's the calling program's job to check that code and retry in a loop if needed.
  4. The trade each side named out loud. The Unix engineer defended the design: Unix's philosophy was simplicity, the right thing was too complex, and programmers could easily insert the extra test and loop. The MIT engineer pointed out that Unix's internals were simple, but the resulting interface was harder to use correctly. The Unix engineer agreed, and said that was the right call, because implementation simplicity mattered more than interface simplicity.
  5. The parting line. The MIT engineer muttered that "sometimes it takes a tough man to make a tender chicken" (a Perdue chicken TV slogan, per the lecture); the New Jersey engineer didn't understand, and Gabriel adds that he isn't sure he does either. One reading (an interpretation, not Gabriel's): someone has to do the "tough" implementation work so the user gets a "tender," easy-to-use interface.
→
Why this exchange matters: this one design decision is the whole essay in miniature. Whichever side tolerates pushing complexity onto the caller — in exchange for a simpler implementation — is the side that ends up easier to build, easier to port, and easier to spread everywhere else.
The mechanism, step by step

How a worse design out-competes a better one

Gabriel's case that "worse" spreads on its own logic, not by secretly being better.

  1. Start from a genuinely "basically right" partial solution. Gabriel is careful here: this step has to actually be sound — a bad 50% solution doesn't win just by being simple.
  2. Because implementation simplicity came first, the software is small and easy to port. Few machine resources required, simple internal structure, straightforward to move to new hardware.
  3. Roughly half of all computers in existence, at any moment, are below the median in power. An easy-to-port design can run on all of them, where a design demanding top-tier hardware can't.
  4. That reach lets it spread into far more places than a heavier, more complete alternative ever reaches — the behavior Gabriel likens to a virus.
  5. Wide spread quietly resets what users expect. A good-enough tool people already have beats a better tool most people never encounter.
  6. Once something is everywhere, far more people have a stake in improving it. Collective effort accumulates disproportionately on the "worse" system until it closes most of the gap toward "almost the right thing."
＋
A side benefit Gabriel names separately: because a New Jersey-style system usually isn't powerful enough to build large, monolithic software in one shot, large systems built on it are forced to decompose into small, reusable components — a genuine engineering upside, distinct from the survival argument above.

M2 · The four criteria, clause by clause

Gabriel calls the worse-is-better list "only slightly different" from the MIT list, and it is: each pair of bullets shares its first sentence and differs in one or two words or one added sentence. Reading the difference word by word is the fastest way to pin the two philosophies down.

CriterionShared wordingMIT / Stanford addsNew Jersey adds
Simplicity"must be simple, both in implementation and interface"Interface simplicity matters more than implementation simplicity.Implementation simplicity matters more than interface simplicity, and simplicity is "the most important consideration in a design."
Correctness"must be correct in all observable aspects""Incorrectness is simply not allowed.""It is slightly better to be simple than correct."
Consistency"must not be [overly] inconsistent""must not be inconsistent"; a design may be slightly less simple and less complete to avoid inconsistency; "Consistency is as important as correctness.""must not be overly inconsistent"; consistency can be sacrificed for simplicity in some cases, but it is better to drop rare cases than to add either implementation complexity or inconsistency.
Completeness"must cover as many important situations as is practical"Reasonably expected cases "must be covered"; "Simplicity is not allowed to overly reduce completeness."Reasonably expected cases "should be covered"; completeness can be sacrificed "in favor of any other quality", and must be sacrificed whenever implementation simplicity is at risk; consistency can be sacrificed for completeness if simplicity is kept; "especially worthless is consistency of interface."

Simplicity: two quantities, not one. Both camps want both kinds of simplicity. The disagreement is only about which one wins when they conflict, and the conflict is common because making an interface simpler usually means moving work behind it. The PC loser-ing story (M4 to M7) is exactly such a conflict.

Correctness: same baseline, different tie-break. Both start from "correct in all observable aspects". The New Jersey bullet keeps that sentence and then allows simplicity to win a close call. Note the word observable: a design can be internally sloppy and still pass the shared clause, so long as nobody can tell.

Consistency: design uniformity, not data consistency. For an OS reader the word is a trap. Gabriel means that similar things behave similarly: no special cases, the same conventions across an interface and inside the implementation. It has nothing to do with memory consistency or crash consistency.

Completeness: "must" versus "should". The MIT bullet makes covering reasonably expected cases a requirement; the New Jersey bullet makes it a goal, and then names it as the first thing to give up.

🎙
From the lecture: what is the metric for each criterion? The slide asked the class to fill in a table (characteristic versus MIT/Stanford versus New Jersey) and then to propose a metric for each. Answers Rosenblum accepted: for simplicity, lines of code, number of arguments and number of API calls, plus portability; his example was the Bell Labs process-creation call taking zero arguments while the Windows call for creating a process takes a large number of arguments, many of them complex structures. For correctness, matching a formal specification and verifying it; he added that software licenses long disclaimed responsibility for doing anything useful, and asked what "correct" means for an AI model. For consistency, the shape of the API (same argument order, same checks everywhere), and he tied it to QA: inconsistency multiplies the cross product of feature combinations you must test. A student asked whether consistency meant consistency of state; his answer was that it means similar routines in a layered system behave the same way, without special cases, in both interface and implementation. No metric for completeness was discussed before the class moved on.

A metric hiding in the essay (inference). Gabriel's own percentages (50%, 80%, 90%, 100%) are completeness measures: the fraction of a wish list of functionality that a system covers. They are the only quantitative axis in the essay, and they measure the criterion that New Jersey sacrifices first.

M3 · The two rankings as decision rules

The bullets read like lists of virtues, but each one also says what wins in a conflict. Collecting those "X may give way to Y" clauses gives each school a precedence order. The Big Idea tab lists the qualities side by side; this section derives the order from the exact wording, and the result differs from a top-to-bottom reading of the lists in one important way.

MIT / Stanford. Correctness is absolute ("simply not allowed"), and consistency is "as important as correctness". A design may be "slightly less simple and less complete to avoid inconsistency", so consistency beats both simplicity and completeness. "Simplicity is not allowed to overly reduce completeness", so completeness beats simplicity, at least in large amounts. Within simplicity, interface beats implementation. Result: correctness ≈ consistency > completeness > interface simplicity > implementation simplicity. Simplicity is listed first in the essay but, by the essay's own clauses, it is the quality the right thing gives up most readily.

New Jersey. Simplicity is "the most important consideration", implementation before interface. Correctness yields slightly to simplicity. Consistency can be sacrificed for simplicity. Completeness can be sacrificed for "any other quality". Result: implementation simplicity > interface simplicity > correctness > {consistency, completeness}.

The tension inside the New Jersey list. The consistency bullet says it is better to drop rarely needed parts of the design (completeness loses) than to introduce inconsistency. The completeness bullet says consistency can be sacrificed to achieve completeness if simplicity is retained (consistency loses), and that interface consistency is "especially worthless". Read literally, each beats the other. This guide's reading of how they fit: implementation simplicity is the referee. If covering a case costs implementation complexity, drop the case. If covering it costs only an irregular interface, keep the case and accept the irregularity.

# The right thing, as a decision rule (this guide's reading of the MIT bullets)
choose design D such that
    correct(D)                                # hard constraint: "simply not allowed"
    consistent(D)                             # "as important as correctness"
    covers(D, every reasonably expected case) # "must be covered"
then minimize, in this order:
    complexity(interface(D))
    complexity(implementation(D))
# allowed:     slightly less simple or less complete, to stay consistent
# not allowed: simplicity that "overly" reduces completeness

# Worse is better, as a decision rule (this guide's reading of the New Jersey bullets)
D = simplest implementation that handles the common cases
for each further case c, most common first:
    if handling c makes the implementation complex:
        drop c                                # completeness "must be sacrificed"
    elif handling c uniformly is complex but an irregular interface is not:
        handle c with the irregular interface # "especially worthless is consistency of interface"
    else:
        handle c
if full correctness needs a complex implementation:
    accept a slightly weaker guarantee        # "slightly better to be simple than correct"

M4 · PC loser-ing: what goes wrong

The setting. A user program invokes a system routine to do "a lengthy operation that might have significant state, such as IO buffers." The invocation is one instruction (a trap). While the routine is running, an interrupt arrives, and the user program's state must be saved so that something else can run in user mode first.

The problem in one sentence. Because the call was a single instruction, "the PC of the user program does not adequately capture the state of the process." The saved user PC points either at the trap or just past it, and neither value says how far the kernel got: which buffers are allocated, how many bytes have moved, whether a device is busy on this process's behalf.

The invariant at stake (this guide's framing). A user process is easy to stop, inspect and resume when its whole state is user-visible: registers, memory, and a PC at an instruction boundary. A process stopped in the middle of a system routine breaks that, because part of its state lives only in the kernel. Gabriel's sentence "The system routine must either back out or press forward" names the only two ways to restore the invariant before user code runs:

  • Back out: undo what the routine has done and set the PC to the trap instruction, so the process looks as if it had not yet made the call.
  • Press forward: finish (or stop at a clean point), report the outcome in registers, and set the PC past the trap, so the process looks as if the call is over.

Either way, when user code runs again, the kernel holds nothing that the PC fails to describe.

The name. "It is called PC loser-ing because the PC is being coerced into loser mode, where loser is the affectionate name for user at MIT." Loser mode is user mode. The phrase describes the kernel forcing the program counter back to a user-mode instruction boundary.

🎙
From the lecture: why an ordinary interrupt is not the problem. A student asked why the kernel can't simply handle the interrupt and carry on. Rosenblum agreed that a plain hardware interrupt during a system call is harmless: the handler runs in the kernel and returns to where the kernel left off. The trouble is a signal (Unix, Linux, macOS), which makes the process jump to a user-level handler and later resume where it was. A signal that arrives while the process is in user code is clean. One that arrives mid-system call, after the kernel has allocated I/O buffers and other per-call state, is messy, and messier still because the handler may legally make further system calls. This pair-discussion question ("What is the PC loser-ing problem?", with ITS on a PDP-10 and "The right way?" on the slide) was the class's trial run of active learning.

M5 · The right thing: back out and rewind the PC (ITS)

Gabriel's statement of the MIT solution: "back out and restore the user program PC to the instruction that invoked the system routine so that resumption of the user program after the interrupt, for example, re-enters the system routine." Step by step:

  1. Trap. The program executes the call instruction at address X. The kernel starts the operation and builds per-call state.
  2. Interrupt. Something requires user-level code to run (or, in ITS's world, requires the process to be stopped and looked at) before the call is done.
  3. Back out. The kernel releases or undoes the per-call state so nothing about the call remains in the kernel, and sets the saved user PC to X, not X+1.
  4. User-level work runs. The interrupt handler runs on a process whose state is fully described by its registers, memory and PC.
  5. Resume. Execution continues at X, which re-executes the trap and re-enters the system routine. From the program's point of view, the call was made once and completed once.

What the program sees. Nothing. No error code, no retry loop. The call behaves as atomic: either not yet started or finished. That is a simple interface, bought with a more complex implementation: every slow system routine must know how to back out cleanly at any point where an interrupt can be taken.

What back-out requires (inference). Re-executing from X only works if restarting the routine gives the same result as continuing it. That holds when the work done so far can be undone, or when the routine's progress is recorded somewhere the restarted call will see it. Three cases where it is harder:

  • Irreversible side effects. If some bytes have already gone out to a terminal or a network, they cannot be un-sent. Restarting from scratch would send them twice. The routine must then press forward, or record the progress.
  • Consumed input. If a read has already taken data out of a pipe, backing out must put it back or hand it over; dropping it loses data.
  • Time-based arguments. A "sleep 10 seconds" call interrupted after 9 seconds and restarted from scratch sleeps 19 in total, unless the argument is adjusted.

Outside the course materials, accounts of ITS (for example Alan Bawden's note "PCLSRing: Keeping Process State Modular") describe the ITS answer to the progress problem: the system call's arguments, which live in user registers or memory, are updated to reflect partial progress before the PC is backed up, so the re-executed call continues where it stopped. That is how "back out" and "press forward" combine in practice. Treat this as background, not as something the essay says.

🎙
From the lecture: "squirrel away" the state. Rosenblum described the ITS approach as saving all the state built up for the system call so it can resume "right in the kernel where it left off" after the signal is processed, and noted that this is messy, that some languages (he named JavaScript) have support for that kind of suspended execution, and that C did not. Notice that this is a slightly different mechanism from the essay's wording. Gabriel says back out and re-enter the routine from the trap instruction; the lecture describes saving the in-kernel continuation and resuming it. Both give the program the same view (one call, no error). They differ in what the kernel must keep while user code runs: nothing (back out) versus a suspended call (save and resume).

M6 · The New Jersey answer: finish with an error, let the caller loop

The Unix answer, as the New Jersey guy put it: the Unix folks knew about the problem, but "the solution was for the system routine to always finish, but sometimes an error code would be returned that signaled that the system routine had failed to complete its action. A correct user program, then, had to check the error code to determine whether to simply try the system routine again."

  1. Trap. Same as ITS: call at X, kernel builds per-call state.
  2. Signal. The kernel notices a pending signal while the call is blocked.
  3. Unwind through the error path. The routine frees its buffers and other per-call state and returns failure. In Unix this is -1 with errno = EINTR ("Interrupted system call"). The saved PC is X+1, past the trap, exactly as for any completed call. This is "press forward" in the narrow sense: the call is over, it just failed.
  4. Handler runs. The signal handler runs on a clean process, then returns to X+1.
  5. Caller retries. The program tests the result. If it failed with EINTR, the program's own loop jumps back and executes the call again.

The point to see. Step 5 is PC loser-ing done in user space. The ITS kernel moved the PC back to X; under Unix, every caller's loop moves it back to X. The end state is the same, a fresh execution of the call, and the work has moved from one place (the kernel) to every call site.

Why the kernel side is cheap. The routine already needs an error path that releases its resources, for ordinary failures. EINTR reuses it. The kernel never has to keep a half-finished call alive while user code runs.

🎙
From the lecture: the slide's two code fragments. The first is labeled "Correct way on Unix", the second "Works almost all the time", followed by the two error messages the second one can print. Reproduced from the slide:
ssize_t n;
do {
    n = read(fd, buf, count);
} while (n == -1 && errno == EINTR);   // retry only when a signal interrupted the call

// "Works almost all the time..."
err = write(fd, buf, count);
if (err < 0) {
    perror("write");
    exit(1)
}
// possible output:  write: No space left on device
//                   write: Interrupted system call

His reading in class: write can fail for many reasons. "No space left on device" is a real error the user can fix by deleting files. "Interrupted system call" means a signal arrived mid-write and the program gave up when it should have retried, and the retry would probably have worked. He said Gabriel would call this bug an artifact of putting the complexity in the interface instead of handling it in the kernel. A student asked whether the Unix kernel still has to save state; his answer was that saving the state needed to resume after the trap instruction is routine, and that for a non-destructive call such as a read the kernel just frees the buffers and unwinds through the error return path it needs anyway.

Details the slide code leaves out (background, not from the essay or lecture). In classic Unix only "slow" calls can fail with EINTR: ones that may block indefinitely, such as reads from terminals, pipes and sockets, or wait. A read from a local disk file normally just completes. If a read or write has already moved some bytes when the signal arrives, it returns that byte count (a success) instead of EINTR, so a fully correct write loop must also advance the buffer pointer and retry the remainder. Both details are the New Jersey pattern again: the kernel reports what happened, and the caller completes the job.

M7 · Where the complexity lands, and what it costs

The MIT guy's objection, in the essay's words: "the implementation was simple but the interface to the functionality was complex." The anecdote is therefore a conflict between the two kinds of simplicity, the first criterion. It is not mainly about correctness: a Unix program that loops on EINTR is correct. What changed is who is responsible for correctness. The essay says so directly: "A correct user program, then, had to check the error code."

The ledger (this guide's reading).

M8 · The dialogue as an argument

The story is short, but each line is one move in the debate. In order:

  1. The setup. Two famous people, one from MIT who knew ITS ("the MIT AI Lab operating system") and one "from Berkeley (but working on Unix)". The MIT person had been reading the Unix sources and found no code that handled PC loser-ing.
  2. The Unix answer. The Unix folks were aware of the problem; the routine always finishes and sometimes returns an error code; a correct program checks and retries.
  3. MIT objection 1: it is "not the right thing".
  4. New Jersey defense: Unix's philosophy is simplicity, the right thing "was too complex", and besides, "programmers could easily insert this extra test and loop." Note that this argument is about cost per programmer and ignores how many programmers there are (compare the ledger in M7).
  5. MIT objection 2: the implementation is simple but the interface is complex.
  6. New Jersey reply: that is the intended tradeoff: implementation simplicity was more important than interface simplicity. The two men agree on the facts and disagree only on the ranking. That is exactly what Gabriel wanted the story to show.
  7. The coda. "The MIT guy then muttered that sometimes it takes a tough man to make a tender chicken, but the New Jersey guy didn't understand (I'm not sure I do either)." The line is the MIT guy's, not Gabriel's; Gabriel only adds that he is not sure he understands it either.
🎙
From the lecture: the tender chicken. The slide asked "Where did this come from? What does it mean?" and linked a video. The source is a broadcast TV commercial for Perdue chicken. In the clip played in class, Frank Perdue says his graders reject 30% of the chickens that government graders accept as grade A, and closes with "it takes a tough man to make a tender chicken." Rosenblum's reading was tentative: Perdue claims to hold his product to a higher standard than the regulators, which may be the MIT guy's point about software standards, but he called it a weird line that does not land well today. One way to read it (this guide's reading, not the lecture's): someone has to be "tough" (the implementer who does the hard back-out work) so that the user gets something "tender" (a call that just works).

M9 · Later Unix: the rewind comes back into the kernel (background)

This section goes beyond the essay and the lecture. It is here because it shows what happens when one assumption of the New Jersey choice changes: once Unix was everywhere and the EINTR bugs were familiar, Unix systems added the ITS-style rewind for the common case. BSD made interrupted calls restart automatically, and POSIX later standardized the choice per signal handler as the SA_RESTART flag.

Linux on x86 makes the mechanism visible. When a slow call is interrupted it returns an internal code such as -ERESTARTSYS instead of -EINTR. On the way back to user mode, the signal-delivery code checks the handler's flags. With SA_RESTART it restores the system-call number into the return register and subtracts 2 (the length of the syscall instruction) from the saved user instruction pointer. Without it, the code becomes -EINTR and the PC stays put. When the handler finishes and its saved registers are restored, the CPU executes the syscall instruction again.

That is Gabriel's sentence nearly word for word: "restore the user program PC to the instruction that invoked the system routine so that resumption of the user program after the interrupt ... re-enters the system routine." It is cheap to add here only because the kernel has already backed out (through the same error path) and only restarts calls with no partial progress. A call that moved some bytes returns the count instead, and Linux handles timed sleeps with a separate restart path that recomputes the remaining time, which is the "record the progress" fix from M5.

M10 · C and Unix as the worked example

After the anecdote, Gabriel turns to "Now I want to argue that worse-is-better is better," and starts with C. The chain of claims:

  1. C was designed for writing Unix, using the New Jersey approach.
  2. So a decent C compiler is easy to write. C "requires the programmer to write text that is easy for the compiler to interpret." The work of mapping a program onto the machine is split so that the programmer does more and the compiler less. "Some have called C a fancy assembly language."
  3. So both are small and portable. Early Unix and C compilers "had simple structures, are easy to port, require few machine resources to run".
  4. And they are only partly complete. They "provide about 50%-80% of what you want from an operating system and programming language."

The same trade as PC loser-ing (inference). C's design puts work on the programmer so the compiler stays simple, just as EINTR puts work on the caller so the kernel stays simple. In both cases the interface (the language, the system call) is harder to use so that the implementation (the compiler, the kernel) is easier to write and therefore easier to port. That last step is what powers the next section.

🎙
From the lecture: simplicity measured by arguments. Rosenblum's example of implementation and interface simplicity at once was process creation: the Bell Labs call that creates a process takes zero arguments, while the Windows call takes a large number of arguments, many of them complex structures. He called it "a really striking design trade off" and said the course would see it again later. (For reference, outside the lecture: the Unix call is fork(), and the Windows call is CreateProcess, with ten parameters including two structures.)

M11 · The virus argument, step by step, and the conditions it needs

This is the core causal claim. Reconstructed as premises and conclusion, using the essay's own sentences:

  1. P1, the hardware distribution. "Half the computers that exist at any point are worse than median (smaller or slower)."
  2. P2, reach. "Unix and C work fine on them," because implementation simplicity has the highest priority, which makes them easy to port to such machines. The right thing, in the big complex system scenario, "only runs satisfactorily on the most sophisticated hardware."
  3. P3, adequacy. "if the 50% functionality Unix and C support is satisfactory, they will start to appear everywhere." The condition is restated later: "the initial virus has to be basically good. If so, the viral spread is assured as long as it is portable."
  4. Conclusion 1. "Unix and C are the ultimate computer viruses."
  5. P4, conditioning. Once spread, there is pressure to improve it, "possibly by increasing its functionality closer to 90%, but users have already been conditioned to accept worse than the right thing."
  6. P5, effort follows users. "even though Lisp compilers in 1987 were about as good as C compilers, there are many more compiler experts who want to make C compilers better than want to make Lisp compilers better."
  7. Conclusion 2, the three stages. Worse-is-better software "first will gain acceptance, second will condition its users to expect less, and third will be improved to a point that is almost the right thing."

A closer look at P1 (this guide's reading). That half of all machines are "worse than median" is true of any distribution by definition, so it adds no information on its own. What does the work is where each design's minimum hardware requirement sits within that distribution. If the New Jersey system runs on nearly all machines and the right thing runs well only at the top, the New Jersey system's reachable market is much larger. The median sentence is a vivid way to say "most machines are not the best machines."

The prediction. "The good news is that in 1995 we will have a good operating system and programming language; the bad news is that they will be Unix and C++." This is Conclusion 2 applied: the viral pair, improved to "almost the right thing".

What breaks the argument if a premise fails (inference).

  • P3 fails (the 50% is not basically right). Then nothing spreads. Gabriel uses exactly this to block the "C for AI" conclusion (M14).
  • P2 stops mattering. If hardware is uniform, or so fast that even a heavy system runs well on below-median machines, portability and small footprint stop being a sorting factor and the reach advantage shrinks. The lecture's "machine speed" question is aimed here.
  • P5 fails. If users cannot or do not improve the system (closed source, no community), the third stage does not happen, and the system stays at 50%. The lecture's "community" question is aimed here.
  • The missing 50% is the part users need. In a domain where an occasional wrong answer is unacceptable, "satisfactory" is a high bar and a 50% system does not meet it. (This guide's reading; the essay does not discuss such domains.)
  • What the essay does not show. It gives no evidence that Unix and C spread because of portability rather than for other reasons. The argument is a plausible mechanism, not a measured one.
🎙
From the lecture: community and machine speed. The "Other issues" slide asked which camp has the better community; the student answer accepted was New Jersey, because Lisp machines from companies like Symbolics never had the crowd of contributors that the widely shared Unix system had. On machine speed, Rosenblum reminded the class that Moore's Law was in full force when the essay was written (machines doubling in speed about every 18 months), so the question is what machine a piece of software assumes. His example: the first VMware product needed a high-end PC for a decent experience because PCs varied so much, while lightweight software can run on low-end machines without forcing users to upgrade.

M12 · The side benefits: conditioned programmers and a tradition of integration

Gabriel lists two more effects, separate from the spread argument.

Programmers trade comfort for efficiency. "the programmer is conditioned to sacrifice some safety, convenience, and hassle to get good performance and modest resource use." The consequence he draws: New Jersey programs "work well both in small machines and large ones," and are portable "because it is written on top of a virus." The PC loser-ing loop is a small example of this conditioning: Unix programmers accept the hassle of the extra test as normal.

Components instead of monoliths. "Because a New Jersey language and system are not really powerful enough to build complex monolithic software, large systems must be designed to reuse components. Therefore, a tradition of integration springs up." The mechanism is a limitation turned into a habit: since the base cannot carry a large integrated system, people build small pieces and connect them. On the other side, Gabriel says the right thing is often monolithic "for no reason other than that the right thing is often designed monolithically. That is, this characteristic is a happenstance." In other words, monolithic design is a habit of the MIT culture, not something its four criteria require.

🎙
From the lecture: monolithic or component-based? The student answer Rosenblum accepted: the MIT/Stanford approach leads to monolithic designs because it wants to handle every case and be maximally correct, while the New Jersey approach leads to building components and adding to them incrementally. His comment: it is easier to make a system consistent when it is all one piece, but you eventually run into complexity limits and can't build further, and people widely agree that building from modules lets a system scale bigger.

M13 · How the right thing loses: the two scenarios

Gabriel then asks how the right thing "stack[s] up" and gives two ways it goes wrong. They fail on different axes, which is easy to blur.

How each maps onto the criteria (inference). The big complex system honors completeness and pays in implementation simplicity, time and hardware, which is the MIT ranking from M3 taken to its end. The jewel honors interface simplicity and consistency and pays in implementation difficulty: the interface is small, so all the cleverness needed for speed falls on the implementer. In both cases the cost lands on the implementation, the one quality the MIT ranking puts last, and in both cases that is what stops the system from spreading.

M14 · The lesson, and the wrong lesson

The lesson. "it is often undesirable to go for the right thing first. It is better to get half of the right thing available so that it spreads like a virus. Once people are hooked on it, take the time to improve it to 90% of the right thing." Note the word "often": it is advice about sequencing, not a claim that the right thing is wrong.

The wrong lesson. Taking the parable literally and concluding "that C is the right vehicle for AI software." His reason: "The 50% solution has to be basically right, and in this case it isn't." That is P3 from M11 failing. Spread requires adequacy for the job, and Gabriel judged C not adequate for AI programming.

The only conclusion he allows. "the Lisp community needs to seriously rethink its position on Lisp design." The essay is advice to his own side, not a manifesto for Unix.

🎙
From the lecture: what is the language of AI? The slide put it as "Language of AI: (not C)?". Rosenblum's answer for today: machine-learning programs are written in Python, but they spend almost all their time in libraries written in lower-level languages such as C++, because nobody wants to do the numerical work in Python itself. The code people write is Python; the code that runs is mostly something else.

M15 · The lecture's frame: metrics, business, and Ousterhout

Why this essay on day one. The 9/22 session was the course introduction, and the essay was the trial run of the discussion format: ten minutes to read in class, then discussion. The slides set up two ideas that the essay illustrates. First, an operating system is a layer on the hardware that manages, abstracts and multiplexes it for applications, and "advanced OS" work means new abstractions, new hardware, better management, and software engineering. Rosenblum noted that operating systems used to be among the largest pieces of software and so were the first to hit the problems of scaling software to millions of lines, which is why many classic OS papers are really about how to structure systems. Second, he said OS abstractions across macOS, Linux, Windows and iOS have largely converged. (This guide's reading: the essay is one account of why the converged abstractions look like Unix.)

From business schools. The slide quoted the definition of a minimum viable product: "a version of a product with just enough features to be usable by early customers who can then provide feedback for future", and the saying "Perfection is the enemy of good enough". Rosenblum heard "MVP" constantly from marketing people at VMware and said business schools are in effect teaching the New Jersey approach. His caution: a product that doesn't work for anybody will not succeed, and the skill is knowing when something less than ideal is good enough to ship.

A 2018 book: John Ousterhout, A Philosophy of Software Design. Rosenblum introduced Ousterhout as a systems professor here and his own advisor at Berkeley. Three chapters were on the slides, each with the question "MIT/Stanford or New Jersey?":

  • Chapter 3, Strategic vs. Tactical Programming. The slide's figure plots total progress against time. Tactical programming (example: early Facebook, "move fast and break things") makes progress faster at first and then flattens; strategic programming (examples: Google and VMware) starts slower and pulls ahead. A student mapped strategic to MIT (take time to get it right) and tactical to New Jersey (ship, then tune, at the risk of technical debt). Rosenblum's comment: Google and Facebook are both very successful, so tactical was not a disaster for Facebook.
  • Chapter 8, Pull Complexity Downwards. "It is more important for a module to have a simple interface than a simple implementation." That sentence is nearly the MIT simplicity bullet word for word, and it is the opposite of the Unix answer to PC loser-ing.
  • Chapter 10, Define Errors Out Of Existence. "Exceptions add complexity (uncommon code paths, etc.)" EINTR is a textbook case of such an exception, and restarting the call in the kernel (M5, M9) is what defining it out of existence would look like.

The transcript shows the discussion reached chapter 3 and then Rosenblum said he would skip ahead to the next-class announcement, so chapters 8 and 10 were on the slides but not discussed.

A caution on the chapter 3 mapping (this guide's reading). New Jersey is not the same as tactical. Tactical programming, as the figure shows it, accumulates complexity; the New Jersey approach makes implementation simplicity its first priority. Unix's EINTR choice kept the kernel small on purpose and pushed a small, documented cost to callers. That is a deliberate design ranking, not a shortcut taken under deadline.

M16 · Connections to other CS240 papers

Only connections where the same mechanism or the same trade appears.

Mesa monitors: the same retry loop. Hoare's monitors hand the monitor directly to the woken process, so the waited-for condition is guaranteed true on wakeup: a simple contract with a harder implementation. Mesa makes NOTIFY a hint, the notifier keeps running, and the waiter must re-check in a WHILE loop. Structurally this is the EINTR loop: the system offers a weaker guarantee and every caller re-tests and retries. Mesa also shows the payoff the New Jersey side would point to. Because a waiter re-checks anyway, spurious wakeups are harmless, which is why the Mesa paper says "it is easy to add" timeouts, aborts and BROADCAST (Mesa §4.1). One more detail from that section lines up with the lecture's Ousterhout slides: Mesa's original timeout raised an exception, and it was changed "because many users simply wanted to retry on a timeout, and objected to the cost and coding complexity of handling the exception." That is chapter 10's "define errors out of existence" argument, applied to the very kind of error that EINTR exposes.

Threads, events, and cooperative task management: who saves the continuation. The lecture's version of the ITS approach (save the in-kernel state of the call and resume it later) requires keeping a suspended computation alive. Event-driven programs face the same problem in user space: a handler that must wait has to save its own state and resume in another handler, which is the "stack ripping" that Adya et al. describe. Unix's EINTR dodges the problem entirely by discarding the in-progress call and letting the caller start it again. Rosenblum's aside that JavaScript has language support for this kind of resumption points at the same idea.

VMware (Adams and Agesen) and ESX: an interface you are not allowed to change. Rosenblum described a virtual machine monitor as a kernel whose interface "looked just like the hardware itself". When the interface is fixed by compatibility, the New Jersey move (simplify the implementation by changing the interface) is unavailable, so all the complexity has to go below the line: binary translation and shadow page tables in the hardware-versus-software virtualization paper, and memory techniques in ESX that work without guest changes, such as content-based page sharing. Ballooning is the exception: it needs a driver in the guest, a small step toward cooperation.

Superpages: transparent versus requested. Navarro et al. make superpages transparent to applications and pay with a complex kernel (reservations, population maps, promotion and demotion). In their related work, IRIX and HP-UX pick the superpage size partly from a user-specified per-segment page-size hint, and the paper's main criticism is that this "is not transparent": it takes experimentation per application to find the right size. That is the PC loser-ing trade again, with "who rewinds the PC" replaced by "who picks the page size".

Eraser: a simpler property instead of the exact one. Eraser checks a locking discipline (each shared variable is protected by some consistent lock) rather than tracking the happens-before order that would identify actual races. It accepts false alarms and needs annotations, and in return gets a checker that does not depend on the particular interleaving that happened to run. That is a deliberate trade of precision for a simpler, more robust mechanism.

Five everyday pictures

What "worse is better" feels like

Every analogy is useful for one part of the intuition and wrong for another.

A weed vs. an orchid

Hardy and everywhere beats exquisite and rare

A weed spreads through any patch of ground it can reach, outcompeting through sheer coverage. An orchid is exquisite but needs exact conditions to survive at all — and most ground never gets to see one.

Where it breaks: a real weed doesn't improve over time the way widely-adopted software does once a large community starts working on it.
A food truck vs. a Michelin restaurant

Showing up everywhere beats being the best in one place

The truck can open on any street corner immediately and get discovered by sheer volume. The restaurant may cook better food but exists in exactly one location, reachable by comparatively few.

Where it breaks: a food truck's recipe isn't rebuilt into five-star cuisine by its customers' collective effort the way widely-used software gets iteratively patched and improved.
A cheap universal charger vs. a proprietary one

"Fits everything" beats "faster, but only for one thing"

The cheap universal charger is good enough and works with almost everything, so it becomes the default one everyone already has lying around. The proprietary charger might charge faster, but only fits one exact device.

Where it breaks: a physical charger doesn't get iteratively improved by its installed base the way software gets patched and rewritten by the people now depending on it.
A chain restaurant vs. a chef's tasting menu

Replicable and consistent beats hand-crafted and singular

A standardized, simple-to-operate recipe can be replicated in any strip mall. A chef's tasting menu can't scale past one kitchen and one chef's attention.

Where it breaks: franchising doesn't make the food taste better over time, whereas Gabriel's mechanism claims the "worse" system actually keeps improving as it spreads.
An early digital camera vs. large-format film

Worse per-shot quality, but distributed to everyone

An early digital camera produced clearly worse images than large-format film — but it was so much easier to use and distribute that it displaced film almost entirely, and then kept getting better.

Where it breaks: film's quality advantage never came bundled with the crippling implementation cost the-right-thing software in this essay carries — the comparison is closer, but not identical.
How "the right thing" loses on its own

Two ways doing it right backfires

Gabriel doesn't just say the-right-thing loses to worse-is-better — he gives two separate ways it defeats itself.

1 · The big complex system scenario

Design for near-complete coverage from the start, and the last stretch of functionality costs a disproportionate share of the total effort — so the system takes a long time to finish, ends up large and complex, needs heavyweight tools to work with, and only runs comfortably on the most capable hardware available.

Gabriel's example: Common Lisp (and, he adds, classic artificial intelligence software).

2 · The diamond-like jewel scenario

Keep the design small and elegant at every step instead, and getting that elegance exactly right can itself take a very long time — and making the result run fast turns out to be either extremely difficult or out of reach for most people trying to implement it.

Gabriel's example: Scheme.

Q·Does "worse is better" mean quality doesn't matter?▾

Q·Why call widely-adopted software a "virus" instead of just "popular"?▾

Q·Did Gabriel keep holding this view for the rest of his career?▾

Q·How does this connect to ideas like "MVP" or "perfect is the enemy of good"?▾

The essay is commonly referenced alongside the Unix philosophy of small composable tools and later "ship early, iterate" thinking.
Q·Is this essay making a claim about hardware, or about software design culture?▾
Software design culture. Gabriel's comparison is between two ways of prioritizing simplicity, correctness, consistency, and completeness when designing software, illustrated through Lisp/Scheme versus Unix/C. Hardware still matters to the argument as the reason the New Jersey style spreads: simple implementations port easily to the below-median half of machines, while the big right-thing system "only runs satisfactorily on the most sophisticated hardware."
Beyond Lisp vs. Unix

Where this argument shows up in advanced OS design today

Gabriel's tension between the-right-thing and worse-is-better didn't stay in 1989 — it's a recurring fault line in real operating-systems and computer-architecture work.

1 · Blocking syscalls vs. io_uring — the PC loser-ing problem, reincarnated

Traditional blocking system calls are exactly Unix's original worse-is-better answer: the kernel implementation stays simple, and the caller absorbs the complexity of checking return codes, handling EINTR (the PC loser-ing case itself) and EAGAIN, and building retry/poll loops (later formalized as select/poll/epoll). Linux's io_uring is a modern, decades-later move toward the-right-thing: a shared submission/completion ring lets the kernel manage complex asynchronous state internally so the interface simplifies dramatically for the caller, at the cost of a genuinely intricate kernel-side implementation. That io_uring took until 2019 to arrive, long after blocking calls had already spread everywhere and become "good enough," is itself an instance of Gabriel's mechanism.

2 · Microkernels vs. monolithic kernels — a textbook rerun

Microkernel research (Mach, later L4-family systems) argued for the-right-thing: strong isolation, a small trusted core, and a clean interface, even at the cost of implementation complexity and performance overhead from message-passing. Monolithic, pragmatic designs — Unix and then Linux — took the New Jersey path: a simpler implementation that ran adequately on modest hardware, spread widely, and then absorbed decades of incremental improvement effort from a vastly larger developer base. The outcome tracks Gabriel's virus argument almost exactly, including the fact that Linux later re-absorbed some microkernel-style ideas (loadable modules, user-space drivers) once it had the installed base to afford the complexity.

3 · CXL memory tiering and page-placement heuristics

In tiered-memory systems (DRAM plus CXL-attached memory), theoretically optimal page-placement algorithms exist but are often expensive to compute and hard to generalize across heterogeneous, real-world topologies. In practice, simple heuristics — access-bit sampling, coarse hot/cold classification, periodic migration — tend to get deployed and iterated on instead, precisely because they're the "basically right, easy to port" 50% solution that can actually ship across varied hardware, rather than the complete, complex answer that only works in a narrow, well-characterized setting.

4 · POSIX as institutionalized worse-is-better

POSIX compatibility is itself a lowest-common-denominator interface that spread so widely it became nearly impossible to displace — even though the OS research community has repeatedly designed more elegant or more complete alternatives (Plan 9's uniform namespace model, various exokernel-style proposals). Those alternatives largely stayed research artifacts; POSIX's "good enough, everywhere" position won the installed base, exactly the pattern Gabriel describes for Unix and C.

5 · eBPF vs. clean-slate extensible-OS research

eBPF extends the kernel by working within its existing simple boundary — a verifier constrains what untrusted code can do, rather than redesigning the kernel's architecture to safely admit extensions. Academic proposals for extensible operating systems (SPIN, Exokernel-style designs) took the-right-thing route: rethink the kernel's structure itself to make extension safe and general by construction. eBPF's narrower, worse-is-better approach shipped, spread, and is now the default way to extend Linux in production — while the more ambitious research designs remain influential but largely unshipped.

🎓
The general pattern to watch for: in advanced OS design, the "right thing" proposal is usually the one that's easier to defend in a paper, and the "worse is better" proposal is usually the one that's easier to actually deploy across real, heterogeneous hardware. Neither fact tells you which one is correct for a given problem — but noticing which side of this line a design choice falls on is a genuinely useful lens for reading any systems paper, not just this essay.
