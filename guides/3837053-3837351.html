Why a CPU stuck copying memory doesn't need to run full speed

Past a point, more clock speed doesn't move more bytes — it just burns watts waiting on memory. This is the plain-English story of CopyCat, a runtime that notices and claws the energy back.

📄 "CopyCat: Harvesting the Frequency Tax of Bulk Memory Copy" · Li & Zhang 🎓 DVFS/CXL/tiering background assumed — primers fill small gaps ⏱ ~25 min read
🌡️

If more frequency buys no more throughput, it's only buying more heat — so measure throughput directly, then turn the clock down.
Probe, don't guess from utilization.

Quick glossary + eight primers

Your tiering/CXL background covers the "why memcpy is everywhere" part. These fill the DVFS/power-management plumbing the paper leans on.

1The memory wall, in one sentence▾

Cores keep getting faster; the path to memory hasn't kept pace. Once enough threads are copying, they collectively saturate the memory controller — adding threads (or clock) stops producing more delivered bytes/sec, it only adds stalled cycles.

Now you're ready: CopyCat's whole premise is this saturation point, where clock speed and throughput decouple.
2DVFS: what you buy and what it costs▾

Lower voltage/frequency cuts dynamic power roughly with the square of voltage. The catch: if a task is compute-bound, running slower just makes it take proportionally longer, often costing more total energy (static/leakage power keeps flowing the whole time).

Now you're ready: DVFS is a net win only when the task doesn't actually need the faster clock — exactly the memory-stalled case.
3schedutil and HWP — two hands on the same dial▾

The OS governor (schedutil) watches per-core utilization and requests a frequency. Modern Intel chips then let HWP firmware make the final call from telemetry the OS never sees. A core that's 100% busy spinning on memory stalls looks indistinguishable from one doing useful compute — both read "100% busy."

Now you're ready: this blind spot is why both layers keep copying cores pinned at max frequency long after it stops helping.
4Why stall counters don't fix it either▾

You might think "just read per-core memory-stall-cycle counters." The paper shows these mislabel some copy kernels — the real problem is a level mismatch: safety to down-clock is a system-wide bandwidth fact (is the memory controller saturated?), but the counter is per-core and can't see the other cores.

Now you're ready: CopyCat sidesteps this by measuring delivered throughput directly instead of inferring from any local proxy.
5RAPL and "package energy"▾

RAPL exposes a running energy counter for the CPU package (cores + uncore). The paper measures CPU-package energy specifically via this counter. Reading it before and after a phase gives joules consumed — the paper's currency for every energy number you'll see.

Now you're ready: "saves 24% package energy" means the RAPL delta over that phase, not an estimate.
6Spatial vs temporal separation▾

A workload alternating memory and compute phases every few microseconds can't be chased by a governor whose frequency changes take tens of milliseconds — temporal tracking is just too slow. The alternative: separate the two phases onto different cores (one fast, one slow) running at the same time — spatial separation needs no fast switching at all.

Now you're ready: this is exactly why CopyCat's async path offloads copies to dedicated cores rather than toggling one core's frequency in time.
7Futexes and the "don't syscall on the fast path" rule▾

A futex lets a thread sleep in the kernel only when it actually needs to wait — checking a user-space flag first avoids the syscall entirely when the answer is already known. Spin briefly first (cheap), then futex-sleep only if still waiting (expensive but rare).

Now you're ready: CopyCat's copycat_sync() uses exactly this three-stage pattern so synchronization is nearly free in the common case.
8Tiered memory's role here (your home turf)▾

CXL-attached memory, remote NUMA, and PMEM commonly offer less bandwidth than local DRAM — though this isn't universal across all CXL configurations — so page migration (TPP-/Pond-style transparent tiering) and DAX-style persistent-memory writes often saturate the memory controller at fewer concurrent threads than pure local-DRAM copying. That shifts CopyCat's "safe to down-clock" threshold lower, not just its applicability.

Now you're ready: this paper treats memcpy as the shared primitive underneath tiering, not a separate concern — see FAQ for how it relates to TPP/Pond-style migration specifically.
The whole story in one place

CopyCat in a nutshell

The waste, the direct fix, and how the pieces fit.

Bulk memcpy is already one of the most expensive standard-library calls in data centers, and tiered memory (CXL, PMEM, remote NUMA) is adding more of it: every page promotion/demotion is a 4 KB memcpy, and lower-bandwidth tiers saturate the memory controller at fewer concurrent threads. Once that saturation hits, a higher core frequency buys next to no extra throughput — down-clocking at this point costs the paper's measured 1–2% or less, while the extra frequency mostly produces more stalled cycles at peak power. On the authors' Skylake-SP server, saturation arrives at 8–10 threads, and the default schedutil+HWP baseline keeps every copying core at max frequency anyway, wasting up to 24% of package energy.

CopyCat is a user-space runtime that intercepts every memcpy call and measures delivered throughput directly — no utilization heuristic, no unreliable stall counter. A router then picks one of two paths: sync down-clocks the copying core in place when there's no compute to free it for; async offloads the copy to a dedicated low-frequency worker core, freeing the issuing core to keep computing at full speed. A joint controller tunes worker-pool size and frequency together using a step-search rule (try a step, keep it if throughput survives, revert if not).

The payoff: up to 24% package energy saved at under 1–2% throughput cost in microbenchmarks; on a blob-cache prototype, async offload in the serve phase delivers +15% throughput at roughly neutral energy, and down-clocking the dedicated workers saves a further ~15% package energy on top of that (net ≈ −15%) — see Worked Example ② for the breakdown.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • Past bandwidth saturation, frequency and throughput decouple — but schedutil+HWP can't see that and stay at max clock.
  • Per-core utilization and stall counters are the wrong signal (level mismatch); CopyCat probes delivered throughput instead.
  • Phases alternate in microseconds; OS frequency changes take ~44 ms — too slow to chase in time, so CopyCat separates phases in space (dedicated low-freq workers) instead.
  • Router picks sync (in-place down-clock) when fully memory-bound, async (offload) when compute and copy alternate.
  • Enqueueing a copy costs ~6 ns, and copycat_sync() returns immediately if the copy's already done — the expensive spin/futex path only triggers when a copy is genuinely still in flight.
  • Result: up to 24% package energy saved, <1–2% throughput cost, validated on a real blob-cache prototype.
Five everyday pictures

How to think about CopyCat

Read in any order. Each ends with where the picture stops being true.

The core problem · Saturation

Revving the engine in a traffic jam

Floor the gas in bumper-to-bumper traffic and you don't arrive faster — you just burn more fuel idling between inches gained. Once enough cars (threads) fill the road (memory bandwidth), speed and progress decouple. CopyCat is the driver who notices the jam and eases off the pedal.

Where it breaks: an idling engine still burns fuel at any RPM; a stalled CPU core genuinely draws less power at a lower clock — the win is real, not just a wasted-effort metaphor.
Async path · Spatial separation

Hiring a slow delivery runner

You keep working at your desk while a (deliberately unhurried) courier fetches the package — you never have to stop and wait at courier speed. CopyCat's async mode is this: copy work moves to a dedicated low-frequency core while the issuing core stays at full speed computing. No one waits for anyone unless the package is needed right now.

Where it breaks: if there's barely any "desk work" to do while waiting, you end up idle anyway, blocked in copycat_sync() — the overlap benefit shrinks when compute is short relative to copy size.
Why temporal tracking fails · C2

Steering a container ship by the second

A cargo ship takes minutes to change heading; you can't use it to dodge a jet ski that swerves every second. Frequency changes take ~44 ms; a copy-then-checksum loop can flip phases every few microseconds — thousands of times faster than the ship can turn. Chasing it in time is hopeless.

Where it breaks: a ship has no alternative but to turn slowly; CopyCat's actual fix isn't a faster rudder, it's assigning a second, differently-tuned vessel (spatial separation) instead of trying to out-turn the problem.
Why utilization lies · C1

Asking "are you busy?" to someone stuck in traffic

A driver stuck on the highway, not moving, will still say "I'm busy — I'm driving!" Utilization asks the same unhelpful question of a stalled core: it's 100% "busy" waiting on memory, indistinguishable from 100% busy computing. CopyCat instead asks "how far did you actually get?" — delivered throughput, not self-reported busyness.

Where it breaks: the driver at least knows they're stuck; a per-core stall counter sees its own stalls but not whether the shared road (memory controller) is actually full — that's a system-wide fact no single core can observe alone.
The router · Workload classification

A triage nurse, not a one-size bandage

Triage doesn't treat every patient identically — it sorts by what kind of care each case needs, then routes. CopyCat's router watches how continuously threads submit copies and sorts into sustained (down-clock in place), fragmented (offload to free compute), or thread-limited (offload for race-to-idle) — then applies the matching mechanism automatically.

Where it breaks: triage categories are usually stable per-patient; CopyCat's workload category can shift mid-run, so the classification has to keep re-checking, not decide once and forget.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Section by section, following the paper's three challenges (C1–C3).

1 · The waste, measured (§2.2)

At 1 thread, halving clock from 2.5→1.2 GHz costs 20% throughput and increases energy (static power keeps flowing over a longer runtime). At 8–10 threads, memory bandwidth is saturated: the same down-clock costs under 2% throughput but saves 20–24% package energy. The saturation point shifts to just 4 threads when copies target remote NUMA memory or are capped by MBA — exactly the tiered-memory case.

2 · Why existing control misses it (§2.3, C1)

schedutil reads utilization, which is always 100% on a copying core whether it's stalled or computing — no signal there. Per-core stall counters fare no better: each of three test kernels mislabels at least one, costing up to 27% throughput if trusted blindly. The root cause is a level mismatch — frequency is set per core, but "is it safe to down-clock" is a system-wide bandwidth fact that no single core's counters can see. CopyCat's fix: skip the proxy, briefly run at two frequencies and compare the actual delivered throughput.

3 · The step-search controller (§3.1)

# runs on a background thread, off the data path
loop every tick:
    if not stable or not saturated: continue   # safeguards
    try_freq = current_freq − 1 step
    measure throughput at try_freq
    if throughput ≥ baseline − tolerance:
        current_freq = try_freq            # keep going down
    else:
        revert to previous step; hold
    periodically re-probe upward             # catch workload shifts

In async mode this same idea runs on two variables jointly: a grow phase (frequency fixed, add workers until throughput plateaus) alternates with a drop phase (worker count fixed, step frequency down). Dropping frequency shifts the per-worker bandwidth curve, so the next grow phase may add one more worker at the new, lower clock.

4 · The three-stage sync protocol (§3.2, C3)

copycat_sync():
    if copy already done: return              # zero-cost fast path
    spin 256× PAUSE                           # catches µs-scale finishes
    if still pending:
        set waiter flag (seq-cst)
        futex_sleep()                          # only now pay the syscall

Enqueueing a copy into the lock-free internal queue costs ~6 ns; the worker only calls futex_wake if it observes the waiter flag, so the common case never touches the kernel.

5 · Routing by workload shape (§3.3, C2)

CategoryPatternCopyCat's response
Sustainedall threads copying continuously, bandwidth-saturatedsync: down-clock in place
Fragmentedthreads alternate copy and computeasync: offload, free issuing cores
Thread-limitedcopy traffic too light to saturate bandwidthasync at full freq → race-to-idle
💡
Size cutoffs matter: copies under 4 KiB run inline (offload overhead would dominate); single copies over 16 MiB also run in place (CopyCat never splits one request across workers, so offloading buys no parallelism there). Both cutoffs, plus the 30 ms control tick, are empirically tuned for this platform.

6 · Scaling the pool (§3.3)

Workers are pre-created once (one pinned thread per core) but mostly parked in a 0.5 ms sleep loop, costing no cycles. An autoscaler wakes/parks workers every 30 ms, stopping once aggregate throughput plateaus — a soft cap that stabilizes at the fewest cores needed to saturate bandwidth, which the joint controller then down-clocks.

⚠
The tension: down-clocking workers slows the copy itself. If the caller's compute phase is short relative to the copy, it ends up blocking longer in copycat_sync(), eating into the overlap benefit — which is exactly why the drop phase stops once throughput falls below its tolerance budget.
Follow the numbers

Worked examples

Four traces using the paper's real figures.

① Async offload's two stacked savings (§4.2, Fig. 6b)

4 app threads alternate 1 MiB memcpy with compute; 6 copy-workers spin up. Net result vs. the sync baseline: −22% package energy, +26% throughput. Where does −22% come from?

  1. Layer 1 — overlap alone (workers still at full 2.5 GHz): offloading lets compute and copy run concurrently instead of serially → +26% throughput, −6% energy (same work, less total wall time, less static-power overhead).
  2. Layer 2 — down-clock the now-isolated workers: CopyCat observes the workers are purely memory-bound and steps them 2.5→1.2 GHz, adding −17% energy at no further throughput cost.
  3. Compound the two multiplicative savings: (1 − 0.06) × (1 − 0.17) = 0.94 × 0.83 ≈ 0.78 → ≈22% total energy saved, matching the reported figure.
🍯
Insight: neither layer alone gets you there — spatial separation (layer 1) is what reveals the opportunity for layer 2, since only isolated, purely-memory-bound cores are safe to down-clock without risking the application's compute.

② Blob-cache serve phase (§4.3, Fig. 6c)

5 handlers process GETs (1 MiB copy + ~200 µs processing each); the pool stabilizes at 5 copy-workers. Offload alone: +15% throughput at neutral energy. Workers down-clocked to 1.2 GHz: a further −15% energy at no throughput cost. What's the net?

  1. Offload layer: +15% throughput, energy unchanged (0% — the extra work is "free" via overlap, so energy per unit time stays flat even as useful output rises).
  2. Down-clock layer: energy ×(1 − 0.15) = 0.85 of the offload baseline, throughput unaffected.
  3. Net vs. no-CopyCat baseline: package energy ≈ −15%, while the +15% throughput gain from overlap is fully retained — the two layers don't trade off against each other here because they act on different axes (speed vs. clock).
💡
Contrast with example ①: here the overlap layer changed throughput, not energy, so the two savings simply stack additively rather than compounding — a reminder that "layer the two benefits" isn't always multiplicative.

③ Compact phase — sync down-clock (§4.3, Fig. 6d)

10 threads rewrite the store in place — purely memory-bound, no compute to overlap with. CopyCat reports −24% package energy at −1% throughput. Why does this match the microbenchmark ceiling exactly?

  1. No compute phase exists here — the router classifies this as sustained, so there's nothing for async offload to free up. Sync in-place down-clocking is the only applicable path.
  2. 10 threads already saturate the remote-NUMA memory controller (§2.2's saturation point), so this sits exactly at the regime where down-clocking is cheapest: near-zero throughput cost, maximal energy savings.
  3. Result: −24% energy, −1% throughput — essentially reproducing the microbenchmark's 8–10-thread sweet spot (§2.2, Fig. 1) inside a real application.
🍯
Insight: the two blob-cache phases are a clean A/B demonstrating the router's job: pick offload when there's compute to protect, pick in-place down-clock when there isn't — same mechanism, different context, both correct.

④ Why temporal tracking is hopeless (§2.3, C2)

A copy-then-checksum loop flips between memory and compute phases every few microseconds. An OS frequency change takes ~44 ms. How much slower is the control loop than the thing it's trying to track?

  1. Phase duration: "a few microseconds" ≈ 1–10 µs.
  2. Frequency-change latency: ~44 ms = 44,000 µs.
  3. Ratio: 44,000 µs ÷ ~5 µs ≈ 8,800× — "three orders of magnitude too slow," as the paper puts it.
  4. Implication: by the time a frequency change lands, the workload has already flipped phase thousands of times over — any attempt to chase it in time is moot, which is exactly the motivation for separating phases in space (dedicated cores) instead.
💡
This arithmetic is the entire justification for the async path's existence — not a performance nicety, but the only physically feasible option at this timescale.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try before opening each answer — every one names the misconception it checks.

Problem 1 · easy
A single thread is running memcpy, far from saturating memory bandwidth. Should CopyCat down-clock it?
Show answer
No. At 1 thread the copy is latency-bound, not bandwidth-saturated — lowering frequency directly slows the copy (measured: ~20% throughput loss) and, because the task now runs longer on the same static-power floor, can increase total energy. CopyCat's controller tries one step down, sees throughput drop, and reverts immediately.Misconception: that down-clocking always saves energy. It only pays off once the core is stalled waiting on memory, not actively limited by its own clock.
Problem 2 · medium
Why can't the OS governor just read "percent of cycles stalled on memory" per core and down-clock whenever that's high?
Show answer
Because stall fraction is a per-core signal while safety-to-down-clock is a system-wide bandwidth fact. The paper's example: 1 thread and 10 threads can show the same ~90% stall-cycle fraction, yet only the 10-thread case actually saturates the shared memory controller. A core can't tell, from its own stalls, whether its neighbors are also hammering the same memory channel.Misconception: that a better per-core counter would fix this. The paper shows multiple counter designs each mislabel at least one kernel — the issue is the level of measurement, not counter accuracy.
Problem 3 · medium
A workload alternates 2 µs of memcpy with 2 µs of compute, repeating continuously. Can the OS governor's DVFS track this by raising frequency during compute and lowering it during copy?
Show answer
No — a frequency change takes ~44 ms, roughly 10,000× longer than a single phase (~2–4 µs). By the time any change takes effect, the workload has already cycled through thousands of phases. Temporal tracking is physically infeasible at this timescale; CopyCat instead separates the phases onto different cores running simultaneously (spatial separation), which needs no fast switching at all.Misconception: that a "fast enough" governor could eventually solve this. The mismatch is ~4 orders of magnitude — no incremental speedup to the governor closes that gap; a different mechanism (spatial, not temporal) is required.
Problem 4 · hard
In the async path, why might down-clocking the copy-workers ever reduce the throughput benefit of offloading in the first place?
Show answer
Offloading only helps as much as the caller's compute phase can overlap with the copy. If the workers run slower, the copy itself takes longer; if the caller's compute phase is short relative to that copy time, the caller finishes its compute early and then blocks in copycat_sync() waiting for the slow copy to land — eating into the overlap window. The drop phase's throughput-tolerance check exists precisely to stop lowering frequency once this starts happening.Misconception: that offload and down-clocking are independent, freely stackable wins. They interact — over-aggressive down-clocking on the workers can erode the very overlap benefit offloading was meant to capture.
Problem 5 · medium
A single 20 MiB memcpy arrives. Why does CopyCat run it in place rather than offloading it to the worker pool?
Show answer
CopyCat never splits one request across multiple workers — a single large copy is handled by exactly one worker (or inline), so routing it to the pool gains no parallelism; it would just add offload overhead for identical total work. The paper's empirically-chosen cutoff for "large enough that offload helps nothing" is 16 MiB.Misconception: that bigger copies always benefit more from offload. Offload's benefit comes from overlapping with the issuing thread's compute, or from parallelizing across many separate copies — not from splitting one copy internally, which CopyCat doesn't do.
Problem 6 · easy
Copies smaller than 4 KiB run inline even though async offload exists. Why?
Show answer
Below a certain size, the fixed cost of enqueueing, dequeueing, and (if needed) synchronizing with a worker exceeds the cost of just doing the tiny copy locally. The 4 KiB cutoff is the empirical point where offload overhead stops paying for itself on this platform.Misconception: that offload is "free" because enqueue only costs ~6 ns. The 6 ns is just the submission cost — the worker side still has real dequeue/dispatch/possible wake-up overhead that dominates for small copies.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Tap any to expand.

Q1Is CopyCat a kernel change or a hardware feature?▾
Neither — it's a user-space runtime. It intercepts memcpy calls via its own API (copycat_memcpy/copycat_sync), manages a pool of worker threads, and adjusts CPU frequency through existing OS/firmware interfaces. No kernel modification, no new silicon.
Q2How is this different from Intel DSA or (MC)²'s memory-controller offload?▾
DSA and (MC)² move the copy itself off the CPU core onto dedicated hardware. CopyCat still runs the copy on a CPU core — its contribution is deciding which core, at what frequency, based on measured throughput. The two approaches are complementary, not competing: CopyCat could, in principle, also apply its frequency logic around hardware-offloaded copies.
Q3Why not just always run copy-heavy cores at the lowest frequency?▾
Because at low thread counts (unsaturated), a lower clock directly slows the copy and can increase total energy (longer runtime on the same static-power floor). The "always low" policy is exactly what CopyCat avoids — it probes and only down-clocks once throughput shows the core truly is memory-stalled, not frequency-limited.
Q4Does this apply equally to CXL-attached memory, PMEM, and remote NUMA?▾
Largely yes: CXL-attached memory, remote NUMA, and PMEM commonly offer less bandwidth than local DRAM (though not universally, depending on configuration), so the saturation point tends to arrive at fewer concurrent threads — the paper shows 4 threads suffice over remote NUMA (~30 GB/s) versus 8–10 for local DRAM. Tiering systems like TPP or Pond, both cited by the paper, drive sustained page-migration traffic that's a natural high-volume source of this saturating memcpy pattern. (HeMem is a related tiering system but isn't discussed in this paper — mentioned here only as external context.)
Q5What exactly triggers the choice between sync and async mode?▾
The router watches how copy requests are submitted: continuous copying with no interleaved compute → sustained → sync in-place down-clock. Copy and compute alternating → fragmented → async offload. Light, infrequent copy traffic that never saturates bandwidth → thread-limited → async offload anyway, for race-to-idle (finishing both sooner lets cores idle earlier).
Q6Could CopyCat make things worse for a co-running workload?▾
Potentially — dedicated low-frequency copy-workers still consume cores, memory bandwidth, and share package-level power/thermal budget with anything else running. The paper's evaluation dedicates these workers to CopyCat and doesn't study contention with unrelated co-tenants; that interaction is left open.
Q7Why measure throughput instead of just trusting RAPL energy readings directly as the control signal?▾
The goal is a throughput budget (don't lose more than ~1–2%), with energy as the thing being optimized given that constraint. Throughput is also available at much finer time granularity without extra hardware support, making it the natural signal for the fast step-search loop; RAPL energy is used for post-hoc measurement/evaluation (§4), not as the live control variable.
Q8What's the actual per-call overhead of using CopyCat instead of libc memcpy?▾
Enqueueing into the lock-free internal queue costs ~6 ns. When copycat_sync() doesn't need to wait (copy already done), it returns immediately at effectively zero cost. The expensive path (spin then futex-sleep) only triggers when a copy is genuinely still in flight.
Q9Is a 1% throughput loss from frequency reduction acceptable for latency-sensitive services?▾
The paper frames it as a tunable budget, not a fixed cost — the tolerance in the step-search controller determines how much throughput loss is acceptable, and the controller reverts a step whenever that budget is exceeded. For strict-SLA services, tightening the tolerance trades away some energy savings for a smaller throughput hit; the paper's reported numbers (≤1–2%) reflect its chosen tolerance, not a hard limit.

Source notes — paper facts vs. framing

From the paper (verifiable against the text above): saturation at 8–10 threads on the Skylake-SP testbed; up to 24% package energy saved at <2% throughput cost; saturation shifting to 4 threads under remote-NUMA or MBA-throttled conditions (13–15% savings there); ~44 ms OS frequency-change latency; stall-counter mislabeling up to 27% throughput cost; ~6 ns enqueue cost; 4 KiB / 16 MiB routing cutoffs and 30 ms control tick as empirical, platform-specific choices; blob-cache results (serve: +15% throughput at neutral energy from offload, further −15% from down-clocking; compact: −24% energy/−1% throughput); async microbenchmark up to −22% energy with +26% throughput; three design challenges C1–C3 and the sustained/fragmented/thread-limited taxonomy.

This guide's framing (not in the paper verbatim): all analogies (traffic jam, delivery runner, container ship, triage nurse, "are you busy?"); the interactive thread-count explorer's specific illustrative curve shape (matched qualitatively to Fig. 1, not digitized from it); the multiplicative-vs-additive distinction drawn between Worked Examples ① and ②; explicit ties to TPP/Pond-style tiering as a motivating traffic source, which the paper references only generally under "heterogeneous memory" — HeMem specifically is not cited by the paper and is flagged as external context wherever it appears.
