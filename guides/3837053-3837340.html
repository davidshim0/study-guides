HotStorage 2026 · A Field Guide

Deciding by evidence, not mailing-list advocacy

Should a Rust rewrite replace a C storage driver? This is the plain-English story of KOxI, a three-gate pipeline that turns that judgment call into a number-backed, reproducible profile — and what happens when you point it at the one pair small enough to check it on.

📄 "To Migrate or Not to Migrate: KOxI" · Lima & Sasaki, HotStorage '26 🎓 Kernel/mm background assumed — stats & fuzzing primers included ⏱ ~25 min read
🚦

ALL THREE gates — safety, fuzzing, performance — must pass before KOxI calls a driver ready for direct replacement. One failing gate means "not ready, as-is" — not a ban on continued migration work, partial upstreaming, or re-running the gates once a gap closes.
Disagreement across gates is the output, not noise to average away.

Quick glossary

The acronyms, up front

You know the kernel; you may not know this paper's measurement vocabulary. Here's every acronym before you hit it in context.

R4LRust-for-Linux — the effort making Rust a first-class kernel implementation language.
CWECommon Weakness Enumeration — labels a bug's mechanism (e.g. NULL deref), not its severity.
ACSACAnnual Computer Security Applications Conference — source of the CWE→Rust-impact taxonomy KOxI reuses (Li et al. 2024).
fioFlexible I/O Tester — the standard block-layer load generator (Axboe).
IOPSI/O Operations Per Second — the throughput metric fio reports per cell.
MWUMann–Whitney U test — nonparametric test for whether one sample tends to exceed another.
A12Vargha–Delaney A12 — effect size: P(random sample from group A > group B). 0.5 = coin flip.
CIConfidence interval — here, a 95% bootstrap interval on the median IOPS delta.
LoCLines of Code — used both as a driver-size proxy and an unsafe-surface proxy.
UAFUse-After-Free — a CWE-416 class memory-safety bug Rust's borrow checker statically excludes.
Before you begin

Nine ideas the paper assumes you know

Collapsed by default — open any that look unfamiliar. The kernel/mm side is likely familiar; the statistical-evidence machinery is where these primers earn their keep.

1Why storage drivers are an R4L test case▾

Block drivers sit on hot I/O paths, carry decades of C bug history, and are hard to test exhaustively — exactly the profile R4L wants to prove itself on. Rust support landed in mainline at v6.1 (2022) as a declared experiment and was formally concluded in December 2025: it's now permanent.

Now you're ready: the open question has shifted from "does Rust belong in the kernel" to "which C drivers should migrate, when" — today a mailing-list judgment call KOxI tries to replace with numbers.
2CWE — labeling a bug's mechanism▾

A CWE ID describes how a bug happens (CWE-476 = NULL dereference, CWE-190 = integer overflow), independent of how bad it was. Li et al. (ACSAC'24) group CWEs into three super-classes by whether Rust's type system structurally prevents them.

Now you're ready: KOxI's safety gate is just "what fraction of this driver's CWE-classified fixes fall in the auto-eliminated super-class?"
3Not all bugs are memory-safety bugs▾

Safe Rust's type system (borrow checker plus bounds/lifetime checking) statically excludes out-of-bounds access, use-after-free, double-free, and data races. KOxI's CWE taxonomy separately classifies NULL deref (CWE-476) and integer overflow (CWE-190) as auto-eliminated too — caught by other Rust guarantees like non-nullable references and checked arithmetic, not the borrow checker specifically. None of this automatically fixes input-validation bugs, race conditions enabled by unsafe, resource leaks, or logic errors — those need Rust-idiomatic discipline, not just the language.

Now you're ready: the gap between "Rust eliminates memory-unsafety" (true, aggregate) and "Rust eliminates this driver's bug history" (an empirical question) is the whole safety gate.
4Coverage-guided fuzzing and crash attribution▾

syzkaller mutates syscall sequences guided by kernel coverage feedback and watches for crashes. Detection is automatic; attribution is not — a campaign also shakes bugs out of the VM, the harness, and unrelated subsystems, not just the driver under test.

Now you're ready: KOxI's fuzzing gate only counts crashes it can tie to the driver by name/path — everything else is noise that must not count against (or for) the Rust rewrite.
5fio basics: block size, pattern, queue depth▾

fio sweeps axes that stress different parts of the I/O path: block size (4 KiB small ops vs 1 MiB large ones), pattern (random vs sequential — fio can do either), and queue depth (how many in-flight I/Os before waiting). Small, random, shallow queues stress per-operation submit/complete overhead most.

Now you're ready: KOxI's 18-cell matrix (3 block sizes × randread/randwrite × 3 queue depths) sticks to random I/O throughout — it's designed to isolate per-operation submit/complete cost, not to compare against sequential access.
6p-values and the Mann–Whitney U test▾

Mann–Whitney asks "does one group's values tend to rank higher than the other's?" without assuming a normal distribution — appropriate for noisy crash counts or IOPS samples. A small p (conventionally <0.05) means the observed difference is unlikely to arise from chance alone, under the null hypothesis of no difference.

Now you're ready: a p-value alone says nothing about how big the difference is — which is exactly why KOxI never uses it alone.
7Effect size: Vargha–Delaney A12▾

A12 is the probability that a randomly drawn sample from group A exceeds a randomly drawn sample from group B. 0.5 = indistinguishable; the paper treats ≥0.71 as a "large" effect. Pairing a significance test with an effect size stops a tiny, practically meaningless difference from triggering a decision just because N was large enough to detect it.

Now you're ready: KOxI's fuzzing gate declares a regression only when p<0.05 and A12≥0.71 — both statistically real and practically large.
8Bootstrap confidence intervals▾

Instead of assuming a formula for the uncertainty in a median, resample your own data (with replacement) thousands of times and look at the spread of medians you get. A 95% CI is the middle 95% of that resampled spread — an empirical, assumption-light way to say "how sure are we."

Now you're ready: KOxI's performance gate computes a bootstrap 95% CI (B=10,000) per fio cell and checks whether its lower bound clears a −5% floor.
9Multiple comparisons and the Holm correction▾

Run 18 independent significance tests at p<0.05 and, by chance alone, you expect roughly one spurious "significant" result even if nothing is really different. The Holm correction tightens the threshold across the family of tests to control that inflated false-positive rate.

Now you're ready: the performance gate's 18-cell sweep is exactly this situation — Holm correction is what lets "no cell survives correction" mean something.
The whole story in one place

KOxI in a nutshell

The decision problem, the three gates, and what they found on the one pair small enough to check.

A block-layer maintainer facing an R4L replacement series must answer four separate questions — does the driver's bug history contain the kinds of bugs Rust retires; does the rewrite shrink or merely relocate unsafe surface; does it survive adversarial workloads; what does it cost on the hot path — and today answers each from scattered, unmatched, unthresholded sources. KOxI is a pipeline that composes those four lenses into a Phase 1 screen plus three explicit, independently reported Phase 2 gates: safety, fuzzing, and performance.

Each gate has a pre-declared threshold — not chosen after seeing the result — and the three are never collapsed into one score. The verdict is worst-of: any failing gate means "not ready for direct replacement," because a safety win doesn't buy back a performance regression and a fuzzing pass doesn't buy back a missing safety win. Disagreement across gates is treated as the decision-relevant output, not noise to average away.

The authors exercise KOxI on null_blk (C) versus rnull (Hindborg's upstream Rust rewrite) — to their knowledge the only public, upstream, behavior-matched C/Rust storage-driver pair, and therefore the smallest faithful end-to-end test of the pipeline. Phase 1 flags null_blk as a maximally strong candidate (3.0/3.0). The three gates then disagree: safety fails (Rust's type system retires only 2 of 11 CWE-classified fixes, 18.2%, against a 34.2% bar), fuzzing passes (no statistically distinguishable, practically large target-attributable regression: p=0.37, A12=0.45), and performance fails hard (all 18 matched fio cells regress, median −14.4%, worst −25.5%).

🧭 Key takeaways

  • KOxI turns an R4L migration call into three independently-reported, pre-thresholded gates instead of one advocacy score.
  • Phase 1 screens candidates on four 0–3 lenses (historical risk, static surface, dynamic robustness, tractability); it ranks, it doesn't veto.
  • The safety gate only credits the ACSAC-taxonomy auto-eliminated CWE super-class — validation/race/lifecycle bugs don't count, even though Rust can still help with discipline.
  • The fuzzing gate is "do no harm": it fails only on a statistically significant and large-effect regression, not on the mere absence of a win.
  • The performance gate uses bootstrap CIs and Holm-corrected significance across an 18-cell fio matrix with no memory backing — isolating per-operation abstraction cost.
  • Verdict aggregation is worst-of, by design — the three failure modes are not commensurable.
  • On the smallest, most favorable-looking pair, the three gates still disagree: safety FAIL, fuzzing PASS, performance FAIL — exactly the signal the authors argue R4L upstreaming needs before it scales.
🔗
Adjacent to tiering work (this guide's framing, not the paper's): the submit/complete abstraction-cost question KOxI isolates for this one synthetic block-driver pair is a question worth asking about any kernel-resident data-path module touching CXL or NVM — would a Rust rewrite of a tiering policy module (page hotness tracking, migration triggers) pay a similar per-operation overhead tax on its hot path? The paper explicitly warns against generalizing its delta magnitudes beyond null_blk/rnull, so whether a tiering module would see anything near KOxI's −14.4% median is an open question this paper doesn't answer, not a transferable number.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about KOxI

Each stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with where the picture stops being true.

Verdict aggregation · Worst-of

A three-judge panel, unanimous required

One judge voting "no" overturns a case even if the other two vote "yes" — the panel doesn't average votes into a score of 0.67. KOxI's three gates work the same way: a safety win and a fuzzing pass don't outvote a performance regression. The verdict is "not ready" the moment any single gate fails, because the gates measure incommensurable things.

Where it misleads: a real panel can deliberate and change a vote; KOxI's gates are mechanically independent — no gate's result adjusts another's threshold.
The safety gate · CWE super-classes

A deadbolt, a guard dog, and an alarm you still must arm

A deadbolt makes a whole class of break-in impossible by construction — that's auto-eliminated (NULL deref, overflow, UAF: Rust's type system just removes them). A guard dog deters but needs training and upkeep — that's needs Rust discipline (leaks, unchecked returns: the language helps, but doesn't guarantee). An alarm you must remember to arm catches nothing on its own — that's language-unaffected (validation, races, logic bugs: present in Rust just as in C).

Where it breaks: the safety gate only credits the deadbolt category. A driver whose history is mostly guard-dog and alarm bugs can still improve meaningfully under Rust discipline — the gate just won't say so.
The fuzzing gate · Attribution

A car alarm in a crowded parking lot

An alarm goes off — but is it your car, or someone else's three rows over? If you credit every alarm to your car, you'll think it's broken when it isn't. KOxI's crash stream mixes target-attributable crashes with VM, harness, and unrelated-subsystem noise; only regex-matched, driver-named/path-named crashes count toward the fuzzing gate's statistics.

Where it breaks: regex attribution is a blunt instrument — a crash whose stack trace doesn't mention the driver by name can be misattributed in either direction. The paper's audited-override path (manual_cwe / validated_crashes.csv) exists precisely to fix this by hand when it matters.
Pre-declared thresholds · Policy, not post-hoc

Setting the high-jump bar before you see the jumper

If you set the bar height after watching someone jump, "clearing the bar" means nothing. KOxI's 34.2% safety threshold, −5% performance floor, and p<0.05/A12≥0.71 fuzzing criterion are declared in a single editable knob file, derived from an external reference (the ACSAC aggregate rate) or an engineering judgment — before the gate is run, not tuned afterward to produce a favorable verdict.

Where it breaks: a pre-declared bar is still a chosen bar. The paper is explicit that a different cutoff family could move a candidate between bands — the auditability is about process, not an objective, driver-independent truth.
Holm correction · Multiple comparisons

Eighteen lottery tickets, one "lucky" winner expected

Buy 18 scratch tickets with a 5% win rate each, and you expect roughly one winner from pure chance — that doesn't mean the tickets are rigged. Run 18 independent significance tests at p<0.05, and by the same logic, expect about one spuriously "significant" cell even if nothing real changed. Holm correction raises the bar per cell so the family-wide false-positive rate stays controlled.

Where it breaks: the lottery analogy assumes independence; adjacent fio cells (same block size, different queue depth) are correlated, so the "expected one spurious win" intuition is approximate, not exact — Holm is conservative precisely to cover that.
Do-no-harm · The fuzzing gate's framing

A clean bill of health is not a lifetime guarantee

A check-up that finds nothing wrong means "no detected illness today," not "will never get sick." The fuzzing gate's PASS means ten 24-hour campaigns found no statistically significant, large-effect increase in target-attributable crashes for rnull versus null_blk — a real, useful, but bounded result.

Where it breaks: a check-up catches what its tests are built to catch. With only N=10 campaigns per driver, the gate is powered to detect large regressions; a small, real regression can sit below the gate's detection floor and still pass.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Gate by gate, with the pipeline's real thresholds and the one worked robustness check the paper performs on its own classification.

1 · Phase 1 — screening, not vetoing

Four lenses are each scored 0–3: historical risk (share of commits carrying a safety-signal regex match: fix/leak/race/overflow/UAF/null-deref), static surface (LoC and AST-level implicit unsafe operations — dereferences, alloc/free, memcpy/memset, casts), dynamic robustness (does cached fuzzing evidence already show target-attributable crashes), and tractability (how many prior fuzzing caches/campaigns exist). The mean of the four determines a band: ≥2.5 strong, ≥1.5 moderate, else weak — with the strong cutoff resolving to a lens total of at least 10 of 12.

🌱
Two lenses are deliberately evidence-starved by default: an unfuzzed driver scores low on dynamic robustness and tractability — that reads as "collect evidence first," not "do not migrate." Phase 1 produces a shortlist, not a verdict.

2 · Safety gate — how much history does the language retire?

Of 69 safety-signal commits, 11 could be assigned a specific CWE and mapped to the ACSAC taxonomy's three super-classes. The gate's elimination rate is the share of classified commits in the auto-eliminated class — 2 of 11 = 18.2% — compared against a 34.2% threshold (the ACSAC-aggregate expectation for Rust's automatic contribution across kernel-driver CVEs). The unit of analysis is the individual fix commit, matching the per-CVE granularity the threshold was derived from.

Robustness check: how fragile is 18.2%?

Two of the 11 classifications are borderline single-classifier calls (a CWE-125 logical-bound read, a CWE-843 union-member confusion). The paper reclassifies them by hand to bound the gate's sensitivity — try it:

auto-eliminated2 / 11
elimination rate18.2%
threshold34.2%
FAIL — below threshold.

Moving one borderline call reaches 27.3% — still failing. Only moving both flips the gate, to 36.4%. The gate's conclusion is not a knife-edge artifact of one disputed call.

3 · Fuzzing gate — matched campaigns, attributed crashes, a large-effect bar

N=10 independent 24-hour syzkaller campaigns per driver, each spawning 4 QEMU/KVM guests (4 vCPUs, 4 GiB) with virtio-blk for the device under test. Raw crash totals are dominated by harness noise, so only the target-attributable stream — regex-matched against driver-name/path tokens — feeds the statistics; the rest splits into infrastructure noise and unknown.

4 · Performance gate — matched fio, bootstrap CI, Holm correction

fio 3.41 sweeps an 18-cell matrix (block size × pattern × queue depth, Table 3) on a 512 MiB target, 30 reps of 30s per cell, both drivers without memory backing for parity. Per cell: median IOPS delta, a bootstrap 95% CI (B=10,000), a Mann–Whitney p, and A12, with p Holm-corrected across the 18-cell family. The gate passes only if every cell's CI lower bound clears −5% and no cell survives Holm-corrected significance as a regression.

⚠
What "no memory backing" measures: upstream rnull doesn't yet implement memory-backed mode, so both drivers run submit/complete-only. The reported deltas are upper bounds on per-operation abstraction cost, not device-throughput deltas — the RFC's own 432-configuration benchmark on a memory-backed device found near-parity, consistent with a data path amortizing this overhead.

5 · Verdict aggregation — worst-of, by design

# KOxI aggregation (conceptually)
if safety.fails() or fuzzing.fails() or performance.fails():
    verdict = "NOT READY for direct replacement"
else:
    verdict = "evidence-supported"
# no weighted sum: a safety win cannot buy back a performance regression
💡
Why not a weighted score? The three failure modes aren't on the same scale and aren't substitutable — the authors explicitly reject averaging because it would hide exactly the disagreement the pipeline exists to surface.
Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Every number here is from the paper's null_blk/rnull run (§4–§5), carried through to the gate verdict.

① Phase 1 screen → candidate band

  1. Historical risk: 69 of 106 observed commits carry a safety-signal (fix/leak/race/overflow/UAF/null-deref) regex match: 69/106 = 65%, clearing the ≥40% cutoff for score 3.
  2. Static surface: 119 functions, 2,491 LoC, 1,071 implicit unsafe operations — clears >2000 LoC for score 3.
  3. Dynamic robustness: cached fuzzing data already shows target-attributable crashes → score 3.
  4. Tractability: both caches present, 10 prior campaigns (≥10 cutoff) → score 3.
  5. Mean = (3+3+3+3)/4 = 3.0, clearing the ≥2.5 "strong" cutoff (and the ≥10/12 total).
🧭
A maximally strong screen is the right place to probe disagreement — a weak-screen driver wouldn't tell you anything about whether the gates can disagree on a case R4L should actually care about.

② Safety gate arithmetic

  1. 69 safety-signal commits observed; only 11 could be assigned a specific CWE (the other 58 are excluded from the gate's denominator — a conservative choice, not counted either way).
  2. Mapping the 11 (Table 4): CWE-190 (sector_t truncation) and CWE-476 (NULL deref while configuring) land in auto-eliminated; CWE-401×2 and CWE-252 land in needs discipline; CWE-20×2, 125, 362, 772, 843 land in language-unaffected.
  3. Elimination rate = 2/11 = 18.2%.
  4. Compare to threshold: 18.2% < 34.2% → gate FAILS.
  5. Sensitivity: reclassifying the CWE-125 call alone → 3/11 = 27.3% (still fails); reclassifying both borderline calls → 4/11 = 36.4% (only this flips the gate to pass).

③ Reading the fuzzing statistics

  1. Mann–Whitney p = 0.3681 (reported as 0.37 in the abstract): far above 0.05 — no evidence the two campaign distributions differ.
  2. Vargha–Delaney A12 = 0.45: a random rnull campaign yields more target-attributable crashes than a random null_blk campaign only 45% of the time — slightly favoring rnull, and far below the 0.71 "large effect" bar even if it had favored null_blk.
  3. Decision rule: regression requires p<0.05 and A12≥0.71. Neither condition holds → gate PASSES under the do-no-harm framing.
  4. Context: target-attributable counts were zero in 19 of 20 campaigns; the one exception was a single crash in one C (null_blk) campaign.

Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
null_blk scores 3.0/3.0 on Phase 1 — the strongest possible screen. Why is that the most interesting case for KOxI to run, rather than the least?
Show answer
Because a pipeline whose three gates only disagree on marginal, weakly-flagged drivers wouldn't be very useful — the paper wants to show the gates can produce a discriminating, non-trivial profile even on the candidate that looks like an obvious "yes" on every Phase 1 lens. A three-way split on the best-looking case is stronger evidence the gates carry independent information than a split on a borderline case would be.Misconception: that a high screening score should predict a clean pass through the gates. Phase 1 measures "worth investigating," not "will pass" — they're different questions by construction.
Problem 2 · medium
69 commits carry a safety-signal regex match, but only 11 are used in the safety gate's elimination-rate calculation. What happened to the other 58, and why not count them as "language-unaffected"?
Show answer
The other 58 couldn't be confidently assigned a specific CWE, so they're excluded from the gate's denominator entirely — neither counted as eliminated nor as unaffected. This is a conservative choice: folding them into "unaffected" would understate the elimination rate by diluting it with unclassified noise, and folding them into "eliminated" would overstate it without evidence. Excluding them keeps the 11-commit denominator matched to what was actually classified.Misconception: that "safety-signal" and "CWE-classified" are the same set. The regex is a cheap screen for the historical-risk lens; the gate's denominator is the narrower, hand-classified subset.
Problem 3 · medium
If the paper's single classifier had assigned CWE-843 (not CWE-125) to the auto-eliminated class instead of language-unaffected, but left CWE-125 alone, what would the elimination rate become, and would the gate flip?
Show answer
Moving one borderline commit into auto-eliminated gives 3/11 = 27.3% — still below the 34.2% threshold, so the gate still fails. The paper states only moving both borderline calls (CWE-125 and CWE-843) together reaches 4/11 = 36.4%, which flips it.Misconception: that single-classifier uncertainty is enough to overturn the gate's conclusion. The paper explicitly bounds this — one disputed call isn't sufficient to flip the verdict, which is exactly why they report the bound.
Problem 4 · hard
Why does KOxI use a worst-of verdict instead of, say, a weighted average of the three gates' scores?
Show answer
Because the three gates measure incommensurable failure modes: a safety win doesn't make a 25% IOPS regression acceptable, and a fuzzing pass doesn't substitute for a missing safety improvement. A weighted average would let a strong score on one axis mask a severe problem on another — exactly the disagreement KOxI is built to preserve and surface to a reviewer, rather than compress into one unauditable number.Misconception: that more information (a combined score) is always better than less (three separate verdicts). Here, preserving disagreement is the useful signal; averaging destroys it.
Problem 5 · medium
p=0.3681 and A12=0.45 on the fuzzing comparison. A reviewer claims this "proves rnull is just as safe as null_blk under fuzzing." What's wrong with that claim?
Show answer
The gate is explicitly framed as do-no-harm: failing to detect a significant, large-effect regression is not the same as proving equivalence or absence of risk. With N=10 campaigns per driver, the test is powered to catch large differences; smaller real regressions could exist undetected. "No evidence of harm" and "proof of safety" are different claims, and the paper only makes the first.Misconception: that a non-significant result is evidence of no effect. It's an absence of evidence for an effect of the size the test could detect — a narrower, weaker claim.
Problem 6 · medium
Why does the performance gate apply Holm correction across the 18 fio cells instead of just checking each cell's raw p<0.05?
Show answer
With 18 independent comparisons each at a 5% false-positive rate, roughly one cell is expected to look "significant" by chance alone even if there were no true difference anywhere. Holm correction tightens the per-cell threshold across the family so the overall (family-wise) false-positive rate stays near 5%, meaning a cell that survives correction is much stronger evidence of a real effect than an uncorrected p<0.05 would be.Misconception: that testing more cells makes a result more convincing by default. Without correction, testing more cells actually makes spurious "significant" findings more likely, not less.
Problem 7 · hard
Both drivers run with no memory backing in the performance gate. Why does the paper argue the reported −14.4% median delta is an upper bound on real-world impact rather than the final word?
Show answer
With no memory backing, every I/O completes without touching real data, so the only cost being measured is the submit/complete abstraction path itself — the one place a language/abstraction-layer difference can show up with nothing else to dilute it. Once a real data path is added (the pending feature-parity series includes memory backing), that fixed per-operation overhead gets amortized over actual data movement, which is why the RFC's own 432-configuration memory-backed benchmark found near-parity. The −14.4% is specific to the worst-case, data-path-free configuration.Misconception: that this delta directly predicts production device throughput loss. It measures abstraction-layer overhead in isolation — a deliberately pessimistic, upper-bound comparand, not a finished verdict on real hardware.
Problem 8 · easy
A maintainer says "KOxI shows Rust-for-Linux storage drivers are generally slower than C ones." What does the paper's own limits section (§6.1) say about generalizing from this result?
Show answer
The paper is explicit that this is a single driver pair: the delta magnitudes and the all-3s Phase 1 screen are specific to null_blk/rnull, and the authors say this result does not support generalizing that R4L storage drivers regress or that their safety gains are small. What transfers is the pipeline's structure — the gates, the attribution method, the audit trail — not the specific numbers.Misconception: that one matched case study licenses a general claim about a whole migration effort. The contribution is explicitly the reusable pipeline, not a verdict on R4L storage drivers as a class.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Ten questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What does "KOxI" stand for?▾
The paper doesn't expand it as an acronym — it's simply the pipeline's name. The substance is the three-gate structure (safety, fuzzing, performance) plus Phase 1 screening, not the name.
Q2Why only one driver pair — doesn't that limit the results?▾
null_blk/rnull is, to the authors' knowledge, the only public, upstream, behavior-matched C/Rust storage driver pair available — which makes it the smallest faithful end-to-end test of the pipeline, not a representative sample. The paper is explicit that the numbers are specific to this pair; what's meant to generalize is the pipeline's structure.
Q3Isn't the historical-risk lens (Phase 1) the same thing as the safety gate?▾
They share a data source (the safety-signal regex over commit messages) but ask different questions. The historical-risk lens counts all safety-signal commits, including races and leaks the safety gate won't credit — it asks "is there pain here?" The safety gate asks "how much of that pain does Rust's type system structurally retire?" Keeping them separate is what lets them disagree.
Q4Why declare thresholds (34.2%, −5%, 0.71) before running the gates?▾
A threshold chosen after seeing the result can always be tuned to produce the answer you wanted. Declaring thresholds in advance — in a single editable knob file, separate from the analysis scripts — keeps the pass/fail line auditable, even though every threshold is an acknowledged policy choice rather than a universal constant.
Q5How can the fuzzing gate pass while the safety gate fails — aren't they both about safety?▾
They measure different things. The safety gate looks backward at historical bug fixes and asks how many are structurally retired by the language. The fuzzing gate looks forward at dynamic robustness under adversarial input and asks whether the Rust rewrite destabilizes the surface relative to C today. A faithful port can preserve behavioral robustness (pass fuzzing) while still inheriting a bug history dominated by classes the language doesn't automatically fix (fail safety).
Q6What exactly does "target-attributable" mean for a crash?▾
A crash whose report matches driver-name or driver-path tokens by regex (or is manually confirmed via the audited override). It excludes crashes attributable to the VM, the fuzzing harness, or unrelated kernel subsystems shaken loose by the same campaign — without this split, raw crash counts would be dominated by noise unrelated to either driver.
Q7Why pair a p-value with an effect size (A12) instead of using one or the other?▾
A p-value alone can flag a statistically real but practically tiny difference as "significant" given enough samples; an effect size alone says nothing about whether the difference could be chance. Requiring both p<0.05 and A12≥0.71 means the fuzzing gate only fails on a regression that is both statistically distinguishable and practically large — an appropriate, conservative bar given only N=10 campaigns.
Q8Does KOxI prove Rust storage drivers are slower in general?▾
No — the paper explicitly disclaims this generalization. The delta magnitudes are properties of this one synthetic, data-path-free pair; what's meant to transfer is the pipeline's gates, attribution method, and audit trail, not the specific percentages.
Q9Can KOxI's thresholds be reused unchanged for a different driver, like an NVMe driver?▾
No — the paper treats every threshold as a per-driver policy decision. A latency-bound NVMe driver shouldn't get the same performance ceiling as a synthetic test driver, and the fio workload matrix itself is a knob: an HDD-class device, for instance, would need sequential-heavy cells since seek latency would otherwise swamp per-operation driver overhead.
Q10What's the natural next step for this case study?▾
Re-running the performance gate once Hindborg's 79-patch feature-parity series (under review as of February 2026) lands memory backing in rnull — that would let the comparison move past the current submit/complete-only upper bound toward a real data-path measurement. The authors also propose pointing Phase 1 at not-yet-migrated block drivers to rank future R4L investment.

Core terms
KOxIThe paper's evidence-gated pipeline: a Phase 1 screen plus three independently-reported Phase 2 gates (safety, fuzzing, performance), aggregated worst-of.
Safety gateElimination rate = share of CWE-classified historical fix commits in the ACSAC "auto-eliminated" super-class; passes above 34.2%.
Fuzzing gateDo-no-harm test on matched syzkaller campaigns: fails only if target-attributable crashes show p<0.05 AND A12≥0.71 for Rust vs C.
Performance gateMatched 18-cell fio sweep; passes only if every cell's bootstrap CI lower bound clears −5% and none survives Holm-corrected significance.
Worst-of aggregationAny failing gate makes the candidate "not ready"; all three passing makes it "evidence-supported." Deliberately not a weighted score.
Target-attributable crashA crash regex-matched (or manually confirmed) to the driver under test, as opposed to VM/harness/unrelated-subsystem noise.
Supporting terms
CWECommon Weakness Enumeration — labels a bug's mechanism; KOxI reuses Li et al.'s three-super-class mapping (Table 2).
Auto-eliminated / needs discipline / language-unaffectedThe three ACSAC super-classes: structurally prevented by Rust; helped but not guaranteed by Rust; unaffected by the language.
Mann–Whitney UNonparametric test for whether one sample's values rank systematically higher than another's.
Vargha–Delaney A12Effect size: probability a random draw from group A exceeds a random draw from group B. 0.5 = no difference, ≥0.71 = large.
Bootstrap CIConfidence interval built by resampling observed data with replacement (B=10,000 here) rather than assuming a parametric distribution.
Holm correctionSequentially-rejective multiple-comparison correction controlling family-wise error across the 18 fio cells.
Phase 1 lensesHistorical risk, static surface, dynamic robustness, tractability — each 0–3, mean sets the candidate band.
null_blk / rnullLinux's synthetic no-data-movement block driver (C) and Hindborg's upstream behavior-matched Rust rewrite.
