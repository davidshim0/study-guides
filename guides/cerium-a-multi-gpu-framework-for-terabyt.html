Turning racks of ordinary GPUs into an FHE ASIC — entirely in software

A hospital wants an AI model to score a patient's genome without ever seeing the raw data. Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) makes that possible — at the cost of ~terabytes of memory and kernels that barely dent the GPU. This is the plain-English tour of Cerium, the compiler and runtime that closes most of that gap.

📄 Jayashankar, Kim, Sullivan, Zheng, Skarlatos · SOSP '26 🎓 Some GPU/systems background assumed — primers cover FHE ⏱ ~25 min read
🧩

Don't hand-tune FHE kernels one workload at a time — compile them from a small algebra of limb operations, and don't let terabytes of ciphertext state exhaust memory — orchestrate it like a tiered system.
A DSL, compiler, and runtime do together what took experts months per workload.

Quick glossary — acronyms you'll see everywhere

1What FHE buys you, concretely▾

A user encrypts a query and sends it to a server. The server runs a model over the ciphertext and sends back an encrypted answer — it never sees the plaintext input, and never needs to be trusted. Confidentiality is mathematical, not a promise about hardware or software.

The catch: every arithmetic operation is now thousands of times more expensive, and data is much bigger encrypted than in the clear.

Now you're ready: Cerium's whole job is clawing back that overhead on GPUs until it's close to what custom FHE silicon achieves.
2CKKS: slots, encoding, encryption▾

CKKS batches a whole vector of real numbers into one ciphertext — each entry is a slot. Encoding turns the vector into a plaintext polynomial; encryption turns that into a ciphertext (a pair of polynomials). CKKS supports exactly three primitive ops on this packed vector: addition, multiplication, and cyclic rotation.

Everything else — matrix multiply, softmax, GELU — has to be built out of those three, since CKKS has no way to touch a single slot directly.

Now you're ready: this is why FHE circuits for ML models are huge and shape-specific — and why Cerium needs a compiler rather than hand-written kernels.
3RNS and limbs▾

Polynomial coefficients in CKKS are huge integers (thousands of bits). Doing modular arithmetic on numbers that large is slow. RNS decomposes the modulus into a set of machine-word-sized primes; a limb is the residue of a coefficient with respect to one of those primes.

Now arithmetic happens limb-by-limb, in parallel, on ordinary 32/64-bit hardware — which is exactly the kind of data-parallel work GPUs are built for.

Now you're ready: Cerium's whole compiler works at the limb level — its "Limb IR" is the atomic unit it reasons about.
4NTT — fast polynomial multiplication▾

Multiplying two large polynomials coefficient-by-coefficient is quadratic. The NTT (a modular FFT) transforms polynomials into a domain where multiplication is just elementwise — and the inverse (INTT) transforms back. Ciphertext limbs are usually kept in this "evaluation domain" by default.

Now you're ready: NTT/INTT are two of the most common — and most fusable — kernels in an FHE program; you'll see them in the fusion diagrams.
5Bootstrapping and the multiplicative budget▾

Every ciphertext has a finite "budget" of multiplications before noise ruins it. Bootstrapping is an expensive maintenance operation that refreshes this budget so computation can continue indefinitely. Since every deep FHE program needs it constantly (Llama3-8B prefill uses 5,890 bootstraps), its speed sets a floor on everything else.

Now you're ready: Cerium's headline "7.5 ms bootstrap" number is the single most consequential benchmark in the paper.
6Evaluation keys and keyswitching▾

Rotations and some multiplications require an auxiliary keyswitch operation, which itself needs large evaluation keys (10–50 GB per model). When you spread computation across multiple GPUs, keyswitching is also where most of the cross-GPU communication comes from.

Now you're ready: the two keyswitch communication patterns (IBKS, OAKS) are exactly what Cerium's multi-GPU optimizations target.
7Why FHE is thousands of tiny kernels▾

Individual FHE primitives (an NTT, a rotation, a limb-wise add) map to kernels that run for only 5–10 microseconds. A GPU launching that many tiny kernels back-to-back spends much of its time on launch overhead and idles waiting for data, instead of computing.

Now you're ready: this is the entire motivation for kernel fusion — combine many tiny kernels into fewer, larger ones.
8CUDA Graphs and Unified Virtual Memory▾

A CUDA Graph captures a whole DAG of kernels once, then replays it with a single low-overhead launch — avoiding thousands of individual launches. UVM (Unified Virtual Memory) lets a program address more memory than physically fits on the GPU, paging data in from host memory on demand — but "on demand" (page faults) is slow if nothing tells it what to prefetch.

Now you're ready: Cerium builds CUDA Graphs automatically from its schedule, and drives UVM with its own compiler-derived prefetching instead of leaving it to page faults.
The whole story in one place

Cerium in a nutshell

The four problems, the fixes, and the payoff.

GPU libraries for FHE hand-optimize kernels for a handful of primitives. That works for small models, but breaks down for large ones for four intertwined reasons: (1) FHE primitives are short-running kernels that under-utilize the GPU, and manual kernel fusion doesn't generalize past the specific patterns someone bothered to write; (2) encrypted LLMs need terabytes of memory — 1.5 TB for BERT-Base, 112 TB for Llama3-8B — for weights, intermediate ciphertexts, and evaluation keys; (3) thousands of tiny kernels means thousands of launch overheads; and (4) scaling past one GPU needs carefully scheduled inter-GPU communication.

Cerium answers all four with a single DSL + compiler + runtime stack. It compiles CKKS circuits into fused GPU kernels using rule-based fusion over a small "Limb IR"; it shrinks plaintext memory by over 100× using an algebraic property of periodic packing; it builds CUDA Graphs automatically and orchestrates host↔GPU memory movement; and it minimizes and overlaps inter-GPU communication.

The payoff: Cerium beats hand-tuned GPU libraries by up to 2.25×, is the first GPU system under 10 ms bootstrapping (7.5 ms), and is the first to demonstrate sub-10-second encrypted BERT-Base inference (8.8 s) and the first Llama3-8B prefill (43 s TTFT) end to end — averaging 1.06×–4.40× faster than specialized FHE ASICs (CraterLake, ARK, Cinnamon-4/8), with zero custom silicon.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • FHE circuits map to short kernels (5–10 µs); automated limb-level fusion is what fixes GPU under-utilization.
  • Periodic plaintext packing wastes memory through repetition; a power-of-two-period algebraic trick compresses it 96–119× losslessly.
  • A memory-pool layout separates data by lifetime (evalkeys, weights, intermediates); reused pools swap via pointer updates between sessions, while large per-layer weights (e.g., Llama3-8B) are still explicitly prefetched from host to GPU.
  • CUDA Graphs are built once during runtime initialization, from the compiler's schedule, and reused across queries.
  • Multi-GPU communication is minimized by algebraically merging keyswitch steps into a single all-reduce, then overlapped with compute.
  • Result: averages 1.06×–4.4× faster than FHE ASICs (CraterLake, ARK, Cinnamon-4/8), at <1% of the effort of hand tuning per workload.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about Cerium

Each stands alone. Every card ends with an honest note about where it breaks.

The core idea · Kernel fusion

One truck, not three separate errands

Driving to three different stores for milk, bread, and eggs wastes most of your time on the road, not the shopping. Combine them into one trip and the fixed cost (driving, parking) is paid once. FHE's short kernels are the separate errands; horizontal fusion batches independent ones into the same trip, and vertical fusion lets dependent ones hand off goods directly in the truck (registers) instead of unloading to a warehouse (global memory) in between.

Where it breaks: pack the truck too full and it can't turn corners — over-fusing stresses the register file until values "spill" to slow memory, so Cerium caps kernel size (Fig. 6) rather than fusing everything possible.
The algebra · Plaintext compression

A rug with a repeating pattern

A patterned rug doesn't need a full-resolution photo — the weaver only needs one tile and a count of repeats. FHE's slot-packing repeats matrix values across a periodic vector to make rotations cheap, but that repetition also survives (in a compressed form) all the way through encoding into the plaintext's numeric representation. Cerium proves it stays contiguous and stores only the distinct values plus their indices — a lossless 90–119× shrink for weights that repeat with a power-of-two period.

Where it breaks: the trick needs the period to be an exact power of two — the theorem's proof leans on powers-of-two aligning cleanly through bit-reversal (Thm. 1). An odd or non-power-of-two repeat pattern doesn't compress this way; it must be padded first.
Memory pools · Pointer swaps

Hot-desking, not moving offices

An office with hot-desking keeps the desks (addresses) fixed; only the nameplate (a pointer) changes when a new team sits down. Cerium's memory pools work the same way: a fixed region is reserved for weights, another for evalkeys, another for intermediate ciphertexts. Swapping to a new session means updating a pointer, not allocating and copying gigabytes of new memory — though moving to a new layer's weights (e.g., in Llama3-8B) still means prefetching that data in from host memory, just into a pre-reserved slot instead of a freshly allocated one.

Where it breaks: hot-desking only works if the last occupant's papers are gone — Cerium only reuses a location within a kernel if it can prove the old value is read by exactly one thread; rotation operands are barred from reuse entirely because thread-block ordering isn't guaranteed.
Launch overhead · CUDA Graphs

A pre-printed itinerary, not asking directions each stop

Stopping to ask for directions before every single turn of a road trip adds up fast; a pre-printed itinerary lets you drive straight through. A CUDA Graph is that itinerary for a program's thousands of kernels — built once by the runtime during initialization, following the compiler's schedule, launched with one call, and reused across every subsequent inference by just updating which weights/evalkeys it points to.

Where it breaks: the itinerary only stays valid if the destinations (kernel dependencies) don't change — Cerium reuses graphs across sessions only because its memory-pool design lets a "new trip" be expressed as swapped pointers, not a redrawn plan.
Direct tie-in · Pinning & prefetch

A compiler-directed, two-tier memory system

Llama3-8B's weights (982 GB, compressed) don't fit on the GPU or even in host RAM comfortably alongside everything else. Cerium pins the things reused constantly — evalkeys, bootstrap matrices, intermediates — in GPU memory ("hot tier"), and shuttles per-layer weight tiles from host memory ("cold tier") just before each layer runs, prefetched ahead of time rather than faulted in reactively.

Where it breaks — and where it's most relevant to memory-tiering research: this is a static, compiler-scheduled tiering policy, not an adaptive one. It works because the call graph and access order are fully known offline; there's no runtime hotness tracking or promotion/demotion like an OS-managed tiering system (e.g. page-fault-driven placement) would use — naive UVM without this schedule is 12.1× slower (§6.5.5).
Communication · Merging keyswitches

A conference call instead of mail-then-remail

If everyone mails a letter to a central office, which tallies them and mails a summary back to everyone, that's two full round trips. A conference call gets the same shared result in one exchange. When an output-aggregation keyswitch (OAKS) is immediately followed by an input-broadcast keyswitch (IBKS), Cerium reorders the intervening step and cancels the redundant scatter/gather, collapsing both into a single all-reduce.

Where it breaks: the shortcut applies specifically when an OAKS output feeds directly into an IBKS — a structural pattern common after matrix multiplications and in bootstrapping, but not a universal fix for every communication step in the program.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Following the pipeline from DSL program to running GPU code.

1 · Four problems, one root cause: no holistic view

Prior GPU FHE libraries optimize kernels in isolation. That misses cross-cutting structure: fusion decisions depend on the whole program's dependency graph; memory decisions depend on which data outlives which function call; communication decisions depend on the whole multi-GPU schedule. Cerium's answer is to lower everything through a compiler that sees the entire program at once.

⚙
Pipeline: DSL program → polynomial IR (hoisting, mod-down merging, double rescale) → Limb IR → horizontal + vertical fusion → kernel codegen → kernel/memory schedule → runtime (CudaGraphs, memory pools, multi-GPU orchestration).

2 · Limb IR fusion — horizontal, then vertical

Cerium reasons about fusion at the limb level because limb operations fall into a small set of classes that don't fuse across each other, keeping the search space tractable. Horizontal fusion first combines data-independent instructions with the same opcode into one kernel's separate thread blocks — this mainly fixes GPU under-utilization. Vertical fusion then combines data-dependent, same-thread instructions so they pass values through registers instead of global memory — this mainly cuts bandwidth. A rule check disallows fusion that would create a cycle, mix opcodes, or unsafely share data across thread blocks (e.g. a rotation's output can't be read in the same kernel by another block).

3 · Plaintext compressed encoding

A periodic plaintext vector (period T, a power of two) stays periodic under the permutation CKKS applies before encoding (Lemma 2); its inverse FFT becomes a sparse polynomial with nonzero coefficients only every N/2T positions (Lemma 3); transforming that sparse polynomial back with the NTT produces evaluation values that repeat with period 2T (Lemma 4); and bit-reversing a periodic vector whose period is a power of two yields a contiguous block of distinct values (Lemma 5) — which is exactly what you need to compress by storing only the unique values plus an index.

// periodic vector (period T, power of 2) → sparse IFFT → repeated NTT values → contiguous → compressed
plaintext P[period=T]  // e.g. a repeated matrix diagonal
   → permute (Lemma 2: stays T-periodic)
   → IFFT     // sparse: nonzero every N/2T coeffs (Lemma 3)
   → NTT       // repeats every 2T slots (Lemma 4)
   → bit-reverse  // contiguous run of ≤2T distinct values (Lemma 5)
   → store distinct values + index map   // lossless, 90–119× smaller
🍯
Concretely: BERT-Base's 1.5 TB of encoded weights compress to 16.6 GB — small enough to fit entirely in GPU memory; Llama3-8B's 112 TB compress to 982 GB. Ciphertext-plaintext operations run directly on the compressed form via an index lookup (Fig. 14), so nothing has to be decompressed before use.

4 · Memory scheduling, pooling, pinning, and prefetch

The compiler's memory schedule reuses buffers in place wherever a value's lifetime ends, and groups GPU memory into typed pools — evalkeys, bootstrap matrices/plaintexts, intermediate ciphertexts, and per-layer weights — based on how often each category changes.

💡
Why this matters beyond FHE: it's a compiler-directed, offline analog of a two-tier memory hierarchy — pinned "hot" pool on the fast device, prefetched "cold" pool on the slower one, driven by known static structure rather than runtime hotness signals. Section 6.5.5's naive-UVM baseline (no schedule) is 12.1× slower — a concrete data point for how much a placement policy is worth when access order is knowable ahead of time.

5 · Multi-GPU: minimize communication, then hide it

Cinnamon's parallelization uses two keyswitch communication patterns: IBKS (broadcast, then compute) and OAKS (compute, then aggregate-and-scatter). When an OAKS feeds an IBKS, Cerium reorders the intervening INTT past the gather step and cancels the resulting scatter/gather pair, replacing two communication rounds with one all-reduce (Fig. 8) — a 44% reduction in bytes communicated over Cinnamon. Remaining communication is scheduled as early as possible, split into smaller chunks, and issued on dedicated CUDA streams so it overlaps with computation instead of stalling it — hiding 99% of host-to-device and 58% of device-to-device latency in the Llama3-8B transformer block.

Follow the numbers

Worked examples

Four traces carried all the way through, using the paper's own measurements.

① Stacking horizontal and vertical fusion (bootstrap)

Horizontal fusion alone gives 1.98× on bootstrapping; vertical fusion adds another 1.68× on top. What's the combined speedup, and does it match the paper's reported number?

  1. Multiply the stages: 1.98 × 1.68 = 3.33×.
  2. Paper reports 3.32× for bootstrapping overall — matches within rounding.
  3. Same pattern on ResNet-20: 1.84× (horizontal) × 1.43× (vertical) = 2.63×, exactly the paper's reported total.
  4. Insight: the two passes compose roughly multiplicatively because they attack different costs — launch/utilization (horizontal) vs. memory bandwidth (vertical) — so gains from one don't cannibalize the other.
🍯
This is an internal ablation against Cerium's own unfused baseline — not a comparison to Cheddar, which starts from a different (already partly hand-fused) baseline.

② Reading the compression ratio

The paper states 96× for BERT-Base (1.5 TB → 16.6 GB) and 119× for Llama3-8B (112 TB → 982 GB). Do the raw numbers match?

  1. BERT-Base, naively: 1500 GB ÷ 16.6 GB = 90.4×, a bit below the stated 96×.
  2. Llama3-8B, naively: 112,000 GB ÷ 982 GB = 114.1×, a bit below the stated 119×.
  3. Reconciling: "1.5 TB" and "112 TB" are rounded headline figures; back-solving from the stated ratios implies the unrounded footprints are closer to ≈1.59 TB and ≈116.9 TB — consistent with reporting truncated figures for readability.
  4. Takeaway for you: treat the 90–120× range as the trustworthy order of magnitude; don't over-read the last digit of either the ratio or the rounded TB figures.
📎
This gap is exactly the kind of thing worth checking rather than assuming — the paper's numbers are self-consistent once you allow for the stated figures being rounded, not contradictory.

③ Strong vs. weak scaling across GPUs

From Table 1 (B200): ResNet-20 goes 456→298 ms (1→8 GPUs); Llama3-8B goes 241→43 s. Which scales better, and why?

  1. ResNet-20 speedup: 456/298 = 1.53× on 8 GPUs → efficiency 1.53/8 ≈ 19%.
  2. Llama3-8B speedup: 241/43 = 5.60× on 8 GPUs → efficiency 5.60/8 ≈ 70%.
  3. Why the gap: ResNet-20 is small (a handful of bootstraps) so keyswitch communication overhead dominates once you spread it across 8 GPUs; Llama3-8B's 5,890 bootstraps give the scheduler abundant independent compute to overlap with communication.
  4. Lesson: Cerium's multi-GPU wins are largest exactly where they're needed most — the biggest, most memory-hungry workloads.
💡
Try the interactive GPU explorer on the "How It Works" tab to see this pattern across all four benchmarks.

④ Reading ASIC speedup ratios

The paper states Cerium (8×B200) is 1.06× over CraterLake, 2.33× over ARK, 3.03× over Cinnamon-4, and 4.40× over Cinnamon-8 — averaged across bootstrap, ResNet-20, and BERT-Base.

  1. "1.06× over CraterLake" means Cerium is about 6% faster on average — the paper calls this "matching" CraterLake, i.e., essentially at parity.
  2. "4.40× over Cinnamon-8" means Cerium is 4.4 times faster than an 8-chip Cinnamon ASIC cluster on the same benchmarks.
  3. Caveat to carry forward: ASIC numbers come from simulation studies, not fabricated silicon — so this is Cerium (real hardware, measured) vs. ASICs (projected, unbuilt) for CraterLake/ARK/Cinnamon.
  4. Why it's still meaningful: it shows a software-only stack on commodity GPUs closing (and in some cases matching) a gap that previously seemed to require custom fabrication.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer.

Problem 1 · easy
Why doesn't Cerium fuse kernels as aggressively as possible, if fusion is generally good?
Show answer
Aggressive vertical fusion increases the number of live values (operands) a kernel must hold in registers at once. Past a threshold, this causes register spills to slow memory — Figure 6 shows a 2× performance drop at a 2K fusion threshold, from 70 KB of spills per thread. Cerium caps kernel size and splits oversized fused kernels instead.Misconception: "more fusion is strictly better." It has diminishing then negative returns — the sweet spot is a tuned threshold, not the maximum possible fusion.
Problem 2 · medium
Unfused GELU takes 84.98 ms; horizontal fusion brings it to 54.87 ms; vertical fusion further to 36.13 ms. Compute the horizontal speedup, the additional vertical speedup, and the total.
Show answer
Horizontal: 84.98/54.87 ≈ 1.55×. Vertical (on top of horizontal): 54.87/36.13 ≈ 1.52×. Total: 84.98/36.13 ≈ 2.35× (matches 1.55×1.52 ≈ 2.36, within rounding).Misconception: adding percentage speedups instead of multiplying factors — a 55% gain followed by a 52% gain is not a 107% total gain, it's ×1.55×1.52.
Problem 3 · medium
Why can't Cerium's plaintext compression apply to a matrix packed with a period of, say, 12 (not a power of two)?
Show answer
The proof chain (Lemmas 2–5) relies on the period dividing the ring dimension as a power of two so that bit-reversal maps equal-residue indices into a contiguous block — a non-power-of-two period wouldn't align this way under the bit-reversal permutation, so the repeated NTT values wouldn't land contiguously and couldn't be compressed by the same simple index scheme.Misconception: "any repeated pattern compresses the same way." The mechanism is a specific algebraic consequence of powers of two, not repetition in general — non-power-of-two periods must be padded up first.
Problem 4 · hard
Naive UVM (no prefetching schedule) makes Llama3-8B take 3334 s instead of 275 s — a 12.1× slowdown. What's actually going wrong, mechanically?
Show answer
Without a schedule, UVM only learns what data is needed when a kernel actually touches an unmapped page — triggering a page fault, a small, blocking, reactive transfer. Cerium's compiler already knows the full call graph and layer order ahead of time, so it issues large, pipelined prefetches for the next layer's weights before they're needed, overlapping transfer with computation instead of stalling on faults.Misconception: "UVM automatically manages host-device movement about as well as an explicit schedule." UVM is a correctness mechanism (nothing crashes), not a performance one — the difference between reactive faulting and proactive prefetching is over an order of magnitude here.
Problem 5 · medium
BERT-Base with compression but no memory-reuse schedule takes 81.68 s; with the schedule, 32.55 s (2.51× faster). What's the schedule actually eliminating that compression alone doesn't touch?
Show answer
Repeated online allocation and deallocation of intermediate ciphertext buffers — every temporary value would otherwise trigger its own allocate/free call. The memory schedule instead reuses fixed buffer locations across non-overlapping lifetimes, so no allocator calls happen during execution at all.Misconception: "compression solves the memory bottleneck." Compression shrinks the footprint of persistent data (weights); allocation overhead is a separate cost from constantly creating and destroying transient ciphertexts, and needs its own fix.
Problem 6 · hard
The paper explicitly scopes out attacks on metadata leakage, model stealing, and a malicious (not just curious) server. Why are each of these out of scope given what FHE guarantees?
Show answer
FHE hides the values being computed on, not the shape of the computation — input size, token count, and which model is queried are all visible from the execution pattern itself, independent of encryption. Model stealing is out of scope because the client legitimately receives decrypted outputs — nothing about FHE prevents someone from learning about a model through its outputs. And FHE gives confidentiality, not integrity: nothing stops a malicious server from returning a wrong (but validly encrypted-looking) answer unless a separate verifiability mechanism is added.Misconception: "FHE means the interaction is fully private and verifiably correct." It specifically protects the input/output values from an honest-but-curious server — three distinct, common misreadings of what that buys you.
Problem 7 · easy
Is 7.5 ms bootstrapping and 8.8 s BERT-Base inference fast enough for a live chat-style application?
Show answer
No — the paper is explicit that these latencies (tens of seconds for full model inference) are unsuitable for most interactive services. They target offline/asynchronous use cases instead: batch clinical risk scoring, legal document discovery, anti-money-laundering screening, or encrypted vulnerability scanning, where the value of confidentiality outweighs a delay measured in seconds to minutes.Misconception: "breaking the 10 ms bootstrap barrier means FHE is now real-time-ready." One fast primitive doesn't make a full model's end-to-end latency interactive — full BERT-Base still takes 8.8 s, and Llama3-8B prefill 43 s, for a single query.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Ten questions newcomers ask most.

Q1What's actually new here — hasn't GPU FHE existed for years?▾
Prior GPU FHE libraries hand-optimize kernels for specific primitives and small models. Cerium is the first to combine an automated, general kernel-fusing compiler, a lossless 100×-class memory compression technique, and multi-GPU communication scheduling into one system — which is what lets it scale to BERT-Base and Llama3-8B rather than just ResNet-20-sized workloads.
Q2Why not just build a custom FHE ASIC instead?▾
ASICs like CraterLake, ARK, and Cinnamon do reach great performance, but they require cutting-edge fabrication nodes, advanced packaging (CoWoS), and HBM — expensive and slow to produce and iterate on. Cerium shows that a sufficiently well-designed software stack running on commodity datacenter GPUs can get within striking distance without any custom silicon.
Q3How does Cerium decide what to fuse, automatically?▾
It works at the "Limb IR" level, where the set of operation classes is small enough to encode as rules: same-opcode, no-cycle instructions fuse horizontally; same-thread, dependency-compatible instructions fuse vertically. The compiler checks these rules pairwise over the program's dependency DAG.
Q4Is the plaintext compression lossy, like JPEG?▾
No — it's exactly lossless. It exploits a specific algebraic fact (Theorem 1): encoding a periodic vector with a power-of-two period produces a bit-reversed NTT representation of the plaintext limb vector with genuinely repeated values, not approximately similar ones. Storing the distinct values plus an index map reconstructs the original exactly.
Q5Why does the memory layout matter so much for Llama3-8B specifically?▾
Llama3-8B's compressed footprint (982 GB) still exceeds a single GPU's memory and can strain host memory too, alongside evaluation keys and intermediate ciphertexts. Without a compiler-directed schedule for what's pinned vs. prefetched, naive automatic paging (UVM) is 12.1× slower — the layout is what makes running the model at all practical.
Q6What exactly does "44% less communication" refer to?▾
It's the reduction in bytes transferred between GPUs during multi-GPU bootstrapping, from merging output-aggregation and input-broadcast keyswitches into a single all-reduce and removing redundant broadcasts, compared to Cinnamon's original parallel keyswitching scheme.
Q7Are the ASIC comparisons against real chips?▾
No — CraterLake, ARK, and Cinnamon are all simulation-based projections from their respective papers, not measurements on fabricated silicon. The comparison should be read as "software on real GPUs vs. projected specialized hardware," which is still a meaningful bar, but not an apples-to-apples measured comparison.
Q8Does Cerium support chatbot-style multi-turn generation?▾
Not yet — the paper only demonstrates the prefill stage (processing the prompt, producing the first token) for Llama3-8B. Efficient encrypted KV-cache management for token-by-token decoding is listed as future work.
Q9Could this approach work for FHE schemes other than CKKS?▾
The authors say yes in principle — Limb IR targets RNS-based modular polynomial arithmetic generally, which underlies other RNS-RLWE schemes like BGV and BFV, not just CKKS. Extending it is future work, not something demonstrated in this paper.
Q10What's the single biggest caveat to keep in mind?▾
Latency: even the fastest configuration is tens of seconds per query for real workloads, and the threat model explicitly excludes metadata leakage, model stealing, and a malicious (not just curious) server. Cerium is a major systems advance for making large-model FHE feasible at all — not a claim that FHE is now fast or maximally secure for every use case.
