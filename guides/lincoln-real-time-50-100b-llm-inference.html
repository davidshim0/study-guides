Don't haul the weights to the chip — compute where they already sleep

A 66–137B-parameter model can't fit in a laptop's DRAM, so it lives in Flash — and every single token, the whole model has to be dragged out through a doorway barely wide enough for a garden hose. This is the plain-English story of Lincoln, which widens the doorway for one phase and skips it entirely for the other.

📄 "Lincoln: Real-Time 50~100B LLM Inference on Consumer Devices..." · Sun et al. 🎓 Basic OS/memory-hierarchy background assumed — primers cover the rest ⏱ ~30 min read
🔌

Give the compute-heavy prefill phase a fat pipe to the NPU (LPDDR), and let the memory-heavy generation phase compute right where the Flash lives — one bottleneck, two completely different fixes.

1Two very different phases of LLM inference▾

Prefill runs once, over the whole input prompt at once: every weight matrix is used as a GEMM (matrix-matrix multiply), which is compute-heavy. Generation then runs many times, once per output token: with only one token as input, the same weights are used as a GEMV (matrix-vector multiply) — far less arithmetic per byte of weight read, so it's memory-heavy instead. Attention also reads the growing KV Cache during both phases.

Now you're ready: Lincoln gives these two phases completely different fixes — a fast interface for prefill, on-Flash compute for generation — because they stress totally different parts of the system.
2Why 50~100B models don't fit in a laptop▾

Consumer devices rarely exceed 64GB of DRAM, while 66B–137B-parameter models weigh in at 100–200+GB. Since every execution iteration must touch essentially the entire weight set, most of the model has to be re-read from Flash storage on every single iteration — the paper calls this "capacity thrashing."

Now you're ready: this capacity mismatch is the whole reason Lincoln exists — Flash has to hold what DRAM can't, and it has to do it fast enough to feel real-time.
3Memory vs. storage: two very different interfaces▾

On a consumer SoC, LPDDR connects the DRAM system through wide, low-latency channels (16 pins/channel, up to 8+ channels, >100GB/s total). Flash storage instead connects through narrow serial links — PCIe (NVMe) or MPHY (UFS), just 4–8 lanes, ~10GB/s — because Flash was historically built to be big and cheap, never fast.

Now you're ready: Lincoln's core architectural trick is smuggling Flash onto DRAM's wide interface, instead of trying to build Flash a fatter narrow one.
4Why Flash arrays are internally slow▾

Flash is organized as dies → planes → blocks → pages. Density-oriented designs use large arrays to maximize capacity, but large bitlines/wordlines carry high parasitic resistance and capacitance, which dominates read latency (tens of µs) and limits how many independent planes can fit — and thus how much can run in parallel — per die.

Now you're ready: Lincoln shrinks the arrays (building on commercial XL-Flash) specifically to cut this RC-dominated latency and fit more parallel planes per die.
5Hybrid bonding — 3D stacking without TSVs▾

Wafer-to-wafer hybrid bonding connects two dies' metal layers directly, letting a chip be split into an array die plus a separate logic die for peripherals — without the cost of Through-Silicon Vias (TSVs). It's already commercialized for consumer Flash (e.g. YTMC's Xtacking).

Now you're ready: hybrid bonding is what lets Lincoln shrink its arrays and add compute logic underneath, without losing storage density or paying HBM-grade packaging costs.
6Near-data / near-storage computing▾

Instead of moving data across a slow interface to a far-away compute unit, put a small compute unit right where the data already lives. Lincoln places tiny FP-Dot units in the logic layer, one beneath every Flash plane.

Now you're ready: near-Flash compute is Lincoln's answer for whenever even LPDDR's >100GB/s isn't fast enough — this is specifically the generation-phase fix.
7Speculative decoding▾

A small "draft" model predicts several future tokens quickly; the large target model then verifies all of them in one batched pass (like a mini-prefill), accepting the longest correct prefix. Since verification reuses each weight once to (possibly) confirm several tokens, memory traffic per accepted token drops.

Now you're ready: Lincoln pairs speculative decoding with near-Flash compute specifically so a >100B model can still hit a real-time per-token latency target.
8Flash reliability: ECC, read disturb, and P/E cycles▾

Every Flash read needs error correction because cells degrade. Repeatedly reading the same cells (read disturb) accumulates errors over time; the fix is periodically reprogramming them ("read reclaim"), which consumes a P/E cycle — the fundamental wear-out budget of Flash.

Now you're ready: Lincoln reads the same weights relentlessly during generation, so it has to budget P/E cycles carefully against read disturb — covered in How It Works.
The whole story in one place

Lincoln in a nutshell

Two bottlenecks, one device-level fix, and two very different architecture-level fixes.

On a consumer device, a >50B LLM's weights mostly live in Flash storage, not DRAM, and nearly all of them must be re-read on every execution iteration. Measured on a baseline system (NVMe SSD + 2 LPDDR packages), that weight loading dominates everything: 94.0% of generation latency on average (per-token generation latency runs up to 41.8s), and 91.4% of prefill latency. Even with unlimited transmission bandwidth, the paper shows the internal Flash array itself still bottlenecks execution — a conventional design tops out around 41GB/s even with 32 dies × 4 planes, far below what an LLM needs.

So there are really two bottlenecks: Flash's narrow external interface (~10GB/s over PCIe/MPHY) and its slow internal array bandwidth. Lincoln attacks both. At the device level, it shrinks Flash arrays (building on commercial XL-Flash) for lower latency and more parallel planes, and uses hybrid bonding to hide the peripheral-area cost — reaching ~34GB/s per die and ~500GB/s across 16 dies, at 74.6% area efficiency (vs. XL-Flash's ~55%). At the architecture level, it applies two different fixes for two different phases: for compute-heavy prefill, it makes Flash dies act as an extra LPDDR rank, riding the DRAM interface's existing >100GB/s pipe to the NPU; for memory-heavy generation, it drops in tiny near-Flash FP-Dot compute units that skip moving weights over the interface entirely (input vectors and results still cross it), further boosted by speculative decoding.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • Two distinct bottlenecks: Flash's narrow external interface (~10GB/s) and its slow internal array bandwidth (~41GB/s baseline cap).
  • Device level: array shrinking + hybrid bonding → ~34GB/s/die, ~500GB/s aggregate, 74.6% area efficiency.
  • Prefill fix: Flash dies act as an extra LPDDR rank — reuse the existing wide DRAM interface instead of building Flash a new one.
  • Generation fix: near-Flash FP-Dot compute skips moving weights over the interface (input vectors and results still cross it); speculative decoding adds further headroom.
  • Timing/correctness handled via a Flash-side SRAM buffer, double buffering, and near-Flash ECC — nothing on LPDDR's critical timing path.
  • One shared weight copy (round-robin layout) serves both the NPU and near-Flash compute — no duplicated storage.
  • Result: up to 13.23×/254.1× prefill/generation speedup, 7.98% near-Flash logic area overhead, acceptable heat and cost.
Seven everyday pictures

How to think about Lincoln

Each stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with where the picture stops being true.

The core trick · Interface bandwidth

A garden hose vs. a highway on-ramp

Flash's external interface is a garden hose (~10GB/s over a few PCIe/MPHY lanes); the DRAM system's LPDDR interface is a multi-lane highway (>100GB/s over 100+ pins). Lincoln doesn't dig a new road for Flash — it simply drives the Flash dies onto the highway already there, disguised as extra DRAM ranks.

Where it breaks: a real highway ramp is free; here, Flash's slow internal reads (µs) and chunky ECC still don't fit LPDDR's ~100ns timing — so a buffer is needed to smooth the on-ramp (see the "prepping ingredients" analogy).
Device level · Array shrinking

One warehouse aisle vs. four short ones

One long warehouse aisle means a forklift travels far before it finds anything (high latency), and only one forklift fits at a time (no parallelism). Split it into several short aisles and each trip is quick, and multiple forklifts (planes) can work at once. Lincoln shrinks each Flash plane the same way, doubling plane count per die without growing the die.

Where it breaks: more, smaller aisles need more doorways (peripheral circuitry) — which is exactly the density cost hybrid bonding has to hide.
Device level · Hybrid bonding

Moving the back office to the basement

A shop's sales floor is more valuable per square foot than its back-office admin. Hybrid bonding relocates the "admin" (page buffers, PHY) to a separate logic-layer basement, freeing the array floor above to be almost entirely storage — 74.6% area efficiency vs. ~55% when admin and storage share one floor.

Where it breaks: row decoders stay upstairs (on the array die) in Lincoln's design, so the basement isn't everything — just the parts that benefit most from being separate.
Packaging · Sharing, not adding, channels

A guest bookshelf that shares the front door

Instead of building a whole new entrance for guest furniture, you slide an extra bookshelf into the existing hallway, sharing the same door and floor space. Lincoln's Flash dies join existing LPDDR channels as an extra rank, alongside the DRAM dies, rather than getting dedicated channels — minimizing both cost and form factor.

Where it breaks: unlike furniture, the guest (Flash) reads far slower than the resident (DRAM) — so it still needs its own internal buffering to not trip over the shared doorway's strict timing.
Overlap · Double buffering

Prepping tomorrow's ingredients while tonight's dinner cooks

A chef preps the next dish's ingredients while the current dish is already cooking, so no one waits idle. Lincoln issues a prefetch for the next weight partition while the NPU computes on the current one — since LPDDR-side computation on a partition takes >4× longer than fetching the next one internally, the fetch (and its ECC) is fully hidden.

Where it breaks: a chef can improvise; Lincoln's prefetch only works because the next partition to access is highly predictable for FC-layer computation — it wouldn't work for unpredictable access patterns.
Optimization · Near-Flash ECC

Spell-checking at the print shop, not after the mail arrives

Catching typos at the printer, before the letter is mailed, is far cheaper than the reader mailing it back for correction. Conventional designs run ECC on the MC/NPU side, forcing a redundant round-trip over LPDDR for every byte read. Lincoln moves ECC into the near-Flash logic layer, correcting data before it ever crosses the LPDDR link — and hides that cost inside the already-overlapped prefetch.

Where it breaks: ECC still needs somewhere to run — Lincoln's near-Flash ECC modules are the same ones introduced for near-Flash computing, reused for a second job, not a free lunch.
Generation phase · Speculative decoding

A fast intern drafts, the expert only proofreads

A junior intern (draft model, ~100× smaller) quickly writes several sentences; the senior expert (target model) reads them all in one pass and only rewrites from the first mistake onward. One expert-level pass can validate several tokens, so each accepted token costs less than a full re-read of every weight.

Where it breaks: the intern can be wrong — a poor acceptance rate erases the benefit, which is why the paper uses the worst reported acceptance rates for its evaluation, not the best case.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Device level first, then the two architecture-level fixes, then the housekeeping that makes it all reliable.

1 · The problem, quantified

On a baseline system (NVMe SSD + 2 LPDDR packages), weight loading consumes 94.0% of generation latency and 91.4% of prefill latency on average across OPT-66B, LLaMA-65B, and LAMDA-137B — generation latency itself runs up to 41.8s per token. Even granting unlimited transmission bandwidth, the Flash array's own read latency (>50µs, sometimes >100µs per 16KB page) caps a conventional design at ~41GB/s internal bandwidth even with 32 dies × 4 planes each — nowhere near what real-time LLM inference needs.

2 · Device level — shrinking the array, hiding the cost with hybrid bonding

Building on commercial XL-Flash (4µs read latency, 16 planes/die), Lincoln shrinks the plane size by roughly half along the bitline direction and doubles plane count to 32/die. This alone would worsen XL-Flash's already-low ~55% area efficiency and its power-supply headroom — so Lincoln uses hybrid bonding to move peripheral circuitry (page buffers, PHY) into a separate logic-layer die stacked beneath the array, leaving only row decoders on the array die.

3 · Architecture level, prefill — Flash as an extra LPDDR rank

Flash dies are stacked inside the LPDDR package alongside DRAM dies, sharing existing channels as an extra rank — no new channels, no new pins beyond a modest power-domain increase. To meet LPDDR's strict ~100ns timing despite Flash's µs-scale internal reads, a near-PHY global SRAM buffer stages readout data; a prefetch command (double buffering) overlaps the next partition's Flash read and ECC with the NPU's compute on the current partition, since LPDDR-side computation on a partition takes >4× longer than the Flash-side fetch.

# prefill pipeline (double buffering + near-Flash ECC), per weight partition
issue Prefetch(partition[i+1])            # Flash read → near-Flash ECC → global buffer
while NPU computing on partition[i] via LPDDR:
    Flash reads + corrects partition[i+1] in the background   # fully hidden: fetch < 1/4 of compute time
NPU accesses partition[i] tiles at will (buffer = random-access SRAM)
i = i + 1; repeat
💡
Why near-Flash ECC matters here: Flash ECC is chunk-wise (1KB) and long-latency (µs), which mismatches LPDDR's 32/64B, ~100ns access granularity. Running it on the controller side forces redundant LPDDR round-trips over the entire weight set. Moving ECC into the logic layer — reusing the same near-Flash hardware built for compute — removes that redundant traffic and folds the remaining latency into the already-hidden prefetch.

4 · Architecture level, generation — near-Flash compute + speculative decoding

For memory-bound GEMV, Lincoln skips moving weights over the LPDDR interface entirely — though the NPU still writes the input vector to Flash-side buffers and reads results back. The NPU broadcasts the input vector to each Flash die's global buffer; each plane's Flash-side controller reads out its rows, corrects them with near-Flash ECC, and its local FP-Dot unit computes a dot product against the broadcast vector, accumulating in a small local register file. Results are pipelined back to the global buffer for NPU readout — reads and compute overlap across partitions.

# one GEMV, one Flash plane's Logic Block (LB)
broadcast(input_vector) -> global_buffer -> all planes
for each plane in parallel:
    page = Flash_read(weight_rows)       # pipelined with the next page's read
    page = near_Flash_ECC(page)          # BCH(9088,8192,64), 14.6 Gb/s/plane
    partial = FP_Dot(input_vector, page) # 2-element dot product @ 400MHz
    local_reg_file += partial
move results -> global_buffer -> NPU readout

For >100B models, near-Flash compute alone still isn't fast enough for real-time generation (a full iteration on an 8-channel, 16-die Lincoln system easily reaches 0.4s). So Lincoln adds speculative decoding: a small draft model (~1B, ~100× smaller) predicts several tokens (~7) sequentially; the target model verifies them all in one batched, near-Flash pass — accessing the target weights only once per batch — and accepts the longest correct prefix.

# speculative decoding, one generation iteration
draft_tokens = draft_model.generate(~7 tokens)       # tiny model, sequential, cheap
verified     = target_model.verify(draft_tokens)     # one batched near-Flash pass, like append-prefill
accept longest correct prefix of verified            # >1 token per target-model weight access

5 · One weight copy, two access patterns

With only ~265GB of Flash available (2 packages), keeping separate NPU- and near-Flash-optimized copies of every weight matrix would cap Lincoln below 71B models. Instead, matrix rows are distributed round-robin across dies (coarse-grained, near-Flash-friendly), and further round-robin across each die's planes. This still lets the NPU pull a tile's rows from multiple dies in parallel — fully using LPDDR channel parallelism — while the same layout serves near-Flash GEMV row-by-row, with no duplicated storage.

🧭
A note for tiering researchers: Lincoln's DRAM/Flash split is static and workload-known, not OS-managed like page migration in TPP, HeMem, or Memtis. FC-layer weights always live in Flash; activations, KV cache, and other weights always live in DRAM — because for LLM inference, which bytes are "cold" is known at compile time, sidestepping the runtime hot/cold classification problem those systems solve.

6 · Endurance and control

Frequent generation-phase reads risk read disturb. Even an optimistic 10K-read-cycle threshold could be exceeded within 2 hours of continuous long-output generation. The fix is periodic read reclaim (reprogramming via P/E cycles), similar to DRAM refresh. SLC endures up to 100K P/E cycles, boosted to 4M by shortening the retention-time requirement from 1 year to 3 days; refresh itself costs only 609 P/E cycles per 5 years. At a conservative 5K-read-cycle disturb threshold and a 5-year warranty, even continuous generation needs only ~310K P/E cycles — well under budget — with reclaim overhead spread across iterations at ~0.4%.

Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four traces using the paper's own figures — bandwidth scaling, compute sizing, area efficiency, and the real-time latency check.

① From one plane to a 16-die system: where "~500GB/s" comes from

The paper states a single plane's internal bandwidth is 1.06GB/s. Does that really add up to ~34GB/s/die and ~500GB/s for the full system?

  1. One die has 32 planes. 1.06 GB/s × 32 = 33.92 GB/s — matches the paper's stated ~34GB/s per Lincoln Flash die.
  2. The 8-channel, 16-die system the paper describes: 33.92 GB/s × 16 = 542.7 GB/s, consistent with the paper's "~500GB/s" (rounded conservatively for realistic overheads).
  3. Contrast with the baseline: even with 32 dies × 4 planes and unlimited I/O, the conventional array design caps out at ~41GB/s total — Lincoln's single die already nearly matches that.
🍯
Insight: the ~500GB/s headline number isn't magic — it's just per-plane bandwidth × planes × dies, once array shrinking makes each plane individually fast.

② Sizing the FP-Dot unit: is "two elements at 400MHz" really enough?

The paper picks a minimal two-element dot-product unit per plane, clocked at a "conservative" 400MHz. Check the arithmetic against the plane's 1.06GB/s supply rate (BF16, 2 bytes/element).

  1. Data supply rate in elements/s: 1.06×10⁹ bytes/s ÷ 2 bytes/element = 530×10⁶ elements/s the plane can deliver.
  2. FP-Dot's consumption rate: 2 elements/cycle × 400×10⁶ cycles/s = 800×10⁶ elements/s it can process.
  3. 800M > 530M — the compute unit's throughput exceeds the plane's data-supply rate by ~1.5×, confirming a bigger (and more expensive) unit would be wasted silicon: the plane, not the FP-Dot, is the bottleneck.
💡
This is a classic roofline-style bandwidth-matching argument — familiar from PIM/CXL compute-near-memory sizing — applied to a single Flash plane instead of a DRAM bank.

③ Where "74.6% area efficiency" comes from

Table I gives plane area (1.702mm²) and per-plane peripheral area that stays on the array die (0.579mm², e.g. row decoders). Derive the area efficiency and total flash-layer area.

  1. Area per plane on the array die: 1.702 + 0.579 = 2.281 mm².
  2. Area efficiency = useful array area ÷ total area = 1.702 ÷ 2.281 = 74.6% — matches Table I exactly.
  3. Total flash-layer area (32 planes): 32 × 2.281 = 72.99 mm² — again matches Table I's reported total.
  4. Compare to XL-Flash's ~55%: without hybrid bonding, page buffers and PHY would also sit on this die, inflating the non-array fraction well beyond 0.579mm²/plane.
🍯
Insight: hybrid bonding doesn't make the array die 100% efficient — row decoders still cost real area — but it removes the larger peripheral costs (page buffers, PHY) that dominate a planar design.

④ Does Lincoln actually hit the real-time targets?

Using Table II's absolute latencies against the paper's targets (4.0s/5.0s prefill, 0.2s generation), which configurations pass?

  1. Hardest case, LAMDA-137B prefill: Lincoln-2 = 3.772s (5.7% under the 4.0s target ✓); Lincoln-4 = 2.667s (comfortably under ✓).
  2. Hardest case, LAMDA-137B generation: Lincoln-2 = 0.264s — 32% over the 0.2s target (✗, misses); Lincoln-4 = 0.142s — 29% under the target (✓, passes).
  3. Every other model/config (OPT-66B, LLaMA-65B, both prefill and generation) clears its target with margin, on both Lincoln-2 and Lincoln-4.
  4. Conclusion, matching the paper's own wording: Lincoln-2 almost meets every real-time target (one narrow miss on the largest model's generation phase); Lincoln-4 fully meets all of them.
💡
This is a direct tail-latency-vs-SLO check — the same shape of argument used for p99/p90 latency targets in serving systems, just applied to per-token generation latency here.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
Why doesn't Lincoln simply add more transmission pins to the conventional Flash package to fix the interface bottleneck?
Show answer
Because the paper explicitly rules this out: adding pins for NAND Flash would require large PHY area increases on the NPU SoC itself, risking violation of the tight area/cost budgets of consumer devices. Reusing the existing LPDDR interface — which already has abundant pins and bandwidth — avoids that cost entirely.Misconception: that "more pins" is a free fix. Pins cost SoC-side area and cost money; Lincoln's insight is to reuse capacity that's already paid for.
Problem 2 · medium
A single Flash plane delivers 1.06GB/s. How much internal bandwidth does one Lincoln die (32 planes) provide, and does that match the paper's stated figure?
Show answer
1.06 GB/s × 32 planes = 33.92 GB/s, matching the paper's "~34GB/s per die." Scaling to 16 dies gives ~543GB/s, matching the paper's "~500GB/s" (rounded down for real-world overheads).Misconception: that per-die bandwidth requires a separate measurement. It's just per-plane bandwidth multiplied by plane count — the parallelism is the bandwidth.
Problem 3 · medium
Why can't controller-side ECC simply run "on demand" while the NPU streams data over LPDDR, the way DRAM ECC does?
Show answer
Flash ECC is performed chunk-wise (1KB/chunk) with long latency (µs), while LPDDR accesses are small (32/64B) and fast (~100ns). Correcting on demand would force the NPU to stall on µs-scale ECC for every small LPDDR access — a drastic mismatch. So ECC must be done for an entire partition before computation starts, which is why Lincoln moves it near-Flash and hides it inside the prefetch instead.Misconception: that ECC granularity doesn't matter as long as it's "fast enough on average." The mismatch is in timing granularity, not raw speed — a slow, chunky operation can't be interleaved with a fast, tiny one.
Problem 4 · hard
Why can't Lincoln's near-Flash logic simply handle the prefill phase too, avoiding the need for the LPDDR path entirely?
Show answer
Near-Flash logic is deliberately minimal (e.g., a 2-element FP-Dot unit per plane) — sized to just match a single plane's ~1.06GB/s data-supply rate, not to do heavy compute. Prefill's GEMM over the whole prompt is compute-intensive and needs the NPU's much larger tensor-core throughput; near-Flash logic is far too weak to meet that demand (the paper notes CXL-PNM-style near-memory-only designs can't even meet 5s prefill latency for this reason). So prefill still needs the NPU, reached via the fast LPDDR path.Misconception: that "more internal bandwidth" always beats "more compute." Prefill's bottleneck is arithmetic throughput, not bandwidth — near-Flash logic solves the wrong problem for this phase.
Problem 5 · medium
The paper notes an optimistic 10K-read-cycle disturb threshold could be exceeded within 2 hours of continuous long-output generation. Why does read reclaim (extra P/E cycles) fix this without hurting performance much?
Show answer
SLC Flash can endure up to 100K P/E cycles (boosted to 4M by shortening the retention target from 1 year to 3 days), while read disturb only accumulates from reads, not writes — so most of the P/E budget sits unused by normal operation. Reprogramming (a P/E cycle) resets the accumulated read-disturb error. At a realistic 5-year-warranty, 5K-read-threshold estimate, total consumption is only ~310K P/E cycles — far under the 4M budget — and spreading reclaim across iterations (e.g. 1/5000th of the model per iteration) keeps the performance cost near 0.4%.Misconception: that frequent reads and frequent writes draw from the same wear budget equally. Read disturb and P/E wear-out are separate mechanisms; reclaim deliberately trades a little of the (mostly unused) write budget to fix a read-only problem.
Problem 6 · medium
Lincoln-4's prefill speedup over Base-4 (13.23×) is barely higher than Lincoln-2's over Base-2 (11.44×), even though Lincoln-4 has 2× the packages and bandwidth. Why?
Show answer
Once weight loading is no longer the bottleneck (which LPDDR mostly achieves), prefill execution time becomes limited by NPU compute throughput, not by how fast Flash can supply data. Doubling bandwidth doesn't help once you're compute-bound — the paper notes this explicitly, observing Lincoln-4's relative speedup over Lincoln-2 is much lower than its bandwidth increase.Misconception: that more bandwidth always yields proportional speedup. Once a system crosses from memory-bound to compute-bound, extra bandwidth stops mattering — the classic roofline-model crossover.
Problem 7 · easy
Why does Lincoln keep only one physical copy of each weight matrix instead of laying it out separately for NPU access and near-Flash access?
Show answer
Capacity is scarce: with only ~265GB of Flash (2 packages), a duplicate copy would prevent Lincoln from serving models larger than ~71B. Instead, a single round-robin row distribution (across dies, then planes) serves both access patterns — the NPU pulls tiles across dies in parallel via LPDDR, while near-Flash logic processes rows in place, row by row.Misconception: that different compute engines always need differently-optimized data layouts. One well-chosen layout can serve two very different access patterns if it's designed for both from the start.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Ten questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What exactly is the "bottleneck" Lincoln fixes?▾
Two of them: (1) Flash's narrow external interface (~10GB/s over PCIe/UFS) versus LPDDR's >100GB/s, and (2) Flash's slow internal array bandwidth (~41GB/s even in an idealized conventional design). Fixing only one still leaves the other dominating LLM execution time.
Q2Why does prefill need a different fix than generation?▾
Prefill is compute-intensive (GEMM over the whole prompt) and needs the NPU's tensor-core throughput, so it needs a fast path to the NPU — LPDDR. Generation is memory-intensive (GEMV, one token at a time) and near-Flash logic's modest compute is enough, so it's cheaper to compute where the data is and skip moving weights over the interface.
Q3Isn't putting Flash on the LPDDR interface risky — won't it slow down normal DRAM access?▾
Lincoln reuses existing channel pins by treating Flash dies as an extra rank. DRAM space can still be managed and accessed exactly as before, and address mapping keeps DRAM and Flash address spaces cleanly separated (the rank bit sits at the highest address bit), so normal OS paging sees no added complexity — though since Flash and DRAM ranks share the same physical LPDDR channels, concurrent Flash traffic can still contend for channel bandwidth with DRAM accesses.
Q4How does the Flash-side buffer avoid violating LPDDR's strict timing?▾
A near-PHY SRAM buffer stages data already fetched (and ECC-corrected) from the Flash array, so LPDDR reads are served from fast SRAM, not directly from the slow array. Combined with double-buffered prefetch, the actual Flash read latency is hidden entirely behind NPU compute.
Q5Why is near-Flash compute "too weak" for prefill but fine for generation?▾
FP-Dot units are sized to match a single plane's modest ~1.06GB/s data-supply rate (a 2-element dot product at 400MHz is already faster than the plane can feed it) — intentionally minimal to keep area/power overhead low (near-Flash compute + ECC logic is 7.98% of package area). That's enough for GEMV's light arithmetic-per-byte, but far short of GEMM's compute demands.
Q6What does speculative decoding actually buy Lincoln?▾
It lets one near-Flash pass over the target model's weights verify several draft-predicted tokens at once, instead of one weight-pass per token. For >100B models, this is necessary — near-Flash compute alone can take ~0.4s per iteration, well past the 0.2s real-time target, but batched verification of ~7 draft tokens amortizes that cost across multiple accepted tokens.
Q7How is read disturb kept from wearing out the Flash?▾
Periodic read reclaim — reprogramming cells via P/E cycles, similar in spirit to DRAM refresh — resets accumulated read-disturb error. Shortening the retention-time target from 1 year to 3 days boosts the usable P/E budget from 100K to 4M cycles, and even continuous 5-year generation only consumes ~310K of that budget.
Q8Is Lincoln's cost actually competitive?▾
The paper estimates $0.72–$1.44/GB depending on yield assumptions, or $191–$382 for a 2-package, 5-year-warranted system — a small fraction of a $3000 36GB-DRAM MacBook, or 5 years of $20/month cloud-LLM access ($1200). It's also far cheaper than reaching equivalent internal bandwidth with 200–500 conventional Flash dies ($1500–$5000).
Q9How does Lincoln relate to OS-managed DRAM/NVM tiering (TPP, HeMem, Memtis)?▾
Those systems dynamically migrate pages between tiers based on runtime-observed hotness — an online classification problem. Lincoln sidesteps that entirely: because LLM inference's access pattern is known in advance, FC-layer weights are always placed in Flash and activations/KV cache/other weights are always in DRAM, as a fixed, workload-aware partition rather than a page-migration policy.
Q10Why not just do the whole inference near-memory, like CXL-PNM?▾
The paper directly contrasts with LPDDR-based CXL-PNM designs: those need extra DRAM capacity (e.g. 8 packages / 64 channels / 256 dies) infeasible for edge devices, and their near-memory unit is used for the entire inference. Lincoln's near-Flash logic is far weaker (it can't meet real-time prefill, and Flash endurance issues make it unsuitable for attention/KV-cache access), so Lincoln keeps the central NPU for compute-heavy work and attention, using near-Flash logic only for the one job — GEMV — it's actually sized for.
