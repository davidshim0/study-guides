HotStorage 2026 · A Field Guide

A satellite leaves in 4.5 minutes — your state has to leave with it

432 times a day, a stateful LEO application hands off to the next satellite, and under checkpoint-style handover its mutable state typically has to hit flash to survive the move. This is the plain-English story of why that write stream can outlast the hardware — and the three-way trade the paper proposes instead.

📄 Bhosale, Gavrilovska, Bhardwaj · Georgia Tech 🛰️ Orbital-mechanics basics assumed — the primers cover them ⏱ ~25 min read
💾

Keep state LOCAL for low latency, or make it DURABLE across the move — demand both, every few minutes, and you spend down a drive's lifetime budget nobody can refill in orbit.
Pick two of latency, durability, wear. The third is the price.

Before you begin

Quick glossary, then nine ideas the paper assumes you know

Skim the acronyms first — they recur constantly. Then open whichever primer is unfamiliar; skip the ones that are second nature from your own tiering work.

Quick glossary — key acronyms

LEOLow Earth Orbit, 200–1600 km altitude — where these compute-carrying satellites fly, visible from a ground point for at most ~12 minutes, usably linked for ~4.5.
ISLInter-Satellite Link — radio/optical link between satellites in the same orbital shell, used instead of routing through a ground station.
SSD / NANDSolid-state drive built from NAND flash cells, each of which tolerates only a bounded number of program/erase (P/E) cycles before it wears out.
QLC / TLC / SLCQuad-/Triple-/Single-level cell flash — more bits per cell means more capacity but far fewer P/E cycles (QLC ≈10³, TLC ≈3×10³, SLC ≈10⁵).
TBW / DWPDTerabytes Written / Drive-Writes-Per-Day — the vendor's endurance rating, in host bytes, over the drive's warranty period.
Host bytesBytes the application actually asks the drive to write, as opposed to the (larger) bytes the flash controller physically writes after internal write amplification.
KV-cacheThe growing key-value cache an LLM builds per conversation during decoding — application state that can reach gigabytes for one session.
CRIU-style checkpointCheckpoint/restore tooling that serializes a running process's state to disk so another instance can resume it elsewhere.
LDWLatency–Durability–Wear — this paper's name for the three-way tension a handover decision creates; its "trilemma."
HandoverThe hand-off of a serving application from the departing satellite to the next one covering the same ground region, ~432 times/day.
1Why a LEO satellite can't just stay put▾

LEO satellites orbit at ~27,000 km/h, so no single satellite stays above a fixed point on Earth. A given satellite is visible from a ground region for at most ~12 minutes, but only usably linked (good elevation angle) for up to 4.5 minutes.

An application that must keep serving that region therefore can't live on one satellite — it has to hand off to the next satellite covering the region as the current one departs, the same way a cell-phone call hands off between towers.

Now you're ready: this paper's whole argument starts from that hand-off being frequent and mandatory, not optional.
2A handover, concretely: 432 times a day▾

Prior work (the authors' own Krios system) measures a median session length of 200 seconds per satellite before handover. 86,400 seconds in a day ÷ 200 ≈ 432 handovers/day for one continuously-served application.

Orchestration systems hide most of this cost — they hand the application off transparently so users never notice the satellite underneath has changed. What they don't eliminate is the data the application needs at the new location.

Now you're ready: 432/day is the H term you'll see throughout — the frequency at which the paper's wear equation gets invoked.
3Stateful vs. stateless applications in orbit▾

A stateless or read-mostly application (e.g. serving static imagery) can just re-fetch or recompute what it needs at the new satellite. A stateful application — an in-orbit sensor aggregator, a session store, an LLM mid-conversation — holds mutable data that exists only on the departing satellite.

A fresh satellite holds no prior copy of that state. So for stateful workloads, the handover can't just be a compute/network event; the state itself must physically move.

Now you're ready: this is the paper's central reframing — "handover is also a storage event," not just orchestration.
4NAND flash endurance: P/E cycles▾

A NAND flash cell must be erased before it can be rewritten, and each program/erase (P/E) cycle slightly degrades the cell's ability to hold charge. After a bounded number of cycles — denoted P — the cell becomes unreliable.

Denser cells store more bits but tolerate far fewer cycles: roughly 10⁵ for SLC, ~3×10³ for TLC, and down to ~10³ for capacity-oriented QLC, the kind a cost-conscious constellation favors.

Now you're ready: this is the P in the paper's lifetime equation — a fixed property of the flash part, not something software controls.
5TBW / DWPD — how vendors budget endurance▾

Multiply a drive's capacity C by its rated cycles P and you get a lifetime write budget E = C·P, measured in host bytes. Vendors publish this as TBW (total terabytes written) or DWPD (drive-writes-per-day) over the warranty period.

A capacity-oriented QLC data-center SSD is typically rated around 0.6 DWPD over five years — meaning the vendor expects well under one full-drive rewrite per day, on average, for the whole warranty.

Now you're ready: the paper measures a LEO workload's actual write rate against this budget — and finds it several times over.
6Write inflation: why one checkpoint isn't one write▾

A handover's checkpoint touches flash twice over, for two independent reasons. First, the state is written once at the departing satellite (the checkpoint) and once at the arriving satellite (on receipt) — a factor of 2. Second, with data journaling on (common for crash-consistency), the filesystem writes each byte twice — once to the journal, once to its final location — another factor of 2.

Multiplied together, that's a write-inflation factor α = 4: every byte of logical application state costs four bytes of host-side writes to the drive. The model is deliberately scoped to host bytes and excludes SSD-internal garbage collection/write amplification — vendor TBW/DWPD ratings already bake that in.

Now you're ready: you'll see α multiply directly into the daily write-rate calculation in the next tab.
7KV-cache: why LLM session state can be large▾

When an LLM serves a conversation, it builds up a key-value (KV) cache in memory — one entry per token, per attention layer — so it doesn't have to recompute attention over the whole conversation at every new token. The longer the conversation, the bigger this cache grows.

For an 8,000-token session on a 70-billion-parameter model with grouped-query attention, stored at half precision, this works out to roughly 2.6 GB — the paper's running example of "large, mutable, session-unique" state.

Now you're ready: this is the kind of state that must be checkpointed at handover — it can't be cheaply refetched, only rebuilt by re-running prefill from scratch.
8Why you can't just service a drive in orbit▾

Modern LEO constellations deploy satellites on roughly a five-year operating horizon. Once launched, a satellite's hardware — including its SSD — cannot be swapped, upgraded, or serviced. Whatever endurance budget ships with the drive is the budget for the mission.

Radiation-tolerant or radiation-hardened flash parts survive the space environment better, but cost several to a hundred times more than commodity parts and pack less densely — so cost-conscious constellations lean on ordinary commodity QLC/TLC flash instead.

Now you're ready: "exhausts endurance in months" is not a recoverable failure mode here — it's closer to the drive simply dying mid-mission.
9Why terrestrial tricks (delta encoding, live migration) don't just transfer▾

On the ground, you cut migration cost by diffing against a base image already at the destination, or by streaming memory pages live over a fast, stable link. Both assumptions break in orbit: each handover targets a different satellite holding no prior copy, and the link is short, variable, and often worse than a stable terrestrial connection.

So the paper treats "shrink what's written and where it goes" as an open systems problem, not a solved one — which is what the Research Agenda tab is about.

Now you're ready: keep this primer in mind for the "Analogies" tab — several of the pictures there are about exactly this gap.
The whole story in one place

LDW in a nutshell

The reframing, the arithmetic behind it, and the trilemma it leaves you holding.

A LEO satellite is overhead for only a few minutes, so a stateful application serving a fixed region hands off to a new satellite 432 times a day. Prior work treats this hand-off as an orchestration problem — masking the motion so users never notice. This paper's reframing: for a stateful application, the hand-off is also a storage event, because the application's state has to be written to the next satellite's flash before it can resume serving there, and a fresh satellite holds no prior copy to diff against.

Run the arithmetic and the problem is immediate. A 1 TB commodity QLC drive (endurance budget E = C·P ≈ 1 PB) absorbing 432 handovers/day of 2 GB state, with a write-inflation factor α = 4 from double-write-at-both-ends plus journaling, takes on roughly 3.4 TB of writes per day — more than three drive-writes-per-day against a QLC part rated for about 0.6 DWPD. The projected result: the drive's endurance is exhausted in about ten months, far short of a constellation's five-year design life, on a part that cannot be serviced once launched.

A more durable drive (TLC, SLC) raises the endurance budget in direct proportion to its cycle rating, but costs correspondingly more and still doesn't erase the underlying tension: keeping state local for latency and durable across the move both push writes onto the same flash budget. The paper names this three-way tension LDW — Latency, Durability, Wear — and argues a stateful handover design can satisfy only two of the three at once.

🛰️ Key takeaways

  • A LEO handover isn't just orchestration — for stateful apps with mutable state, checkpoint-style handover typically means a flash write, ~432 times/day (memory-to-memory transfer, or state that's refetchable/recomputable, can avoid it).
  • Lifetime model: L = C·P / (H·S·α) — drive capacity × cycle rating, divided by handover rate × state size × write inflation.
  • At S = 2 GB/handover, a 1 TB QLC drive is projected to last ~10 months — far short of a 5-year constellation lifetime.
  • A higher-endurance drive (TLC, SLC) buys proportionally more lifetime, at proportionally higher cost — it doesn't remove the tension.
  • None of H, S, or α is a property of the drive — they're set by orbit and workload, which is where a storage-systems fix has to live.
  • The LDW trilemma: local persistence sacrifices wear, remote persistence sacrifices latency, memory-only sacrifices durability. Pick two.
  • The paper proposes a research agenda — not a single system — for designs that trade partial amounts of all three instead of sacrificing one outright.
💡
Why this matters for tiered memory, not just orbit: LDW is the same shape of trade-off OS-managed DRAM/NVM tiering already navigates — keep hot data local for latency, make it durable against loss, spend write endurance doing both. Here the "migration" is forced by orbital mechanics instead of a tiering policy, and the endurance budget is fixed for five years with no way to replace the device — which is a harsher version of the NVM wear-leveling constraint, not a different one.
Seven everyday pictures

How to think about LDW

Each picture stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the picture stops being true.

The storage event · Handover as checkpoint

A relay race with a photocopier

A normal relay just passes the baton. This relay stops at every exchange so the whole race history can be photocopied onto the baton before it's handed off — because the next runner has never seen any of it before. That copy is the checkpoint write; it happens at every single exchange, not occasionally.

Where it breaks: a real baton doesn't carry gigabytes. The size of "the race history" (application state) is exactly the knob that determines how expensive each exchange is — small for a sensor log, huge for a growing LLM conversation.
Endurance budget · P/E cycles

A gift card with no reload

A SSD's write endurance is a one-time balance — E = C·P bytes — loaded once at launch, never topped up, because the drive can't be serviced in orbit. Every handover checkpoint spends from that same fixed card. Spend faster than the five-year mission needs, and the card is empty long before the mission ends.

Where it breaks: a gift card just stops working at zero; a dead SSD can take an entire stateful application down with it, since there's no fallback drive to switch to mid-orbit.
Write inflation · α = 4

Shipping one package, paying four shipping labels

One logical item (the application state) ends up costing four physical writes: one when it's checkpointed at the old address, one when it's written on arrival at the new address, and the filesystem's journal doubles each of those. The item never changed size — the handling quadrupled it.

Where it breaks: real shipping labels are flat-fee; α = 4 is a fixed multiplier the paper measures for this specific journaling + double-write setup — a different filesystem or a memory-to-memory transfer would change it.
The trilemma itself · LDW

"Fast, safe, cheap — pick two"

The classic project-management trade-off maps almost exactly: fast is latency (state stays put), safe is durability (state survives the move), and cheap is the SSD's lifetime (wear spared, so the hardware isn't the bottleneck). Every pure handover strategy sits on one edge of that triangle, deliberately giving up the opposite corner.

Where it breaks: "fast, safe, cheap" is usually resolved by a one-time project decision; LDW is re-decided at every single handover, hundreds of times a day, so the "cost" of the choice compounds in a way a one-off trade-off doesn't.
Reducing S · Hot/cold split & bounded logs

Repacking the whole house every move

A family that moves every few minutes packs the entire house each time, instead of keeping a go-bag of what's actually used daily and slow-shipping the rest. Shrinking a handover's write means the same thing: identify the small hot/active slice of state and move only that, deferring or logging the rest.

Where it breaks: a go-bag works because daily needs are predictable. A workload whose access pattern is uniform across its whole state (not skewed hot/cold) gets little benefit from splitting — there's no "rarely used" fraction to leave behind.
Reducing S · Base-plus-log

A textbook plus sticky notes, not a new book

If your textbook barely changes but you annotate it daily, you don't reprint the whole book each night — you keep sticky notes (the log) against a base that's refreshed only occasionally. A handover built the same way ships only the log against a base the destination already has, instead of the full state every time.

Where it breaks: the base has to be present and current on every satellite the application might land on next — at least 475 of them, per the paper's own prior measurement — so the "savings" only pay off if the base changes rarely enough to amortize staging that many copies.
A prior-approach pattern · Replicate-everywhere (distinct from the paper's "remote persistence" corner)

Pre-mailing your letter to 475 possible addresses

To guarantee a letter is already waiting wherever you might move next, you could mail a copy to every address you might ever live at. That only makes sense if you move rarely — the paper's own prior system keeps an application resident on at least 475 satellites, which pays off only if the state changes less than about once a day, since every update then costs 475 writes against a mere 432 handovers/day on the direct path.

Where it breaks: for state that updates every handover (session stores, growing KV-caches), 475 copies to maintain is strictly worse than the direct handover path — this corner only wins for slow-changing, read-mostly state.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Section by section, with the paper's key figures rebuilt as visuals you can read at a glance.

1 · The handover is a storage event

As a satellite moves out of range of a region, it checkpoints the application's in-memory state to its own flash, then transmits it toward the next satellite. That satellite writes it on arrival, then restores it into memory before it can resume serving the same users. Both the checkpoint write and the arrival write hit flash — and this whole cycle repeats at every one of the ~432 handovers a day.

2 · The wear model (Equation 1)

A drive of capacity C whose cells tolerate P program/erase cycles can absorb E = C·P host bytes before wearing out. Each handover writes application state of size S, inflated by a factor α, at a rate of H handovers/day — so the drive absorbs W = H·S·α bytes/day. Lifetime is simply the budget divided by the burn rate:

L = E / W = (C · P) / (H · S · α)     # lifetime, in days
SymbolMeaningSet by
C, Pdrive capacity, rated P/E cyclesthe drive you buy
Hhandovers per day (≈432)orbit + session length
Sapplication state written per handoverthe workload
αwrite-inflation factor (≈4: double-write + journaling)the handover mechanism
⚠
None of the three denominator terms is a drive property. A bigger, more durable drive scales E up proportionally — but H, S, and α are untouched by that purchase. That's exactly why the paper calls this a storage-systems design problem, not a hardware shopping problem.

3 · Sensitivity: duty cycle and drive sizing

The handover rate H is really an upper bound: a satellite that carries the application's load for only a fraction ρ of the day (because, say, load is shared across a shell) writes at ρH instead, and lifetime scales as 1/ρ. At ρ = 1/3, the paper projects the QLC drive to last about 2.4 years — longer, but still short of five. Scaling C or P up (bigger drive, higher-endurance part, more drives per satellite) buys endurance directly, but each option spends capacity, power, or launch mass the mission needs elsewhere — there's no free lever here.

4 · The LDW trilemma, operationalized

Three pure strategies sit at the trilemma's corners, and existing LEO cloud systems map onto them directly: offloading state to the ground is remote persistence (durable, spares the drive, pays in latency over an intermittent downlink); checkpointing to the serving satellite — the authors' own prior system, Krios — is local persistence (fast, durable, spends the endurance budget this paper measures); never persisting is memory-only (fast, spares the drive, loses everything on a fault or handover). Terrestrial flash-management techniques don't transfer cleanly either, since they're built for a stable workload on a fixed drive, not full-state rewrites every few minutes.

5 · Research agenda: trading partial amounts of all three

Rather than a single new system, the paper sketches four directions, each shrinking what lands on the local drive and pushing cost onto the network instead:

Bounded log shipping

Ship a bounded operation log, replay it at the destination. Fits key-value/session stores; doesn't fit large derived state like a KV-cache, and un-replayed ops are lost on source failure.

Policy-triggered flushing

Transfer memory-to-memory by default; write to flash only when the link is lossy, the state is too large to transfer live, or no ISL path reaches the destination.

Hot/cold split

Keep the hot working set local and volatile; replicate the cold remainder to the ground. Suits skewed access (a content cache); a cold miss costs a contact-window wait.

Base-plus-log handover

Refresh a base image slowly (e.g. once per orbit); ship only a small log each handover. Requires staging the base on all ≥475 satellites the app may land on.

💡
What to measure: the paper proposes a (wear, latency, window) triple per strategy — wear as bytes written to the local drive/time, latency as added time-to-serve, and "window" as the unpersisted state at risk if a fault hits. It proposes bytes written avoided against a local-persistence baseline as the wear coordinate, computable from write counters systems already keep.
Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Five traces, each carried through with the paper's own numbers: the QLC baseline, the endurance-class comparison, duty-cycle sensitivity, an LLM KV-cache session, and the replicate-everywhere break-even.

① Deriving the QLC drive's ~10-month lifetime from scratch

1 TB QLC drive (P ≈ 10³), H = 432 handovers/day, S = 2 GB/handover, α = 4.

  1. Endurance budget: E = C·P = (1×10¹² bytes) × 10³ = 1×10¹⁵ bytes = 1 PB.
  2. Daily write rate: W = H·S·α = 432 × 2 GB × 4 = 3,456 GB/day ≈ 3.4 TB/day.
  3. As drive-writes-per-day: 3.4 TB ÷ 1 TB capacity ≈ 3.4 DWPD — more than five times the ~0.6 DWPD a capacity-oriented QLC part is rated for.
  4. Lifetime: L = E / W = 1×10¹⁵ ÷ 3.456×10¹² ≈ 289 days ≈ 9.5 months — the paper's "about ten months."
  5. Against the mission: a 5-year constellation needs ~1,825 days of lifetime. 289 days covers under 16% of that.
🛰️
Insight: the handover's write isn't exotic — it's the same kind of write a terrestrial system makes all the time. What's exotic is doing it 432 times a day against a fixed, unserviceable budget.

② TLC and SLC: more durable, not durable enough (or cheap enough)

Same W = 3.4 TB/day. Swap only P.

  1. TLC (P ≈ 3×10³): E = 3×10¹⁵ bytes. L = 3×10¹⁵ ÷ 3.456×10¹² ≈ 868 days ≈ 2.4 years — "a little over two years," still short of five.
  2. SLC (P ≈ 10⁵): E = 1×10¹⁷ bytes. L = 1×10¹⁷ ÷ 3.456×10¹² ≈ 28,935 days ≈ 79 years — comfortably clears five years at this S, but at a much higher per-byte cost and lower density.
  3. The crossover with S: solving L = 5 years for S at each class gives the threshold where that class just clears the design life: QLC ≈ 0.3 GB/handover, TLC ≈ 1 GB/handover, SLC ≈ 30 GB/handover — matching the paper's Figure 2 crossing points.
💡
Endurance budget scales directly with the cycle rating P, and higher-endurance flash (TLC, SLC) costs more per byte — but the paper doesn't quantify a proportional cost relationship, just that there's no endurance class here that's both cheap and clears five years at realistic state sizes. That's the "not cheaply" in the paper's claim about buying a better drive.

③ Duty-cycle sensitivity: when one satellite doesn't carry the full load

If a satellite only hosts the application for a fraction ρ of the day (load shared across a shell), it writes at ρH instead of H.

  1. At ρ = 1: QLC lifetime is the baseline ≈ 9.5 months (from ①).
  2. At ρ = 1/3: the write rate drops to a third, so lifetime scales by 1/ρ = 3×: ≈ 9.5 × 3 ≈ 28.5 months ≈ 2.4 years — the paper's reported figure.
  3. Still short of five years — duty-cycling alone buys time, not survival, for QLC at this S.
🛰️
This is the orbital analogue of spreading writes across more devices to extend aggregate endurance — the same lever as striping writes across a larger tiered-memory pool rather than hammering one NVM bank.

④ An LLM session's KV-cache, run through the same model

An 8k-token session on a 70B-parameter model with GQA, half precision, needs ≈ 2.6 GB of KV-cache (paper's Table 1 example). Apply Eq. 1 to this state size directly, holding H = 432, α = 4, 1 TB QLC drive.

  1. Daily write rate: W = 432 × 2.6 GB × 4 = 4,493 GB/day ≈ 4.5 TB/day — about 1.3× the paper's 2 GB baseline rate.
  2. Lifetime: L = 1×10¹⁵ ÷ 4.493×10¹² ≈ 222 days ≈ 7.3 months — a single conversation-length session checkpointed this way wears the drive out even faster than the paper's baseline example.
  3. Why it's worse than it looks: this is one session on one satellite; a satellite serving multiple concurrent LLM sessions multiplies S directly (S = N·s for N residents), shrinking lifetime further in direct proportion to N.
⚠
This extends the paper's own example (not a number stated verbatim in the text) by plugging its Table 1 KV-cache figure into its Equation 1 — it's the kind of calculation the paper's model is built to support.

⑤ When does "replicate to 475 satellites" actually pay off?

The replicate-everywhere corner keeps a copy on ≥475 satellites the application might land on. Every update to that state costs 475 writes (one per copy); the direct handover path costs ~432 writes/day regardless of update frequency.

  1. Break-even condition: replicate-everywhere wins when (updates/day) × 475 < 432, i.e. updates/day < 432/475 ≈ 0.91.
  2. In words: the state must change less than about once a day for the 475-copy approach to cost fewer total writes than paying the handover path every time.
  3. Where it lands: matches the paper's own statement exactly — this corner suits slow-changing, read-mostly state, and is a bad fit for anything (like a growing KV-cache) that mutates every handover.
🛰️
Insight: "475 copies" isn't free redundancy — it's a standing write tax that only beats the direct path for state update rates under roughly once per day.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the common misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
A 2 TB TLC drive (P ≈ 3×10³) serves a workload with S = 5 GB/handover, H = 432, α = 4. Project its lifetime in years.
Show answer
E = C·P = 2×10¹² × 3×10³ = 6×10¹⁵ bytes. W = 432 × 5×10⁹ × 4 = 8.64×10¹² bytes/day. L = 6×10¹⁵ ÷ 8.64×10¹² ≈ 694 days ≈ 1.9 years — short of a 5-year design life.Misconception: that doubling capacity (1 TB → 2 TB) doubles lifetime on its own. It does scale E linearly, but if S also changes the two effects compound — always recompute W and E together.
Problem 2 · medium
Why is remote persistence the one corner of LDW that doesn't resolve to a single fixed (wear, latency) point, unlike local persistence or memory-only?
Show answer
Local persistence always writes H·S·α bytes/day and risks nothing (fixed point). Memory-only always writes 0 bytes and risks the whole working set (fixed point). Remote persistence's writes are small and roughly fixed, but its latency depends on where the satellites actually are — it rides an ISL path or a ground-station contact window whose quality and availability vary with the orbit at that moment, so the achievable latency isn't a single number.Misconception: that "offload to the ground" is a uniformly cheap fix. Its cost is network-path-dependent and therefore variable, not free or fixed.
Problem 3 · easy
Where does the write-inflation factor α = 4 actually come from? Name both contributing effects.
Show answer
(1) The checkpoint is written once at the departing satellite and once more at the arriving satellite on receipt — ×2. (2) Data journaling writes each byte twice, once to the journal and once to its final location — another ×2. Together: 2 × 2 = α = 4.Misconception: that α is a generic "overhead fudge factor." It's two specific, named mechanisms the paper accounts for explicitly — and it excludes SSD-internal garbage collection, which TBW/DWPD ratings already account for separately.
Problem 4 · hard
A colleague argues: "Just apply terrestrial live migration — stream memory pages directly between satellites and skip the flash write entirely." Live migration and delta encoding each depend on a different ground-side assumption — name both and explain why neither holds cleanly in LEO.
Show answer
Live migration assumes a fast, stable link that stays up long enough to stream memory pages across before the state goes stale — it doesn't itself require a prior copy at the destination. Delta encoding relies on a separate assumption: a base image already staged at the destination to diff against. Neither holds cleanly in LEO: the ISL/downlink contact window is short (≤4.5 min) and variable in throughput, so a live transfer that takes too long can't complete before the window closes; and each handover generally targets a different satellite with no base image staged there, so there's nothing to diff against. Both failure modes push the system back toward checkpoint/restore.Misconception: that avoiding the drive write is just an engineering substitution. It requires conditions (a stable enough link for migration, a staged base image for delta encoding) that the orbital setting frequently doesn't provide — which is why "policy-triggered flushing" in the research agenda treats memory-to-memory transfer as conditional, not default.
Problem 5 · medium
If S doubles (e.g. a workload's session state grows from 2 GB to 4 GB), how does projected drive lifetime change, holding everything else fixed? Is the relationship linear?
Show answer
L = C·P / (H·S·α). Since L is inversely proportional to S, doubling S exactly halves L. It is linear in 1/S — e.g. the paper's QLC example goes from ~10 months at S=2GB to ~5 months at S=4GB, all else equal.Misconception: assuming diminishing or amplifying returns on S. The model is a simple inverse proportionality — no saturation, no superlinear penalty — which also means small reductions in S pay off directly and proportionally.
Problem 6 · medium
An Earth-observation satellite writes captured imagery to its drive in long sequential streams, far more data per day than a stateful serving application. Does the paper's wear concern apply to it the same way? Why or why not?
Show answer
No — the paper explicitly distinguishes this case. Earth-observation writes are large but written once, in long sequential streams, with no handover-driven re-write cycle; they consume comparatively little endurance per byte of useful work. The wear concern is specifically about mutable state rewritten at every handover (hundreds of times/day), not about total bytes written in general.Misconception: that "more total data written" automatically means "more wear risk." It's the write frequency and repetition — the same state being rewritten 432 times a day — that drives the endurance problem, not raw volume alone.
Problem 7 · hard
For a sensor-aggregation workload with ~2 kB of state per handover, is LDW actually a binding concern? Use the model to justify your answer.
Show answer
Barely. W = 432 × 2 kB × 4 ≈ 3.5 MB/day — negligible against a 1 TB QLC drive's ~1 PB budget; lifetime would project to many centuries, far past any constellation's deployment horizon. LDW becomes binding only once S grows into the hundreds of MB–GB range, as with session/KV-cache/content-cache workloads — exactly why the paper frames severity as scaling with per-handover state, not as a universal problem for every LEO workload.Misconception: that LDW is a constraint on all stateful LEO applications equally. Its severity is workload-dependent — tiny, infrequently-mutated state is essentially free; large, frequently-mutated state is where the trilemma bites.
Problem 8 · easy
True or false: buying a higher-cycle-rated (TLC or SLC) drive removes the LDW trilemma.
Show answer
False. A higher P raises the endurance budget E = C·P proportionally, extending lifetime at a fixed write rate — but the write rate itself (H·S·α) is set by orbit and workload, untouched by the drive choice. The trilemma — that a single handover decision sets latency, durability, and wear together — still holds; a better drive just moves where the wear axis becomes binding, at a real cost premium.Misconception: treating "buy better hardware" as a systems fix. The paper is explicit that this is a storage-systems design problem, not a hardware-shopping one.

Frequently asked questions

Eleven questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What exactly counts as "a handover" in this model?▾
One transition of a continuously-served application from its current satellite to the next satellite covering the same ground region — occurring whenever the current satellite's usable link window (≤4.5 min) closes. The paper's reference rate, from prior measurement, is 432 such events per day for one application.
Q2Why does the paper insist a higher-endurance drive doesn't "solve" the problem?▾
It raises the endurance budget E = C·P in direct proportion to the cycle rating P, which does extend lifetime — but the daily write rate W = H·S·α is set entirely by orbit and workload, not the drive. A part durable enough to clear a five-year mission at realistic state sizes (TLC/SLC) costs significantly more and is less dense, so it trades the wear problem for a cost/capacity problem instead of eliminating the tension.
Q3Does LDW apply to read-mostly workloads like imaging or CDN caching?▾
Much less. Read-mostly content can often be refetched at the destination on a miss, trading the write for latency rather than paying it unconditionally; Earth-observation imagery is written once in long sequential streams rather than rewritten at handover cadence. The binding case is mutable state unique to a session — sensor aggregators, session/KV stores — that must move at every handover with no cheaper alternative.
Q4Why can't delta encoding (shipping only the changed bytes) fix this the way it does on the ground?▾
Delta encoding needs a base image already present at the destination to diff against. Each handover targets a different satellite that, in general, holds no prior copy of this application's state — so there's nothing to diff against unless a base image has been deliberately staged there in advance (the "base-plus-log" research direction).
Q5What's the difference between the "P" in the wear model and TBW/DWPD ratings?▾
P is the physical program/erase cycle count a flash cell tolerates — the underlying hardware number. TBW and DWPD are vendor-published derived ratings (bytes, or drive-writes-per-day) that already fold P, capacity, and the drive's expected internal write amplification together into one practical budget figure. The paper's model (E = C·P) recovers something in the same units as TBW, built from first principles.
Q6Is the wear risk confined to one or two unlucky satellites?▾
No — the write stream follows the workload, which runs on whichever satellite is currently serving the region. As the serving satellite changes every few minutes, the same wear pattern recurs across every satellite that ever hosts that application, so the projected exhaustion risk extends constellation-wide, not to a single device.
Q7Why not just keep everything in RAM and never write to flash (memory-only)?▾
It's the fastest and least wear-costly corner of LDW — but it has no durability: a handover or any fault loses the application state outright, since nothing survives except what's live in memory on the one satellite currently holding it. It's viable only for state that's cheap to lose or regenerate, which rules out the mutable, session-unique state this paper is about.
Q8Does internal SSD garbage collection make the wear worse than the model suggests?▾
The model deliberately works in host bytes (what the application asks the drive to write) and compares against TBW/DWPD ratings that are also quoted in host bytes and already account for the drive's internal write amplification from garbage collection. So GC isn't double-counted or omitted — it's folded into the rating side of the comparison.
Q9How does this connect to terrestrial DRAM/NVM tiering or CXL memory pooling?▾
Structurally it's the same shape of trade-off: keep hot data close for latency, make placement decisions durable against loss, and both cost write/move overhead on a medium with bounded endurance (NVM wear, or here, flash P/E cycles). The LEO setting sharpens the constraint in two ways a terrestrial tiering system rarely faces simultaneously: the "migration" is forced on a fixed external clock (orbital handover) rather than chosen by a tiering policy, and the device literally cannot be replaced for five years if it wears out.
Q10What would it actually mean to "solve" LDW?▾
Not eliminating the trilemma — the paper argues at most two of the three can be fully satisfied — but building designs that trade partial amounts of all three deliberately, instead of sitting at one of the three pure corners by default. The four sketched directions (bounded log shipping, policy-triggered flushing, hot/cold split, base-plus-log) are examples of such partial trades, each evaluated along (wear, latency, durability window) rather than optimizing one axis alone.
Q11Is this paper proposing a system, or something else?▾
A research agenda, not a built system. Its contributions are the first-principles wear model (§3), the LDW framing with prior approaches placed at its corners (§4), and four sketched, not-yet-validated directions for partial trade-offs (§5) — explicitly offered as open problems for the storage-systems community, not a shipped mechanism.

Glossary & further reading

Every term in one place, then a path to go deeper, then exactly what in this guide is the paper's and what's framing.

Core terms
LDWLatency, Durability, Wear — the paper's name for the three-way tension a handover write decision creates. At most two can be satisfied together.
HandoverThe hand-off of a serving application from a departing to an arriving satellite, ≈432 times/day for a continuously-served application.
Wear model (Eq. 1)L = C·P / (H·S·α) — projected drive lifetime as endurance budget divided by daily write rate.
Write-inflation factor (α)The multiplier (≈4 here) on logical state size from double-write-at-both-ends plus filesystem journaling.
Local persistenceCheckpointing state to the serving satellite's own drive. Fast and durable; spends SSD endurance. One LDW corner.
Remote persistenceWriting state synchronously to a fixed ground or designated-satellite home. Durable and spares the local drive; costs latency. Another LDW corner.
Memory-onlyKeeping state in volatile RAM, never persisted. Fast and spares the drive; loses state on handover or fault. The third LDW corner.
Bytes written avoidedThe proposed wear metric: drive writes a strategy saves relative to a local-persistence baseline on the same workload.
Supporting terms
Host bytesBytes the application issues to the drive, before/excluding internal SSD write amplification — the unit TBW/DWPD ratings use.
P/E cycleProgram/erase cycle — one full rewrite of a NAND flash cell; endurance is a bounded count of these.
TBW / DWPDTerabytes Written / Drive-Writes-Per-Day — vendor endurance ratings over the warranty period, in host bytes.
Inter-satellite link (ISL)Radio/optical link between satellites in the same orbital shell, avoiding a ground-station hop.
KV-cachePer-token, per-layer attention state an LLM accumulates while serving a conversation; grows with session length.
Bounded log shippingShipping a compact operation log instead of full state; replayed at the destination.
Policy-triggered flushingDefault memory-to-memory transfer; write to flash only when policy requires (lossy path, oversized state, no ISL route).
Hot/cold splitKeep the latency-sensitive working set local and volatile; replicate the cold remainder to the ground.
Base-plus-log handoverA slowly-refreshed base image plus a small per-handover log, amortizing the full-state cost over many handovers.
