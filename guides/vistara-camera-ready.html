Before you begin

Quick glossary + the production-specific pieces

You already know CXL, NUMA, and tiered memory generally. What's new here is the production engineering: a real ASIC, real ACPI tables, real cgroups. Glossary first, then nine short primers on the parts specific to Vistara's deployment.

1CXL.io vs CXL.mem, in one breath▾

A CXL Type-3 (memory) device speaks two sub-protocols over the same PCIe Gen5 wire: CXL.io handles enumeration, config-space access, and management commands (like ordinary PCIe); CXL.mem is the load/store memory-semantic path the CPU actually uses to read and write expander DRAM.

Vistara's "CXL2DDR Bridge" is literally the block that terminates CXL.mem and CXL.io and turns them into DDR4 commands.

Now you're ready: when the paper says "≈150ns extra latency comes from the expander datapath," that's CXL.mem traversing this bridge — not a property of DRAM itself.
2NUMA modeling of an expander▾

Linux doesn't get a new "CXL" abstraction — it gets a NUMA node with no CPUs attached. ACPI's CEDT tells the kernel this node exists and how it maps to address ranges; HMAT tells the kernel its relative latency/bandwidth so allocation and migration policy can be tuned per-tier.

Bad ACPI tables are a real failure mode in production — Vistara's team had to validate topology with platform vendors directly.

Now you're ready: "CXL memory as NUMA node 1" is the entire OS-visible abstraction. Everything else — TPP, cgroups, zone_movable — is built on top of that one fact.
3TPP, in one paragraph▾

You know TPP already: minor-fault-driven hotness detection, promotion of hot pages to local DRAM, reclaim-driven demotion of cold pages to the slower tier, tunable via numa_balancing and demotion sysctls. This paper's contribution isn't a new algorithm — it's proving that this exact, already-simple mechanism is enough at hyperscale, with under 0.5% CPU overhead.

Now you're ready: resist the urge to look for a smarter hotness classifier here (contrast with Memtis, HeMem-style approaches) — the paper's point is that plain LRU-ish TPP was already sufficient for their fleet.
4Why ZONE_MOVABLE for CXL▾

Some kernel allocations (slab, page tables) are pinned — they can never migrate. If they land on CXL memory and that memory tier ever needs to be drained or hits a transient error, you're stuck. Onlining CXL memory as ZONE_MOVABLE tells the allocator: only migratable pages may go here.

Now you're ready: this is what lets TPP freely shuttle hot user pages back from CXL without worrying that some immovable kernel structure got stranded there first.
5Opting out with cgroups, not BIOS▾

Some workloads shouldn't touch CXL at all — latency-critical services, or anything not yet validated on tiered memory. Vistara's answer is the cpuset.mems cgroup controller: restrict a workload's allowed NUMA nodes to just local DRAM. No reboot, no BIOS reconfiguration, fully automatable by the resource orchestrator.

Now you're ready: every server in the fleet is uniformly a "1TB machine" — opt-out is a software policy layered on top, not a hardware mode switch. That's what preserves fleet homogeneity.
6Why an ASIC beats an FPGA here▾

Prior work reported unstable, long tail latencies on a CXL device under contention. That device was an FPGA prototype — likely limited by small on-chip SRAM (few outstanding-transaction credit buffers). Vistara is a purpose-built ASIC with deep completion buffers, wide flow-control windows, and sufficient replay depth.

Now you're ready: "CXL has bad tail latency" was likely a claim about one FPGA's buffer sizing, not an inherent property of the CXL protocol — Vistara's CDF tracks local DRAM closely even at 100 concurrent threads.
7Why DRAM reuse is economically hard▾

Most commercial CXL controllers bundle their own DRAM — you can't plug in an old DDR4 DIMM salvaged from a decommissioned server. Vistara was built specifically to accept industry-standard DDR4 RDIMMs of mixed vintage, at the cost of supporting a messy diversity of DIMM vendors/speeds in the fleet.

Now you're ready: the carbon argument (DRAM ≈ 69% of embodied emissions) only pays off if the controller lets you reuse DIMMs — that constraint shaped the whole ASIC design, not just a software feature.
8How the production A/B tests work▾

Identically configured MemServer nodes serve live production traffic; the only difference is whether cpuset.mems permits CXL. The load balancer splits traffic evenly, so both groups see the same request mix and diurnal pattern; experiments run ≥1 week for steady state.

Now you're ready: every "+25%" or "−29%" number in this guide comes from exactly this A/B setup on live traffic, not a synthetic benchmark.
9The "knee of the curve" idea▾

As you force more of a workload's footprint to be actively ("hot") accessed, latency and CXL bandwidth stay flat for a long stretch, then rise sharply past some threshold — because TPP can no longer keep the whole hot set in local DRAM. This threshold is the safety margin the rest of the paper leans on.

Now you're ready: this is the paper's central quantitative artifact — it's the Big Idea tab's signature figure.
The whole story in one place

Vistara in a nutshell

The problem, the empirical fact that makes tiering safe, and how the ASIC + OS pieces fit together.

About 43.8% of servers across Meta's fleet — out of millions — are memory-capacity bound: CPU, storage, and network sit underused because DRAM runs out first. Worse, DRAM DIMMs account for 69% of the fleet's embodied carbon emissions, and useful memory lifetime (10–14 years) far outlasts server lifetime (5–7 years) — so early retirement of good DIMMs is pure waste.

CXL looks like the fix: decouple memory capacity from on-socket channels, and reuse decommissioned DDR4 as expansion memory. But six years after CXL's introduction, nobody had reported it actually working at hyperscale — commercial controllers bundle their own DRAM (blocking reuse), and prior characterizations warned of high tail latencies and heavy tiering-software overhead.

Vistara is the hardware half of the fix: a custom CXL 2.0/1.1 ASIC built specifically to accept reused DDR4 RDIMMs, with a ≈50ns idle ASIC-internal datapath latency (the end-to-end CXL memory access latency the host sees is ≈250ns idle) and ≈9W power draw. The software half builds on TPP: cold pages migrate to CXL, hot pages stay local, entirely transparently, exposed as one extra NUMA node.

The whole approach rests on one empirical fact: most fleet memory is cold. At the 25th percentile, pages go untouched for anywhere from a few seconds to a few minutes depending on workload (Table I's P25 values run from 4.2s to 4.3 minutes) — idle times only stretch into the tens of minutes at the P50–P75 percentiles for some workloads. A large majority of memory sits idle most of the time either way. Because the software stack deliberately keeps cold pages on the slow tier, CXL's ~10× lower bandwidth and ~60% higher latency barely matter — CXL bandwidth utilization in production stays under 10%.

The payoff: up to 25% fewer servers for disaggregated ML inference, 29% lower average latency for a distributed cache, and TPP-attributable overhead under 0.5% — all with tail latencies that track local DRAM even under heavy contention, refuting the FPGA-era concerns about both hardware and software.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • ~44% of the fleet is memory-capacity bound; DRAM is 69% of embodied carbon — CXL addresses both at once.
  • Vistara is a custom ASIC (not FPGA) built to accept reused DDR4 DIMMs — most commercial CXL controllers can't.
  • Tiering works because most memory is cold: cold pages tolerate CXL's ~10× lower BW / ~60% higher latency.
  • TPP, run largely unmodified, keeps CPU/tiering overhead <0.5% — no exotic OS redesign needed.
  • A measured "knee" at ~75% hot footprint is the safety margin; production workloads sit well below it.
  • Results: up to 25% fewer servers (ML inference), 29% lower latency (caches), tail latency ≈ local DRAM.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about Vistara

Each stands alone. Every card ends with an honest note on where the picture stops being true.

The core idea · Hot/cold tiering

The kitchen pantry vs. the storage unit

You keep tonight's ingredients within arm's reach in the kitchen (local DRAM); the seasonal gear you touch twice a year goes in an offsite storage unit (CXL). It works because most of what's in storage really is rarely used — the idle-time data across every workload shows most memory untouched for seconds to hours.

Where it breaks: a storage unit needs a drive and a manual trip. CXL memory is electrically part of the same machine — TPP fetches it back the instant you reach for it, no round trip to schedule.
DIMM reuse · Carbon

Reusing an engine, not the whole car

Decommission a server, and its DDR4 DIMMs are like a good engine block pulled from a totaled car — still useful for 10-14 years, but only if the new chassis (Vistara) accepts that part. Most commercial CXL controllers bundle their own DRAM, like a car that only takes a factory-sealed engine. Vistara was built to accept the used part.

Where it breaks: a junkyard deals with one make at a time; Vistara has to support a real mix of DDR4 vendors and speeds across the fleet — a messier supply chain than swapping one engine.
Multi-tenant · Fair Share

Two roommates, one shared closet

Two containers share a host: one closet (local DRAM) and an overflow unit (CXL). Without ground rules, the busier roommate quietly fills the closet and shoves the quieter one's stuff into the overflow unit — a 90%/70% local split for identical loads, and a 20-point P99 latency gap. Fair Share sets a per-roommate quota on the closet, restoring balance to ~74%/71%.

Where it breaks: roommates can negotiate on the fly; Fair Share's quotas (locallow/localhigh) must be pre-configured by the orchestrator — there's no runtime haggling.
Refuting a misconception · ASIC vs FPGA

The pontoon bridge vs. the real one

A prior study measured CXL tail latency on an FPGA likely limited by small SRAM credit buffers — like testing traffic on a narrow, temporary pontoon bridge and concluding all bridges jam under load. Vistara is the permanent, properly engineered bridge: its latency CDF tracks local DRAM closely even at 100 concurrent threads.

Where it breaks: this doesn't mean "any" ASIC would be fine — Vistara's stability came from specific choices (deep completion buffers, wide flow control, replay depth), not from being an ASIC per se.
Transparency · TPP promotion/demotion

A relay baton, not a mail package

TPP moves a page from CXL back to local DRAM the moment it's touched again — like a relay runner grabbing the baton mid-stride, not like mailing a package and waiting for delivery. The application never pauses to ask; it just runs, and the page is where it needs to be by the time the next access needs it.

Where it breaks: the very first touch of a demoted page still pays the full CXL latency before promotion happens — the baton hand-off is instant, but the runner still has to reach it.
Safety margin · Knee of the curve

The elevation profile of a hike

Most of the trail is a gentle, flat stretch — you barely notice the grade. Then, past a certain mile marker, it turns into a wall. The knee-of-the-curve stress test found that exact mile marker for hot-footprint fraction: flat below ~75%, a cliff above it. Production workloads' coldness data puts them well before the wall.

Where it breaks: a trail's elevation profile is known in advance from a map; the knee here is empirical and workload-dependent — you don't know your own workload's exact cliff until you stress-test it.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Hardware first, then OS integration, then the flexibility mechanisms that make it fleet-safe.

1 · The Vistara ASIC — a purpose-built bridge, not a generic controller

Vistara's job is narrow: bridge DDR4 memory to a host over CXL 2.0/1.1, cheaply and with low latency, while accepting reused DIMMs of mixed vintage. It exposes two independent 72-bit DDR4 channels (up to 3200MT/s, run at 2400MT/s in production for power/compatibility), delivering 128GB per chip from 4×32GB DIMMs — two chips per board give 256GB of CXL-attached memory per server.

2 · OS integration — one extra NUMA node, tiered transparently

The Linux CXL driver onlines Vistara's memory as ZONE_MOVABLE on its own NUMA node, guided by ACPI's CEDT/HMAT tables. From there, TPP and TMO do the actual work: track access recency via minor faults, promote hot pages to local DRAM, demote cold pages under memory pressure or proactively. Two sysctls matter most in production: numa_balancing=2 (migrate pages) and zone_reclaim_mode=7 (reclaim aggressively across zones).

3 · Fleet homogeneity — every server is a "1TB machine"

Deliberately, CXL memory is always integrated into system memory on every MemServer node — no selective offlining, no special "modes." That uniformity is what lets the same provisioning, health-monitoring, and firmware-update tooling apply everywhere. Flexibility (opting a specific workload out) is layered on afterward via cgroups, not by making some servers structurally different from others.

4 · Fair Share — per-container tiering quotas

On multi-tenant hosts, naive TPP can let one co-located container hog local DRAM while its neighbor gets pushed disproportionately onto CXL — a 90%/70% split in the paper's two-container CacheA test, a 20-point P99 latency gap, and a container getting OOM-killed under a noisy-neighbor load spike. Fair Share enforces configurable per-container bounds (locallow, localhigh) on local-DRAM usage: exceed localhigh and pages demote, regardless of whose turn LRU thinks it is.

💡
Why bound instead of just letting LRU decide? Plain recency has no notion of "container," so one busy tenant's access stream can starve a quiet tenant's working set — Fair Share re-introduces per-tenant accounting on top of TPP's per-page recency.
Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four traces through the paper's own tables — sizing the server, reading the latency gap, tallying a real cache win, and the multi-tenant fairness fix.

① Sizing a MemServer node

From Table VI: 768GB local DDR5 + 256GB CXL DDR4, ≈9W per Vistara ASIC × 2, plus ≈32W for the 8 DIMMs.

  1. Total memory = 768GB + 256GB = 1024GB (1TB) per node.
  2. CXL's share of capacity = 256 / 1024 = 25% — the default local:CXL ratio is 3:1.
  3. CXL expansion power = 2 × 9W (ASICs) + 32W (DIMMs) = ≈50W, against a 300W CPU TDP and 450–560W total server power — under ~10% of total power for +33% memory capacity.
  4. Peak CXL bandwidth ≈ 76GB/s vs local's 614GB/s — CXL is the minority tier on every axis (capacity, power, bandwidth) except cost.
🍯
Insight: Vistara isn't trying to match local DRAM — it's sized to add a quarter more capacity for a tenth more power, precisely because it only needs to hold cold pages.

② Reading the latency gap (Table IV)

At 60% bandwidth utilization: native latency 234ns, CXL latency 372ns. Does this match the abstract's "~60% higher latency" claim?

  1. Absolute gap = 372 − 234 = 138ns.
  2. Relative gap = 138 / 234 = ≈59% — yes, matches the abstract's "~60% higher latency" for the expanded memory used in production.
  3. At lower utilization (10%): native 169ns, CXL 269ns → gap = 100/169 = ≈59% too — the relative penalty is roughly stable across utilization, even as absolute latency rises with load.
  4. Why the penalty doesn't matter in practice: production CXL bandwidth utilization stays under 10% (Table VIII), so nodes mostly sit near the 10% row, not the 60% row — and the pages being hit there are cold ones the workload wasn't latency-sensitive about anyway.
💡
This is the arithmetic behind "CXL's higher latency is real but irrelevant" — the penalty is a fixed ~59-60%, but it's applied to accesses the workload barely makes.

③ CacheA's real production win

Memory footprint grew from 680GB to 890GB when CXL was enabled; QPS rose 33%; retention age went from ≈1 minute to ≈5–10 minutes.

  1. Capacity growth = (890 − 680) / 680 = +31% more cache capacity, mostly landing on the CXL tier (the hot connection/hashtable heap stays local).
  2. Throughput: sustainable QPS per server rose 33% — more objects cached means more requests served from memory instead of falling through to flash/remote.
  3. Retention age: 1 minute → 5–10 minutes is a 5–10× increase — objects survive far longer before eviction, smoothing out rebalancing during traffic surges.
  4. Flash side-effect: fewer misses propagate to SSD, directly extending flash endurance (write amplification drops) — a capacity win that also pays off as a reliability win.
📎
A note on the throughput number: the paper's main text and Figs. 9–10 report CacheA's throughput gain as +33% (used above); Table VII lists the same metric as +25%. The paper doesn't reconcile the discrepancy — treat both as pointing to a large, but not precisely agreed-upon, QPS improvement.
🍯
Insight: one CXL-enabled capacity increase (+31%) cascades into three separate wins — throughput, retention, and flash lifetime — because they're all downstream of "more objects fit in memory."

④ Fixing multi-tenant fairness

Two co-located CacheA containers, baseline vs. Fair Share (Table IX).

  1. Baseline imbalance: Container A keeps 90% of its working set local, Container B only 70% — a 20-point gap despite identical ≈2.5M QPS loads.
  2. Baseline P99 latency: 283µs (A) vs 327µs (B) — B pays for being pushed onto CXL more.
  3. Fair Share rebalances to 74%/71% local — nearly equal — and P99 drops to 176µs (A) and 185µs (B).
  4. Improvement factor: A improves 283/176 ≈ 1.6×; B improves 327/185 ≈ 1.8× — both containers get faster, not just the previously-starved one.
  5. Noisy-neighbor resilience: under a load spike on B, A's QPS drop falls from 65% (baseline, ending in an OOM-kill) to just 12% (Fair Share, recovers in seconds).
🍯
Insight: the baseline's problem wasn't that CXL is slow — it's that TPP's per-page recency has no notion of "which tenant," so one busy neighbor can starve another even when total capacity is enough for both.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
CXL memory in this deployment has ~10× lower bandwidth than local DRAM. Why doesn't this tank performance for capacity-bound workloads?
Show answer
Because the software stack deliberately places cold pages on CXL — pages accessed rarely by definition generate little traffic. Measured CXL bandwidth utilization in production stays under 10%, while local bandwidth utilization runs around 60%. The bandwidth-per-capacity on CXL is intentionally low, not accidentally tolerated.Misconception: that a device's raw bandwidth spec determines its suitability. What matters is the bandwidth actually demanded of it, which depends on what you choose to place there.
Problem 2 · medium
At 60% bandwidth utilization, native latency is 234ns and CXL latency is 372ns. Compute the relative latency penalty, and say whether it matches the abstract's headline claim.
Show answer
(372 − 234) / 234 = ≈59% — yes, this matches the abstract's "≈60% higher latency" for the expanded memory used in production. The gap is fairly stable across utilization levels (also ≈59% at 10% utilization).Misconception: that this penalty comes from inferior old memory. The paper is explicit that the higher latency is due to the expander datapath (≈150ns) and the deliberately lower DDR4 data rate (2400MT/s), not the DRAM chips themselves.
Problem 3 · medium
A prior study reported unstable, anomalous tail latencies for a CXL device under contention. Why doesn't Vistara reproduce this, and what does that imply about "CXL has bad tail latency" as a general claim?
Show answer
The prior device was an FPGA, likely limited by small on-chip SRAM — insufficient credit buffers for outstanding transactions, causing queuing anomalies under load. Vistara is a custom ASIC with deep completion buffers, wide flow-control windows, and sufficient replay depth; its latency CDF tracks local DRAM closely even at 100 concurrent threads. So the tail-latency problem was likely a property of that FPGA's engineering, not of the CXL protocol.Misconception: that "CXL" as a technology has inherently unstable tail latency. It's a claim about specific hardware implementations, and careful ASIC engineering avoids it.
Problem 4 · medium
Some prior work argued TPP-style memory tiering introduces substantial software overhead, motivating complex OS redesigns. What does Vistara's production data say?
Show answer
CPU and system overheads attributable to TPP — including minor-fault-based hotness detection — stay below 0.5% in production, with no evidence of TPP-induced regressions across the evaluated services. A simple LRU-based hotness detector was sufficient for most applications, and page-migration traffic was low enough that DMA-engine acceleration of migration is unlikely to help.Misconception: that transparent tiering necessarily needs a sophisticated hotness classifier or hardware-accelerated migration to be practical. At this fleet's scale, the simple, already-existing mechanism was enough.
Problem 5 · hard
On a host running two co-located CacheA containers, why does naive TPP end up giving one container 90% local residency and the other only 70%, even though both have identical load?
Show answer
TPP's promotion/demotion decisions are based on per-page recency, with no notion of which container a page belongs to. If one container's access pattern happens to generate slightly more or better-timed "hot" signals — e.g., due to timing of requests or hashtable layout — its pages win the recency competition and accumulate in local DRAM, at the other container's expense, even though total demand is equal. Fair Share fixes this by adding an explicit per-container accounting layer (locallow/localhigh bounds) on top of TPP's page-level recency.Misconception: that per-page recency-based tiering is automatically fair across tenants. Fairness across containers needs its own explicit mechanism; recency alone doesn't provide it.
Problem 6 · medium
Table I shows most workloads' pages go untouched for seconds to hours at the P25–P75 percentiles. Connect this to the "knee of the curve" result (Table X) and explain why production workloads are safe.
Show answer
The knee-of-the-curve stress test shows latency and CXL bandwidth stay flat as long as the actively "hot" fraction of memory is below ≈75% — TPP can keep that whole hot set in local DRAM. Since real workloads' idle-time data shows the large majority of pages are cold (untouched for a meaningful stretch), the fraction that's genuinely hot at any moment is well under 75% — comfortably inside the flat, safe regime, not near the cliff.Misconception: that "some memory is on a slower tier" is inherently risky. The risk only materializes if the hot working set itself exceeds what local DRAM can hold — which the idle-time data shows isn't the case for these workloads.
Problem 7 · easy
Why is CXL memory onlined as ZONE_MOVABLE instead of a regular kernel zone?
Show answer
Some kernel allocations (slab caches, page tables) are non-migratable — they must never move once placed. If they ended up on the slower, less-tested CXL tier, the system couldn't safely relocate them away from a degraded link or drain that memory for maintenance. ZONE_MOVABLE restricts CXL memory to migratable allocations only (user pages, movable kernel data), keeping all immovable structures on local DRAM.Misconception: that any free NUMA node can just absorb "overflow" allocations indiscriminately. The migratability of what you put there matters for reliability, not just capacity.
Problem 8 · hard
Most commercial CXL memory-expander products bundle their own DRAM with the controller. Why does this specifically block the carbon-reduction argument for CXL, even if the controller itself is efficient?
Show answer
The carbon case for CXL rests on reusing DIMMs that are 10–14 years serviceable but attached to servers retired at 5–7 years — avoiding new DRAM manufacturing, which is 69% of the fleet's embodied emissions. A controller with bundled DRAM forces you to buy new memory chips alongside the controller, defeating the reuse premise entirely — you'd be adding new DRAM production, not avoiding it, regardless of how power-efficient that controller's own chips are.Misconception: that any CXL-attached memory automatically yields the sustainability benefit. The benefit is specifically tied to DIMM reuse, which requires a controller architected to accept external, industry-standard, mixed-vintage DDR4 — a real constraint most commercial products don't meet.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Twelve questions newcomers to this deployment ask most.

Q1Is this paper proposing a new tiering algorithm?▾
No — it deliberately builds on existing TPP (page placement) and TMO (proactive reclaim), largely unmodified. The contribution is the end-to-end system: a custom ASIC that makes DRAM reuse and low latency possible, and the production engineering (ACPI, cgroups, fleet homogeneity, Fair Share) that makes it deployable at hyperscale. It's an existence proof, not a new algorithm paper.
Q2Where does CXL's extra latency actually come from?▾
Two sources, both explicit in the paper: ≈150ns from the expander datapath itself (controller/PHY/bridge — the CXL2DDR bridge logic), and the choice to run the expander-side DDR4 at a lower data rate (2400MT/s) for power efficiency and compatibility across mixed-vintage DIMMs. It is not a property of the reused DRAM chips being inferior.
Q3Does any production workload actually saturate CXL bandwidth?▾
No — across the seven workloads in Table VIII, CXL bandwidth utilization tops out at 13 GB/s (DevInfra), while local bandwidth reaches up to 300 GB/s (a data warehouse workload). The design intentionally keeps CXL as a low-traffic, capacity-only tier.
Q4What exactly is the "knee of the curve," and why 75%?▾
It's the fraction of a workload's memory footprint that can be actively "hot" before TPP can no longer keep the whole hot set in local DRAM, causing latency and CXL bandwidth to rise sharply. The stress test found this inflection at ≈75% hot footprint for the tested MemServer configuration; it's a property of the specific local:CXL capacity ratio (3:1) and bandwidth gap, not a universal constant.
Q5How does the OS decide a page is "cold" in the first place?▾
Through TPP's existing mechanism: NUMA hint faults track recent accesses, and pages that go unaccessed long enough under memory pressure (or via TMO's proactive scan) are demoted. This paper doesn't change the classifier — it validates that the simple, already-existing recency signal is accurate enough for most production applications.
Q6Why cgroups (cpuset.mems) instead of a BIOS toggle for opting out of CXL?▾
A BIOS toggle requires a reboot and creates hardware heterogeneity across the fleet (some servers "have" CXL enabled, some don't). cpuset.mems is a software policy that can be applied and reversed instantly by the resource orchestrator, while every server remains uniformly a 1TB machine underneath — preserving fleet-wide tooling consistency.
Q7Why does multi-tenancy need a separate "Fair Share" mechanism on top of TPP?▾
TPP's promotion/demotion decisions operate purely on page recency, with no concept of which container owns a page. On single-tenant hosts (the dominant production mode) this is fine. On hosts stacking multiple containers, recency alone can let one busy tenant crowd out a quieter one's working set — Fair Share adds explicit per-container local-DRAM quotas to prevent that.
Q8Does Vistara replace or compete with TPP?▾
Neither — it's the hardware Vistara's software stack runs on top of TPP. Vistara (ASIC) solves the hardware side (low-latency, DIMM-reusable CXL expander); TPP (already-published, from the same lab lineage) solves the software side (transparent page placement). This paper's novelty is showing the two work together at hyperscale.
Q9Why run the CXL-side DDR4 at 2400MT/s instead of its rated 3200MT/s?▾
Power and mixed-vintage compatibility. Since decommissioned DIMMs come from a variety of vendors and generations (Figure 13 shows at least nine distinct component types in the fleet), running at a conservative, universally-supported speed avoids per-DIMM qualification headaches and reduces power draw — at the cost of some of the extra latency this paper is explicit about.
Q10Is the carbon/reuse story just marketing, or does it show up in the results?▾
It's structural, not incidental: DRAM DIMMs are 69% of the fleet's embodied carbon, and the entire ASIC was designed around accepting external DDR4 RDIMMs (unlike most commercial controllers, which bundle DRAM and block reuse) — that requirement shaped chip design choices like ECC and multi-vendor DIMM support, described in detail in §III and §VI, not just claimed in the abstract.
Q11Does this generalize beyond Meta's specific fleet?▾
The paper is explicit that its numbers are workload- and fleet-specific (knee at 75% for this hardware ratio, ≈59% latency gap for this ASIC/DDR4 configuration). But the underlying argument — most datacenter memory is cold, so tiering's downside rarely bites — echoes coldness data reported by other hyperscalers and lines up with the design premise behind TPP, Pond, and similar systems.
Q12What's left unsolved?▾
The paper focuses on multiprogrammed, single- and (to a lesser extent) multi-tenant workloads on one platform generation. It doesn't address CXL 3.0-style pooling/sharing across hosts, and the ≈75% knee and 3:1 local:CXL ratio are specific to this hardware — workloads or ratios that push past that knee would need either higher-bandwidth CXL links or a different local:CXL split.
