Printing a city on a grain of sand, a billion times a day

One island, one Dutch company, and a handful of irreplaceable suppliers make nearly every advanced chip on Earth. This is the plain-English story of how — and why almost nobody else can.

Before you begin

Quick glossary, then the concepts the piece assumes

Quick glossary — acronyms you'll hit immediately

EUVExtreme UltraViolet light — the ~13.5 nm wavelength used to print the finest chip features. Generated by vaporizing tin droplets with lasers, not by any lamp or lens.
DUVDeep UltraViolet — the older, longer-wavelength lithography light (made with krypton/argon-fluoride excimer lasers) that EUV eventually had to supersede.
ICIntegrated Circuit — the "chip" itself: a single piece of semiconductor with transistors carved directly into it, replacing hand-soldered individual transistors.
GPUGraphics Processing Unit — Nvidia's chip category, originally for video games, whose architecture turned out to be ideal for A.I.
TSMCTaiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company — the Hsinchu-based "foundry" that fabricates almost all of the world's most advanced chips, for clients who design but don't manufacture.
ASMLThe Veldhoven, Netherlands firm that is the sole maker of EUV lithography machines — the one tool without which no advanced chip can be printed.
CHIPS ActThe 2022 U.S. law appropriating $50 billion to reindustrialize domestic chipmaking, including TSMC's Arizona fab.
GDPGross Domestic Product — used here to size the stakes: a Taiwan crisis is estimated to cost roughly a tenth of the world's.
1What a chip physically is▾

A microchip is a sliver of semiconducting material — mostly silicon — with billions of microscopic switches, called transistors, carved directly into it. Each transistor can be flipped on (conducting, like a metal) or off (blocking, like glass). Wire enough of them together in the right pattern and you get a circuit that can compute anything.

Feature sizes are now smaller than a virus. The piece describes a chip as a city etched at atomic scale, where a golf ball shrinks to the size of a hemoglobin molecule.

Now you're ready: everything that follows is about how you reliably carve a city's worth of detail into a fingernail-sized piece of sand, a billion times a day, without a single wrong brick.
2Moore's Law — a schedule, not a law of physics▾

In 1964, Gordon Moore noticed transistor density had been doubling roughly every year and extrapolated the trend forward. It wasn't a physical law like gravity — it was an industrial promise that the whole sector then organized itself to keep, at staggering cost, for six decades.

By the early 1990s, the promise looked broken: deep ultraviolet light seemed to be the last affordable option, with a hard wall around 2005. EUV is the technology that rescued the schedule.

Now you're ready: the EUV story isn't a nice-to-have upgrade — it's the thing that kept a sixty-year self-fulfilling prophecy from quietly ending twenty years early.
3The foundry model: designing chips vs. making them▾

Historically, chip companies designed and manufactured their own chips in-house ("real men have fabs," as one executive famously put it). Morris Chang's insight at TSMC was to split the two: TSMC builds nothing of its own design, only other companies' chips, as a foundry.

That split is why Nvidia, Apple, and Qualcomm can exist at all — they design chips without ever owning a $20-billion fab.

Now you're ready: when the piece says Nvidia is the world's most valuable company, remember it has never manufactured a single transistor itself — TSMC does, under ASML's light.
4Lithography: printing with light▾

A chip's blueprint is etched onto a stencil ("mask"). Light shone through that stencil projects the pattern onto silicon coated with a light-sensitive chemical — exactly like developing a photograph, except the "photograph" is a city of circuitry.

The finer the detail you need, the shorter the wavelength of light required. That single fact drove the industry from ordinary lamps, to violet light, to deep UV lasers, to extreme UV — each step harder than the last.

Now you're ready: "lithography" is the whole game. Everything ASML does is in service of making that one step of printing-with-light go finer, faster, and more reliably.
5Why EUV light had to be manufactured, not borrowed▾

Extreme ultraviolet light doesn't come from any natural source we can harness on Earth (the nearest is the sun). It also can't pass through ordinary lenses — it's absorbed by almost everything, including air. So ASML had to generate it artificially and bounce it, in a vacuum, off mirrors instead of focusing it through glass.

The generation method: fire molten tin droplets into a vacuum chamber and blast them with lasers sixty thousand times a second, igniting plasma fifty times hotter than the sun's surface.

Now you're ready: when the piece calls this "the industrial production of miniature supernovas," that's not hyperbole — it's the literal physical description of how every advanced chip's pattern gets printed.
6Yield, and the "learning curve"▾

Yield is the percentage of chips on a wafer that come out working. Early production lines can have near-zero yield; Morris Chang's first real engineering triumph at Texas Instruments was dragging a line's yield from ~0% to over 20% in months.

TSMC's whole business model rests on the "learning curve": every unit you manufacture is a chance to improve the process, so making more of something makes each unit both better and cheaper.

Now you're ready: this is why T.S.M.C. "manufactures the best chips... because it has come to manufacture almost all of them" — yield compounds with volume.
7Advanced packaging — chips made of chips▾

Once you can no longer easily shrink a single die further, you can still gain performance by combining several dies — logic, memory, accelerators — into one tightly interconnected package. This is advanced packaging, and it's its own manufacturing discipline, with its own fab (TSMC's AP6, opened 2023).

The hard part: dies vary slightly at atomic scale and must be matched for compatibility, which one TSMC executive compared to running "the world's largest dating site."

Now you're ready: today's AI chips (Nvidia's, for instance) are increasingly won or lost in packaging, not just transistor count — this is the newest front in the race.
8A supply chain of monopolies, not a market▾

At nearly every stage — ultra-pure quartz, polysilicon purification, silicon-ingot growth, EUV light, advanced fabrication — only one or two firms on Earth can do the job. This isn't collusion; the work is so specialized that concentrating expertise in one place is the only way it gets done at all.

A finished chip can cross international borders more than seventy times before reaching a customer, each crossing a hop between one of these effective monopolies.

Now you're ready: the piece's framing — "cooperative," "planned economy" — is doing real work. Ordinary competitive-market intuitions mislead you here.
9Taiwan, the "silicon shield," and strategic ambiguity▾

Because T.S.M.C. makes almost all of the world's advanced chips, Taiwan's economic indispensability functions as an informal deterrent against invasion — the "silicon shield." This is distinct from any formal U.S. military promise, which Washington deliberately leaves ambiguous ("strategic ambiguity").

Bloomberg Economics estimated the first year of a war over Taiwan could cost the world economy $10.6 trillion — roughly a tenth of global G.D.P.

Now you're ready: every geopolitical claim in the piece — CHIPS Act, export controls, Arizona fabs — is downstream of this one structural fact.
The whole story in one place

Why this is the hardest thing humans make — on purpose

The thesis, the irreplicable chain that proves it, and how it ties to the stakes.

The piece's central claim: the global semiconductor supply chain, not the moon landing, is modern civilization's most impressive collective achievement — because unlike a rocket, it is reproduced by the billion, continuously, and because no single institution, government, or genius could have built it alone. "Semiconductors are an eleven," one investor says, on a complexity scale where rockets rate a five or six.

Two forces made chips indispensable rather than interchangeable commodities ("potato chips, computer chips, what's the difference?"). First, Moore's Law — not a law of physics but a sustained industrial promise — demanded light short enough to print atomic-scale features, which only ASML's extreme-ultraviolet machines can generate, at up to $400 million apiece. Second, that light is useless without T.S.M.C.'s "foundry" model and decades of compounding yield expertise, concentrated almost entirely in Taiwan.

The result is a chain where nearly every step — ultra-pure quartz, polysilicon, silicon ingots, EUV light, fabrication, advanced packaging — is a natural monopoly or duopoly, bound together not by arm's-length markets but by decades of trust, dedicated capacity, and tacit craft knowledge that cannot be copied from a blueprint ("there simply is no 'file' of our machines").

The stakes: that fragile, irreplicable chain now underwrites both the global economy (AI, phones, cars, dishwashers) and the balance of military power — and it runs almost entirely through one island a hundred miles from a superpower that disputes its sovereignty.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • Chips aren't commodities — unlike potato chips, their production can't be relocated at will. Each stage is a near-monopoly.
  • Moore's Law is a sustained industrial choice, kept alive past its near-death in the 1990s only by EUV light.
  • ASML is the sole source of EUV machines; T.S.M.C. is the dominant maker of the most advanced chips — and they depend on each other and on Zeiss, their lens partner.
  • Intellectual property here is distributed and tacit, not a transferable "file" — which is why blueprints alone can't be copied by a rival.
  • Taiwan's "silicon shield" is economic deterrence, not a formal security guarantee — the U.S. keeps "strategic ambiguity" on actual defense.
  • A Taiwan crisis is estimated at roughly 10% of global GDP in year one — chips are now a geopolitical fault line, not just an economic one.
💡
Why this matters for tiering/CXL work: the EUV-driven transistor-density curve is the entire reason DRAM, HBM, and CXL controllers keep getting denser and cheaper per byte — the piece's "$1 million to $2 a gigabyte" collapse (Tab 05) is the raw material your tiering research treats as a given.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about the chip chain

Each stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with where the picture stops being true.

Scale · Density

A city engraved on a fingernail

Imagine a whole metropolis reproduced at atomic scale: every golf ball shrunk to the bulk of a hemoglobin molecule. That's the literal scale claim behind a modern chip — billions of switches, each narrower than a virus, etched into something you could lose in a carpet.

Where it breaks: a sculpture just sits there; this "city" is functional — a working metropolis for busy electrons, not a static monument. The analogy captures scale, not behavior.
Momentum · Moore's Law

A relay race with no finish line

Moore's Law isn't gravity — it's more like a relay race the whole industry quietly agreed never to stop running. Each generation hands off a harder baton (aluminum → copper wiring, visible light → EUV) so the pace never breaks, even when the next leg looks physically impossible.

Where it breaks: relay runners know the course in advance. The industry didn't know EUV was physically achievable when it committed — it bet the race on a leg nobody could yet run.
EUV's mechanism · Manufactured light

A supernova cannon, firing on repeat

EUV light doesn't exist naturally on Earth, so ASML manufactures it: molten tin droplets fired into a vacuum, blasted by lasers sixty thousand times a second, igniting plasma fifty times hotter than the sun. A real supernova happens once; this one has to happen continuously, for years, without a single misfire ruining a mirror.

Where it breaks: a real supernova is uncontrolled chaos; this one must be aimed, timed, and cleaned (with a hydrogen-gas "hurricane") precisely enough to hit the same spot sixty thousand times a second. The violence is the easy part — the control is the miracle.
Structure · Not a free market

A cooperative, not a marketplace

Econ-101 markets assume many interchangeable sellers haggling at arm's length. The chip chain is the opposite: one valve-maker, persuaded by decades of trust to build a part ASML will never buy in normal quantities, is more like a guild member than a vendor. The piece calls it "a kind of cooperative, or a planned economy."

Where it breaks: a cooperative usually shares governance and risk explicitly. Here the "membership" is implicit and informal — built from relationships and sunk trust, not contracts that formally bind anyone to show up next year.
Fragility · The "choke point"

A single valve for the whole plumbing

Cut off a city's water at one street valve and every tap downstream goes dry, no matter how good the rest of the plumbing is. ASML's EUV monopoly is that valve for the entire chip industry — which is exactly why its own executives flinch at the word "choke point": it invites someone to try turning it.

Where it breaks: a water valve can be forced open or replaced by force; ASML's "valve" is made of decades of distributed tacit knowledge across hundreds of suppliers, so seizing the company wouldn't hand anyone the ability to actually turn it.
Deterrence · The "silicon shield"

A diamond nobody wants broken

A priceless, irreplaceable diamond kept on open display deters theft less through locks than through the sheer cost of breaking it — everyone loses if it shatters. Taiwan's economic indispensability works the same way: not a guaranteed guard, but a reason the whole world has skin in keeping the display case intact.

Where it breaks: a diamond's value is static; T.S.M.C.'s value depends on a living, functioning island of workers and suppliers. A blockade doesn't need to "shatter" the chips to break the shield — just interrupt them for a season or two.
The full explanation

How a chip actually gets made

Sand to silicon to supernova to shipping container — with the chain's own diagrams rebuilt as visuals.

1 · From a mountain to a wafer

Leading-edge chips start with polysilicon purified to "eleven nines" — 99.999999999% — a feat only a handful of firms (based in Michigan, Germany, and Japan) can perform. That silicon is melted in crucibles made from the world's purest quartz, nearly all mined from one deposit in North Carolina, then grown into crystalline ingots and sliced, like a metallic salami, into wafers. This step alone is handled by essentially three companies on Earth.

2 · Why lithography had to go extreme

A chip's pattern is printed by shining light through a stencil onto a light-sensitive coating — the same logic as a photograph. As transistors shrank, the industry marched down the light spectrum: visible light → violet → deep ultraviolet (DUV, via krypton/argon-fluoride lasers) → and, by the early 1990s, off the edge of what seemed physically possible.

⚠
The near-miss: deep UV looked like reality's final offer. Without a breakthrough, chips were set to flatline around 2005 — no iPhone, no Uber, but also no reliable five-day weather forecasts, pocket ultrasound, or rapid genetic sequencing. EUV is the reason that didn't happen.

3 · T.S.M.C.'s thousand steps

A wafer is coated, exposed to light, etched with plasma, washed, polished, and inspected — then that whole loop repeats roughly a hundred times for the most advanced chips, "as if an entire city skyline were being raised one story at a time." T.S.M.C. supplies the discipline: constant monitoring (every shift opens with a handover briefing reviewing step-by-step efficiency, down to seconds of idle machine time) and the compounding "learning curve" that improves yield with every wafer produced.

4 · Advanced packaging: when shrinking isn't enough

Once transistors are hard to shrink further, performance increasingly comes from combining multiple dies — logic, memory, accelerators — into one package. T.S.M.C.'s AP6 fab, opened in 2023, is devoted entirely to this; its clean rooms, roughly the floor area of twenty-two soccer fields, serve only its top five customers. The hard constraint is matching dies with atomic-scale manufacturing variance, "like running the world's largest dating site."

💡
Why this should sound familiar: this die-matching problem — pairing heterogeneous components for compatible performance rather than raw capacity — rhymes with the matching problem in heterogeneous-memory systems (DRAM/NVM/CXL tiers), just enforced at the physical rather than access-latency layer.

5 · The global chain, enforced by scarcity of expertise

Most steps along this chain are monopolies or duopolies — not from market manipulation, but because each task is specialized enough that concentrating all available expertise in one or two places is the only way to do it at all. A chip can cross international borders more than seventy times before reaching a customer.

Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Five figures from the piece, carried all the way through the arithmetic.

① Moore's prediction, and the chip it accidentally named

In 1964, the state of the art was 60 transistors. Moore extrapolated to 1975. The newest chip that year had 65,536. How many doublings is that, over how many years, and what is 65,536, exactly?

  1. Set up the ratio: 65,536 ÷ 60 ≈ 1,092.3× growth over 11 years.
  2. Convert to doublings: log₂(1,092.3) ≈ 10.1 doublings — close to "once a year," matching the trend Moore had actually observed.
  3. Notice the number itself: 65,536 = 2¹⁶ exactly. That's not a coincidence of extrapolation — it's the bit-count of a 64-kilobit memory chip, a real DRAM generation from the mid-1970s.
🍯
Insight: the "famous Moore's Law bet" landed almost exactly on a specific, real memory part — a reminder that "transistor count" in this era was often literally "bits of DRAM." The curve your tiering work treats as background noise is this same curve, just sixty more years along it.

② How concentrated is Taiwan's choke point, versus oil's?

Gelsinger compares the Strait of Hormuz (≈20% of world oil) to Taiwan (≈80% of the world's advanced-chip supply chain). How much more concentrated is the chip choke point?

  1. Take the ratio: 80% ÷ 20% = 4× more concentrated.
  2. Cross-check against the dollar estimate: Bloomberg Economics puts the first year of a Taiwan war at $10.6 trillion. Against a roughly $106 trillion global economy, that's ≈ 10% of global GDP — matching the piece's own "roughly a tenth" framing.
💡
A single-point failure 4× more concentrated than the oil market, worth a tenth of world output in year one, is the quantitative backbone behind every "silicon shield" sentence in the piece.

③ The EUV wattage deadline, and how long "too crazy to work" actually took

Samsung's 2006 order required ≥100 watts of usable EUV light for the economics to work. ASML was stuck at 5 watts. The deadline was blown by seven years; the first EUV-printed chip shipped in 2019.

  1. Size the gap at order time: 100W ÷ 5W = a 20× shortfall — not a tweak, a different machine.
  2. Total elapsed time: 2006 order → 2019 first shipped chip = 13 years of sustained investment before the product existed at all.
🌱
Thirteen years is longer than a PhD plus two postdocs — the piece's point is that this kind of timeline is normal in this industry, and only survives because institutions commit to it before the physics is proven out.

④ How far memory prices actually fell

When ASML was founded, a gigabyte of memory cost nearly $1,000,000. EUV-printed memory now costs about $2 a gigabyte.

  1. Take the ratio: $1,000,000 ÷ $2 = a 500,000× price collapse per gigabyte.
  2. Put it in tiering terms: this is the economic precondition for treating a gigabyte of DRAM, let alone NVM or CXL-attached memory, as a commodity resource to be partitioned and migrated — rather than a mainframe-era treasure to be rationed by hand.
🍯
Insight: every tiering policy assumes memory is cheap enough to over-provision and move around. That assumption has a specific, dated cause: EUV.

⑤ From six transistors to ten billion

The Apollo guidance computer's chip had 6 transistors. The first EUV-printed phone chip (2019) had more than 10 billion.

  1. Take the ratio: 10,000,000,000 ÷ 6 ≈ a 1.67-billion-fold increase in transistor count on a similarly sized chip.
  2. Sanity-check against doublings: log₂(1.67×10⁹) ≈ 30.6 doublings over roughly 53 years (1966–2019) — about one doubling every 1.7 years, consistent with a Moore's-Law pace that slowed but never stopped.
💡
The same curve that took a chip from guiding astronauts to the moon to underpowering a pregnancy test is the curve that makes today's CXL/DRAM controllers themselves possible — they are, after all, chips too.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
A 1980s government official reportedly said, "Potato chips, computer chips, what's the difference? They're all chips." What's wrong with that reasoning, given what you now know about the supply chain?
Show answer
Potato chips can be made wherever there's potatoes, oil, and a factory — the inputs and process are widely replicable, so free-trade logic (let the cheapest producer win) applies cleanly. Computer chips depend on a handful of irreplaceable, decades-in-the-making monopolies (EUV light, specific quartz deposits, tacit fab expertise) that can't be relocated or rebuilt quickly. Losing domestic chip capacity isn't like losing a domestic snack-food plant.Misconception: that all manufactured goods are economically interchangeable "widgets." Semiconductor production is structurally unlike almost everything else we manufacture.
Problem 2 · medium
An ASML engineer told Chinese rivals they couldn't build an EUV machine even if handed the blueprints. Why would having the blueprints not be enough?
Show answer
There is no single "file" — the intellectual property is spread across hundreds of specialized suppliers' own proprietary know-how (e.g., Zeiss doesn't even hold the full recipe for its own glass; its glass supplier does), plus tacit, hands-on craft skill that can't be written down (an engineer tuning a dial "by feel"). A blueprint describes the design; it doesn't transfer the distributed expertise needed to execute it.Misconception: that intellectual property equals a transferable document. Here, much of the real "IP" is embodied in people, relationships, and uncodified craft.
Problem 3 · medium
Using the figures in the piece, verify: does Moore's 1975 prediction (65,536 from a 1964 base of 60) actually imply a literal doubling every single year? What's the more accurate description?
Show answer
log₂(65,536/60) ≈ 10.1 doublings over 11 years — close to, but not exactly, one doubling per year; it's consistent with "roughly annual" doubling with some slack, not a mathematically precise yearly doubling. (Moore himself later revised the cadence to roughly every two years, which the piece's 1975→2019 jump — ~17 doublings in 44 years — fits better.)Misconception: treating "Moore's Law" as one fixed, eternal doubling rate. The actual cadence changed over the decades, and the industry adjusted to keep the promise rather than the original number.
Problem 4 · medium
Why does the piece describe the chip supply chain as "a kind of cooperative, or a planned economy" rather than a competitive free market?
Show answer
At nearly every stage there's only one or two capable suppliers on Earth — not due to collusion, but because the work is so specialized that pooling the available expertise in one place is the only way it happens. Relationships, accumulated trust, and dedicated (non-fungible) capacity substitute for price-based arm's-length competition — as when ASML persuaded a valve-maker to engineer a custom part it would never buy in normal commercial quantities.Misconception: assuming intense global competition fully explains the industry's structure. Natural-monopoly specialization, not competitive dynamics, is doing most of the explanatory work.
Problem 5 · hard
China's best A.I. models reportedly lag the best Western ones by only three to six months, yet the piece treats chip export controls as strategically decisive. Why does chip access matter more than model quality here?
Show answer
Deployed A.I. capability depends on compute at scale, not just algorithmic parity — training and serving frontier models requires enormous, continuously available chip supply. Even a China with equally good algorithms is constrained if it can't access comparable volumes of advanced chips; the five-year gap in chipmaking capability (not model quality) is what the export-control regime is trying to preserve.Misconception: conflating "A.I. capability" with model quality alone. Compute supply is a separate, often binding constraint on deployed capability.
Problem 6 · hard
T.S.M.C.'s Arizona (Phoenix) fab took about four years to complete; a comparable first phase in Taiwan (AP7) rose in eighteen months. Explain the gap structurally, not just as "different labor skill."
Show answer
Taiwan's speed comes from an entire local ecosystem: a graduated ladder of trusted contractors, specialized vendors (down to custom shock-absorbing handcarts made by a single Taiwanese company), a clean-room laundry industry, and a workforce raised inside the T.S.M.C. culture. None of that transplants along with a factory shell — Arizona had to import even bunny-suit laundering across the Pacific. It's not one building; it's rebuilding a slice of an entire "superorganism."Misconception: treating a fab as a self-contained building you can replicate anywhere with the right staff. The surrounding supplier/logistics ecosystem is often the actual bottleneck.
Problem 7 · medium
Why does "advanced packaging" matter so much for today's AI chip crunch specifically, rather than just being a minor finishing step?
Show answer
As shrinking individual transistors gets harder and more expensive, performance gains increasingly come from combining multiple dies (logic, HBM memory stacks, accelerators) into one tightly matched package — which is its own yield-critical manufacturing discipline (T.S.M.C. built an entire dedicated fab, AP6, for it) serving only its top handful of customers. For today's GPUs, packaging capacity is now as binding a constraint as raw fabrication capacity.Misconception: that "packaging" is a trivial final assembly step. It is now one of the primary bottlenecks in AI-chip supply.
Problem 8 · easy
Does Taiwan's "silicon shield" mean the U.S. is treaty-bound to defend it militarily if China invades?
Show answer
No. The "silicon shield" is an economic deterrent — the world has so much to lose from disrupted chip supply that it creates pressure against invasion. U.S. policy on actual military defense is deliberately "strategic ambiguity": Washington has not committed, one way or the other, to intervene. Economic indispensability and formal security guarantees are two different things, even though people often conflate them.Misconception: assuming economic importance automatically implies a guaranteed military response. The piece is explicit that U.S. policy keeps this ambiguous on purpose.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Eleven questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What's the real difference between a "foundry" and companies like Intel historically?▾
A foundry (T.S.M.C.'s model) manufactures chips designed by other companies and designs nothing of its own; a traditional integrated firm designs and manufactures its own chips in-house. The foundry split let fabless companies like Nvidia, Apple, and Qualcomm exist without ever owning a fab.
Q2Why can't lamp light or even deep-UV lasers print today's finest chip features?▾
Finer features require shorter wavelengths of light. Deep UV (via excimer lasers) was the industry's last conventional option, and by the early 1990s it looked like the end of the line — until ASML engineered extreme ultraviolet (EUV), which required abandoning lenses for mirrors and generating the light artificially in a vacuum, since nothing else transmits it.
Q3Why is ASML a monopoly, and why does the firm dislike being called a "choke point"?▾
ASML and its lens partner Zeiss are the only firms that cracked EUV generation and optics after decades of investment few others were willing to make. "Choke point" worries executives because it invites governments to treat the firm as a strategic asset subject to seizure or forced nationalization, rather than a private company.
Q4Is Taiwan's dominance just about cheap labor?▾
No — it's about T.S.M.C.'s decades-compounded yield expertise, its foundry business model (pioneered by Morris Chang against skepticism from Gordon Moore himself), and a dense local supplier/workforce ecosystem that can't be quickly relocated, as the slower Arizona build-out demonstrates.
Q5What does the CHIPS Act actually try to fix?▾
It appropriated $50 billion to rebuild domestic chip manufacturing capacity, including T.S.M.C.'s Arizona fab (the act's second-largest award). The goal is explicitly described by one administration official as "a generational program" — seeding an ecosystem, not completing one.
Q6Why does a single Taiwan crisis matter so much economically?▾
Bloomberg Economics estimates the first year of a war over Taiwan could cost $10.6 trillion globally — roughly a tenth of world GDP — because chip shortages would cascade into cars, phones, medical devices, and the AI-driven stock market within a season or two of disrupted supply.
Q7Why does a chip cross more than seventy borders before reaching a customer?▾
Because each specialized stage — quartz, polysilicon, ingots, EUV light generation, fabrication, packaging — exists in only one or two places on Earth, and those places are rarely adjacent to each other. The chain is global almost by physical necessity, not by choice.
Q8Is Moore's Law over?▾
It nearly ended in the 1990s–2000s when deep UV hit its limits; EUV extended it. The cadence has slowed from roughly annual to roughly biennial doublings, and gains increasingly come from advanced packaging rather than pure transistor shrinkage — but the underlying "promise to keep improving" the piece describes is still being kept, just by different means.
Q9Why does advanced packaging matter for AI chips specifically?▾
Modern AI accelerators increasingly gain performance from combining multiple dies (compute, HBM memory) into one package rather than from shrinking a single die further. T.S.M.C. built an entire dedicated fab (AP6) for this, serving only its top five customers — packaging capacity is now a binding constraint on AI chip supply.
Q10What's the actual relationship between Nvidia, T.S.M.C., and ASML?▾
Nvidia designs chips but manufactures nothing; T.S.M.C. fabricates Nvidia's designs in its Taiwan fabs; T.S.M.C.'s most advanced fabrication is only possible because of ASML's EUV machines (built with Zeiss optics). Each layer is dependent on the one below it, and none can substitute for another.
Q11Does the piece think this fragile system is a problem, or something to admire?▾
Both. The writer frames it as one of civilization's great collective achievements — comparable to Stonehenge, built not by secret lost technology but by massed, patient coordination — while also treating its concentration in one geopolitically contested island as an acute, specific danger.
