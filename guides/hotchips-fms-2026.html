The AI memory wall, and the new rungs being built to climb it

Two August-2026 conferences told one story: HBM is too small, the SSD is too far, so the industry is inserting brand-new tiers — High-Bandwidth Flash, CXL memory pools, and compute placed inside the memory — between them. Your DRAM/NVM tiering work just became the main event.

🔥 Hot Chips 2026 · Aug 23–25 · Stanford 💾 FMS 2026 · Aug 4–6 · Santa Clara 📰 From public coverage — official decks open ~Dec ⏱ ~30 min read
🧱

When you can't make the fast tier bigger or the big tier faster, add a new tier between them — and move the compute to the data instead of dragging the data to the compute.
That single move explains almost every announcement below.

Before you begin

Seven ideas the 2026 announcements assume

You live in the memory-tiering world already, so these skip the fundamentals and pin down the specific 2026 vocabulary — the memory wall's two halves, CXL vs UCIe, near-memory vs PIM, and the HBM-packaging and NAND-scaling terms the vendors kept using.

DRAM-class fast memory (HBM, DDR5) Flash-class (HBF, NAND, SSD) the fabric & near-memory compute (CXL, UCIe, PIM) the AI memory wall / limits
1The "memory wall" has two walls, not one▾

For LLM inference the bottleneck is memory in two independent ways. Bandwidth wall: generating each token streams the whole model's weights past the compute, so tokens/sec is capped by bytes/sec out of memory — this is why GPUs strap on HBM. Capacity wall: a frontier model plus its KV-cache for many concurrent users needs terabytes resident, but an HBM stack holds only tens of GB, so you buy GPUs you don't need just for their memory.

Compute (FLOPS) has raced ahead of both. The 2026 news is almost entirely about attacking one wall or the other.

Now you're ready: read every announcement below by asking "is this buying bandwidth, capacity, or getting compute out of the way?"
2The hierarchy today: HBM · DDR5 · SSD (and the gaps)▾

Roughly, in 2026: HBM gives ~1–2+ TB/s per stack but only tens of GB, on-package and expensive. DDR5 server DRAM gives ~0.1–0.5 TB/s per socket and up to a few TB, cheaper. NVMe SSD gives ~GB/s and near-unlimited TB, but at microsecond+ latency and block, not byte, access.

Between HBM and SSD is a yawning gap: nothing offers HBM-ish bandwidth at SSD-ish capacity and cost. Filling that gap is the theme of both shows.

Now you're ready: HBF and CXL-pooled memory are two different attempts to occupy that empty middle band. The signature figure in "The Big Idea" plots it.
3CXL, in one screen▾

CXL (Compute Express Link) rides on the PCIe physical layer but adds cache-coherent load/store semantics, so a device can expose byte-addressable memory the CPU reaches with ordinary loads — not a block device. A Type-3 device is pure memory expansion; pooling lets several hosts share one box of memory, allocating capacity where it's needed instead of stranding it in each server.

2026 CXL is at 3.x over PCIe 6: more bandwidth, switching, and the pooling/sharing features maturing from spec to silicon.

Now you're ready: "CXL-pooled memory" attacks the capacity wall and stranding; "CXL Type-3 + compute" (XCENA) also attacks bandwidth by adding processing at the device.
4UCIe — and why HBF rides it, not CXL▾

UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express) is a die-to-die standard for stitching chiplets inside one package over an interposer — very short reach, enormous aggregate bandwidth, low energy-per-bit. It's how you glue an accelerator to memory that sits millimeters away, not centimeters.

HBF chose UCIe precisely because its pitch is "HBM-like, in-package throughput" — you place the flash stack right next to the compute, unlike an SSD hanging off a PCIe/CXL cable.

Now you're ready: interconnect choice signals intent: UCIe = "in-package, bandwidth-first" (HBF); CXL/PCIe = "out-of-package, capacity/pooling-first" (memory expanders).
5Near-memory compute vs Processing-in-Memory (PIM)▾

Both fight the bandwidth wall by not moving data so far. Near-memory compute puts ordinary processors next to the DRAM (same package/board), so they enjoy the device's huge internal bandwidth without squeezing it through a thin host link — this is the XCENA MX1 approach (thousands of small RISC-V cores beside 2 TB of DDR5). PIM goes further, embedding compute into the memory die itself (e.g., SK hynix's CMM-Ax), so operations happen at the DRAM banks.

Now you're ready: when a device advertises "TFLOPS" alongside "TB of memory," it's a near-memory/PIM play — the point isn't peak FLOPS, it's FLOPS per byte moved over the host link.
6HBM packaging words: base die, MR-MUF, hybrid bonding▾

An HBM stack is many DRAM dies on a base die (the logic layer that talks to the host); owning that base-die design is now a competitive lever. Stacking method matters: MR-MUF (mass-reflow molded underfill) is today's mainstream bonding; hybrid bonding (direct copper-to-copper, no bumps) is the next step that lets stacks go taller/cooler as the industry bumps into a ~775 µm package-height ceiling.

Now you're ready: "hybrid bonding for HBM5" and "in-house base die" are bandwidth-and-thermals plays — squeezing more out of the fast tier that already exists.
7NAND scaling words: layers, TLC/QLC, 4D / wafer bonding▾

3D NAND stacks charge-storage cells vertically; more layers = more bits per wafer (2026 is at ~300–375 layers). TLC stores 3 bits/cell, QLC 4 (denser, cheaper, slower, less endurance). Areal density (Gb/mm²) is the real yardstick. "4D NAND"/wafer bonding puts the control logic on a separate wafer bonded under the array (CMOS-under-array taken further), freeing area for cells.

Now you're ready: denser NAND is the raw material for both cheaper SSDs and for HBF — HBF is "NAND arranged for bandwidth," so NAND scaling directly feeds the new middle tier.
The whole story in one place

One thesis, three moves

Strip away the logos and both conferences argue the same thing — and respond in the same three ways.

The thesis: AI has broken the memory hierarchy's shape. Compute grew faster than either memory bandwidth or memory capacity, so the old three-rung ladder — HBM / DRAM / SSD — has a hole in the middle: there is no tier with HBM-like bandwidth at DRAM-or-SSD-like capacity and cost. Every 2026 headline is one of three responses to that hole.

Move 1 — build a new rung (High-Bandwidth Flash). At FMS, SK hynix and SanDisk published the first HBF standard through the OCP: NAND arranged like HBM (stacked, wide, in-package over UCIe), reportedly up to 512 GB per device at 0.4–3.0 TB/s. It slots exactly into the gap — roughly an order of magnitude more capacity than an HBM stack, at HBM-ish bandwidth, at lower cost — with Google publicly backing it.

Move 2 — pool the capacity you have (CXL). CXL 3.x memory pooling lets terabytes of DRAM be shared across hosts instead of stranded per-server, and SK hynix showed it accelerating agentic "ReAct" serving. This attacks the capacity wall without new silicon physics — it's a fabric-and-allocation story, squarely in your tiering wheelhouse.

Move 3 — stop moving the data (near-memory compute / PIM). At Hot Chips, XCENA's MX1 put 3,072 RISC-V cores next to 2 TB of DDR5 on a CXL Type-3 device, doing the bandwidth-bound math on-device so it never crosses the thin host link — reportedly 200+ GFLOPS where an external accelerator on the same CXL link manages ~64. SK hynix's PIM (CMM-Ax) makes the same bet inside the DRAM.

Around these, the fast tier keeps getting tuned: Samsung's zHBM claims big power/bandwidth gains over HBM4e, SK hynix pushes hybrid-bonded HBM5, and NAND marches to 375 layers — the raw material HBF is made of. The DRAM/NVM tiering you study is no longer a research niche; it's the industry's roadmap.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • The AI memory wall is two walls — bandwidth and capacity — and both now bind harder than FLOPS.
  • HBF is the marquee new tier: NAND arranged HBM-style over UCIe, ~512 GB at 0.4–3.0 TB/s, filling the gap between HBM and SSD (first OCP spec by SK hynix + SanDisk; Google backing).
  • CXL 3.x pooling attacks the capacity wall by de-stranding DRAM across hosts (SK hynix ReAct agent-serving demo).
  • Near-memory compute / PIM (XCENA MX1; SK hynix CMM-Ax) attacks the bandwidth wall by computing where the data already lives.
  • The fast tier is still improving: Samsung zHBM, SK hynix hybrid-bonded HBM5, in-house base dies, a ~775 µm height ceiling driving new bonding.
  • NAND hit ~375 layers (SK hynix) and record areal density (Kioxia/SanDisk BiCS10) — feeding both cheaper SSDs and HBF.
  • Vendor numbers here are marketing claims as reported by the press, not measured — see Source notes.
Five everyday pictures

How to think about the 2026 moves

Each stands alone; each ends with an honest note on where it stops being true.

Move 1 · High-Bandwidth Flash

An express aisle in the warehouse

Your fast picking-shelf (HBM) is tiny; the warehouse (SSD) is vast but a forklift ride away. HBF adds a wide express aisle right behind the counter — far more stock than the shelf, reachable almost as fast, because it's stacked in the same room (UCIe, in-package) instead of across the yard. You still keep the hottest items on the shelf, but now the "almost-hot" tier isn't a forklift trip.

Where it breaks: flash isn't DRAM — writes wear it out and are slow, so the express aisle is mostly read-optimized (weights, KV-cache reads), not a scratchpad you rewrite constantly.
Move 2 · CXL pooling

A shared motor pool

Give every household its own car (DRAM bolted to each server) and most sit idle in driveways while one family is stuck. A shared motor pool (CXL memory pool) keeps the same total cars but hands them to whoever needs one right now. No new cars are built — you just stop stranding capacity, so the fleet does more work.

Where it breaks: the pooled car is a short drive away, not in your garage — CXL pool latency is higher than local DRAM, so it suits colder/shareable data, not the hottest working set.
Move 3 · Near-memory compute

Do the arithmetic in the archive

If a clerk must haul every ledger box to head office to add a column, the hallway (the host link) is the bottleneck. Cheaper to send a swarm of interns into the archive (3,072 small RISC-V cores beside the DDR5) to add the columns where the boxes already sit, and mail back only the totals. Each intern is slow, but they never fight over the hallway — so total throughput soars for bandwidth-bound sums.

Where it breaks: interns are dim — for work that needs one brilliant mathematician (latency-bound, branchy, single-thread), the archive swarm loses badly to a real GPU/CPU. It's a bandwidth play, not a general one.
Fast tier · zHBM & hybrid bonding

Same bricks, better mortar

You can't change the bricks (DRAM cells), but you can stack them tighter and wire them smarter. zHBM and hybrid bonding are re-mortaring the same HBM wall — thinner joints (no solder bumps), a smarter base floor (in-house base die) — to pull more bandwidth and less heat from the tier you already own, right up against a hard height ceiling.

Where it breaks: better mortar has limits — it doesn't add capacity, so it eases the bandwidth wall while leaving the capacity wall for HBF/CXL to handle.
Raw material · 4D NAND

Move the plumbing to the basement

A tower with pipes running through every floor wastes living space. 4D NAND / wafer bonding puts all the control plumbing (CMOS logic) on a separate basement wafer bonded underneath, so all 375 floors above are pure apartments (cells). More bits per wafer, better power — the cheaper, denser NAND that both commodity SSDs and HBF are built from.

Where it breaks: taller towers strain the elevators — more layers means harder etching, string-current limits, and read/program timing pressure, so density gains fight physics each generation.

Hot Chips 2026 — the silicon

★ XCENA MX1 — near-memory compute on a CXL Type-3 device

The most on-topic talk for your work. XCENA (with Samsung) showed a CXL Type-3 device that fuses three ideas: memory expansion, SSD-backed byte-addressable memory, and near-memory processing. Reported specs:

aspectMX1 (as reported)
Compute fabric3,072 RISC-V cores · 24 subsystems × 128 · in-order @1.1 GHz · 32-core clusters share 128 KB L2 · 4 KB L1/core · custom vector · 2× Cortex-A53 control

Why it matters to you: it's a working embodiment of tiered memory + near-data compute — DRAM caching an SSD tier presented as byte-addressable CXL memory, with a page-mapped cache and pinning policy that reads like an OS memory-management design rendered in silicon. The 200-vs-64 GFLOPS figure is the whole argument for computing behind the host link rather than in front of it.

Samsung zHBM & HBM4E

Samsung pushed the fast tier: zHBM reportedly delivers ~70% higher power efficiency and ~230% greater DRAM bandwidth versus a standard HBM4e stack, and the company is readying HBM4E at 16 Gbps/pin (up from 14). It also spotlighted in-house base-die design as the lever behind its HBM4 position. All bandwidth/efficiency figures are Samsung's claims.

SK hynix — advanced packaging & HBM5

SK hynix detailed the packaging roadmap: extending MR-MUF through the NVIDIA Rubin generation, evaluating Intel EMIB, and moving toward hybrid bonding for HBM5 and 3D structures as stacks approach the ~775 µm height ceiling. Read this as "keep wringing bandwidth and thermals out of HBM while the new tiers grow up."

FMS 2026 — the memory/storage roadmap

★ High-Bandwidth Flash (HBF) — the first standard

The headline. SK hynix and SanDisk published the first HBF specification via the Open Compute Project — an open standard for NAND stacked and wired like HBM. Reported envelope: capacities up to 512 GB per device, bandwidth 0.4–3.0 TB/s, over UCIe, placed in-package beside the compute. Positioned between HBM and SSD: HBM-like throughput at ~an order of magnitude more capacity, at lower cost than HBM or DRAM. Google is publicly backing it; notably, Samsung was reported as not part of the open spec, hinting at a possible standards split.

◆
Why HBF is a tiering story, not just a chip. Flash can't be a DRAM replacement — its writes are slow and wear out. HBF's home is read-mostly, capacity-hungry, bandwidth-hungry data: model weights and large KV-caches streamed during inference. That makes placement and migration policy — which bytes live in HBM vs HBF vs CXL vs SSD — exactly the OS/runtime tiering problem you study, now with a new rung to schedule.

SK hynix "Tiered Memory" + CMM-Ax PIM + CXL pooling

SK hynix framed its whole pitch as Tiered Memory: rather than one hero product, orchestrate HBM, server DRAM, HBF, and SSD together. Alongside it: CMM-Ax, a processing-in-memory part aimed at efficient long-context LLM serving, and a CXL pooled-memory demo accelerating agentic ReAct serving versus legacy systems. Some press assigned tier labels (e.g. "G0.5" 3D-stacked DRAM at near-SRAM bandwidth, "G1.5" HBF at ~1 TB / Tb/s-scale, "G2.5" CXL-pooled multi-TB); treat those specific names as press shorthand rather than confirmed official nomenclature.

NAND scaling — 375 layers & record density

The raw material advanced on schedule. SK hynix showed 10th-gen, 375-layer 4D NAND (wafer-bonded, data-center-optimized) claiming ~2.5× perf/watt over the prior generation. Kioxia and SanDisk pushed BiCS10 to ~37 Gb/mm² QLC and ~29 Gb/mm² TLC at 332 layers, with interface speeds up to 4,800 MT/s. Denser, faster NAND is what makes both cheaper SSDs and viable HBF.

◇
The through-line. Both shows are converging on the same picture: a continuum of memory from on-package HBM/HBF down to pooled CXL and SSD, with compute sprinkled along it. "Memory" and "storage" are becoming one tiered fabric — which is why FMS now calls itself the Future of Memory and Storage, not Flash Memory Summit.
Put numbers on it

Worked examples

Back-of-envelope calculations that make the 2026 moves concrete. Figures are illustrative / vendor-reported; the arithmetic is exact.

Example 1 — the capacity wall in one sum

Serve a 1-trillion-parameter model at 8-bit weights, plus KV-cache for many users.

  1. Weights: 1e12 params × 1 byte = 1 TB just for weights, before any activations or KV-cache.
  2. HBM you'd need: at ~64 GB per HBM stack, 1 TB / 64 GB ≈ 16 stacks — i.e. several top-end GPUs bought largely for their memory, not their FLOPS.
  3. With HBF: a single ~512 GB HBF device holds half the model at HBM-ish bandwidth; two of them hold the weights in the fast-ish tier at a fraction of the HBM cost, freeing HBM for the latency-critical KV-cache.
◆
Takeaway: the expensive part of "serving a big model" is increasingly capacity at bandwidth, not compute. HBF and CXL exist to stop you buying FLOPS you won't use just to get bytes you will.

Example 2 — why near-memory beats "pull it over the link"

The XCENA argument, in round numbers. A bandwidth-bound reduction over 2 TB of DDR5.

  1. Host-side path: an external accelerator must pull operands across the CXL 3.2 ×8 link — ~64 GB/s usable each way — so its throughput is link-capped at ~64 GFLOPS for this bandwidth-bound work (reported).
  2. Near-memory path: the on-device RISC-V swarm reads DDR5 over the device's internal buses (far wider than the ×8 host link), hitting 200+ GFLOPS — ~3×+ — without the operands ever crossing the host link.
  3. The lever: it's not more FLOPS, it's FLOPS per byte crossing the thin link. Move the compute to the data and the link stops being the ceiling.
▲
Misconception it kills: "a CXL device is just slow memory." With compute on it, the right metric isn't its link bandwidth but how much work it finishes without using the link. For the wrong (latency-bound, single-thread) workload, though, those 1.1 GHz in-order cores lose to a real CPU — near-memory is a scalpel, not a hammer.

Check yourself

Practice

Answer before opening. Each answer ends with the misconception it's built to catch.

Warm-up
In one sentence each, which wall — bandwidth or capacity — does HBF primarily attack, and which does CXL pooling primarily attack?
Show answer
HBF primarily attacks the capacity-at-bandwidth gap: HBM-ish throughput but ~8× the capacity, in-package. CXL pooling primarily attacks the capacity wall (and stranding): more usable TB by sharing existing DRAM across hosts, at higher latency. Catches: lumping all "new memory" together. Each move targets a specific wall; HBF is bandwidth-preserving capacity, CXL is capacity-via-sharing.
Diagnose
Why did HBF's designers choose UCIe rather than CXL/PCIe as its interconnect, and what does that choice reveal about its intended role?
Show answer
UCIe is a die-to-die, in-package interconnect: millimeter reach, huge aggregate bandwidth, low energy/bit. Choosing it means HBF is meant to sit right next to the compute like HBM, bandwidth-first — not hang off a cable like an SSD or a CXL expander. The interconnect encodes the intent: HBF is a fast in-package tier, not remote capacity. Catches: assuming interconnects are interchangeable plumbing. Reach and energy/bit define the role — UCIe = in-package/bandwidth, CXL/PCIe = out-of-package/capacity-pooling.
Diagnose
XCENA's MX1 reports ~200 GFLOPS on-device but only ~64 GFLOPS for an external accelerator on the same CXL link. The MX1's cores are slow, in-order, 1.1 GHz. How can the "weaker" device win?
Show answer
Because the workload is bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound. The external accelerator is throttled by the ~64 GB/s host link it must pull operands through; the MX1's cores read DDR5 over the device's much wider internal buses, so they're fed far faster. Throughput is set by bytes delivered, and near-memory delivers more. Catches: ranking devices by peak core performance. For memory-bound work, effective bandwidth to the operands — not FLOPS or clock — sets the ceiling.
Design
You run an LLM inference service and gain an HBF tier between HBM and SSD. Sketch a placement policy: what data goes in HBM, HBF, CXL-pooled DRAM, and SSD, and why?
Show answer
HBM: the hottest, latency-critical, write-touched state — active KV-cache for in-flight requests, current-layer activations. HBF: read-mostly, bandwidth-hungry, large-but-static data — model weights and warm KV-cache read repeatedly (flash's slow writes/wear are fine for read-dominant weights). CXL-pooled DRAM: shareable, colder capacity that benefits from de-stranding across hosts — overflow KV-cache, less-active models. SSD: cold checkpoints, rarely-touched models, spillover. The runtime migrates by access temperature and read/write ratio — your tiering problem, now four-tiered. Catches: treating HBF as "just more HBM." Its write penalty and endurance make it a read tier; putting write-hot state there would wear it out and stall.
Design
Samsung's zHBM claims "+230% bandwidth" and HBF claims "TB/s-class." If both land, does the memory wall go away? Argue both sides.
Show answer
Eases, doesn't vanish. For: more HBM bandwidth + a high-capacity HBF tier directly lifts both walls, and near-memory compute cuts how much must cross links at all. Against: model/context sizes and concurrency are growing faster than any one generation's gains; flash writes/endurance cap HBF's role; CXL/HBF add latency and placement complexity; and the wall is fundamentally that compute scales faster than memory — new tiers move the bottleneck around rather than deleting it. The realistic outcome is a deeper, better-scheduled hierarchy, not a flat fast memory. Catches: reading vendor percentage claims as "problem solved." A tier that's 3× better still sits below compute's growth curve; hierarchy management is the durable answer, not any single tier.
Loose ends

Frequently asked

The questions that come up once the three moves click.

QIs HBF just an SSD glued closer, or something new?▾
It's NAND flash, but architected like HBM: stacked dies, a very wide interface, in-package over UCIe — engineered for parallel bandwidth rather than an SSD's serial, block, far-away access. Same storage medium, radically different packaging and access model, aimed at a different tier. Think "flash reorganized to behave like slow, huge, cheap HBM," best for read-mostly data.
QHow is this different from Optane / 3D XPoint, which also tried to fill the gap?▾
Same goal (a tier between DRAM and SSD), different bet. Optane was a new medium (phase-change) offering byte-addressable persistence at DRAM-ish latency, and died on cost/ecosystem. HBF instead reuses mainstream NAND (cheap, scaling to 375 layers) and wins on bandwidth and capacity via HBM-style packaging, not low latency — a supply-chain-friendlier attack on the same gap, and one that leans on CXL/tiering software that now exists.
QWhy is Samsung reportedly outside the HBF spec — does that matter?▾
Reported, and worth watching. An open OCP spec backed by SK hynix, SanDisk, and Google sets a de-facto standard; a major vendor staying out risks a competing approach or a standards split (as happened in other memory standards). For you it's a roadmap-risk flag, not a technical verdict — track whether the spec consolidates before betting a design on HBF availability/interop.
QDoes any of this need OS / kernel changes, or is it transparent?▾
Mostly it needs software. CXL memory already surfaces via the kernel's tiered-memory machinery (promotion/demotion, NUMA-like nodes); HBF as a new tier will want the same placement/migration logic extended to a fourth rung with a read/write-aware policy. Near-memory devices (XCENA) need runtime/driver support to offload the right kernels. This is precisely where OS-managed tiering research (your area) meets the 2026 hardware.

Yes. This guide is built from public press coverage because the official Hot Chips/FMS materials are still attendee-gated. Hot Chips opens its decks and videos to everyone around early December 2026; a scheduled poll (HotChips2026Poll) will drop them into the harness queue then, and the harness will build deeper, slide-grounded guides per talk.
