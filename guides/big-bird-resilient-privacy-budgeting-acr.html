SOSP 2026 · A Field Guide

Spending a shared privacy budget on real clicks, not on Sybil domains

The W3C's new ad-measurement standard promises differential privacy, one budget per advertiser. This is the plain-English story of why that promise quietly breaks — and how Big Bird makes a single shared budget both sound and safe from denial-of-service.

📄 "Big Bird" · Tholoniat, Caulfield, et al. (SOSP '26) 🎓 Some DP background assumed — primers cover it ⏱ ~30 min read
🐦

A domain may draw on the shared privacy budget only in proportion to the genuine user actions it can produce across distinct sites — never in proportion to how many spare domains it can register.

1What a privacy budget (ε) actually limits▾

Differential privacy adds calibrated noise so that any single person's data barely moves the output. ε (epsilon) is the dial: small ε means strong protection but noisier answers; each query "spends" some ε, and once a budget is exhausted, further queries must be refused or return nothing informative.

Attribution enforces this on the device: your browser, not a server, tracks how much ε you have left and decides whether to answer.

Now you're ready: the whole paper is about who shares which ε budget, and how to stop that sharing from being gamed.
2Individual DP and sparsity (Cookie Monster's trick)▾

Traditional DP charges every query a worst-case amount, even for users who contributed nothing relevant. Individual DP (IDP) instead charges based on what a user's device actually holds: if a device has zero impressions relevant to a conversion, that epoch costs it zero privacy loss.

This matters for ads because most impressions never convert and most conversions have no matching impression — IDP means those non-events are free.

Now you're ready: Big Bird builds directly on top of this device-epoch IDP accounting (from the authors' prior Cookie Monster paper) — it doesn't replace it, it fixes its scope.
3The Attribution cast of characters▾

An impression site (e.g. a news site) shows an ad and calls saveImpression(). A conversion site (e.g. a shop) later calls measureConversion() to ask "which ads led here?" An intermediary (an ad-tech company) may also request its own report. An aggregation service combines many devices' encrypted reports and adds DP noise via MPC or a TEE.

The browser mediates everything: raw impressions never leave the device; only noisy aggregates do.

Now you're ready: a querier is whoever receives a report — today, mainly the advertiser measuring its own conversions.
4Privacy filters and composition▾

A privacy filter is a running total: each query proposes to spend some ε; the filter accepts if the running total plus the new charge stays under capacity, and rejects (or the browser silently drops the contribution) otherwise. Composing many queries just means their ε's add up against the same capacity.

Filters can be layered: a per-querier filter, a per-site filter, a global filter — each capped independently, each drawing on the same underlying user data.

Now you're ready: the paper shows that stacking independent filters (per-querier + global) is exactly where things go wrong.
5Per-querier vs. global enforcement scope▾

Per-querier enforcement gives each advertiser its own ε budget, so no single advertiser can over-collect. Global enforcement instead gives one shared ε budget across every querier on that device-epoch, bounding total leakage regardless of who's asking.

Per-querier feels natural — advertisers are separate businesses — but the paper's sharper point is that a per-querier guarantee can fail even for a single querier's own view: adaptive data generation across queriers or a shared safety-limit side channel can leak more than that querier's own ε permits, with no explicit combining or collusion required.

Now you're ready: the paper's first result is that per-querier guarantees are unsound even without any advertiser colluding with another.
6Why "device-epoch" is the privacy unit▾

Each device's data is split into epochs (e.g. one week each). Privacy accounting happens separately per (device, epoch) pair: one week's budget exhaustion doesn't touch next week's. This bounds how far any single decision's consequences can spread in time.

A defense that lets one epoch's outcome influence another epoch's budget silently breaks this isolation — a trap the paper calls out explicitly.

Now you're ready: Big Bird's whole quota design is built to never let a check in epoch A affect epoch B.
7Denial-of-service on a privacy budget▾

This isn't a network flood. Because any web domain can call the Attribution API, an attacker who registers many domains (a Sybil attack) can rack up fake privacy-loss charges against a user's shared global budget, exhausting it before a legitimate merchant ever gets to measure anything.

The victim isn't the attacker's own data — it's every benign querier who now gets null reports on that device-epoch.

Now you're ready: global enforcement fixes the privacy-soundness problem but opens exactly this new availability problem — which Big Bird is designed to bound, under assumptions spelled out in Theorem 4.1.
8Atomic transactions (two-phase commit)▾

When one action must check and deduct from several budgets at once (querier, global, site quotas), you want all-or-nothing: either every budget has room and all get charged, or none do. Checking budgets one at a time can waste capacity on a request that ultimately fails elsewhere.

A two-phase commit (2PC) — check everything, then commit everything — avoids that waste and, as a side effect, makes a single low-quota Sybil cheap to reject.

Now you're ready: Big Bird's core enforcement step is exactly this kind of atomic multi-budget check.
The whole story in one place

Big Bird in a nutshell

Two contributions: a proof that the current scope is unsound, and a mechanism that fixes the scope without opening a new attack.

Contribution 1. Attribution enforces individual differential privacy (IDP) per querier — each advertiser gets its own device-epoch ε budget. The paper proves this is unsound in a general multi-querier system model, in two ways that don't require collusion: (1) when new data can depend on past query results across queriers (streaming adaptivity), a querier's view can leak more than its own ε permits (Thm. 3.1); (2) when a shared "safety limit" is layered on top of per-querier budgets — exactly what Attribution does today as defense-in-depth — that shared limit itself becomes a side channel that leaks across queriers (Thm. 3.2). Both excess-leakage bounds grow linearly in the number of queriers.

The fix in scope is to enforce IDP globally, across all queriers jointly, rather than per querier. But global enforcement introduces a new, purely operational problem: in an open system where any web domain can call the API, a shared budget is a target for Sybil depletion — cheaply-registered fake domains that drain a user's global budget from a single visit, locking out every legitimate advertiser for that device-epoch.

Contribution 2. Big Bird bounds attacker impact by tying budget consumption to the stock-and-flow structure of genuine Attribution use: impressions create potential loss (stock), conversions realize it (flow), and meaningful consumption should scale with real user actions across distinct sites — not with how many domains an attacker owns. It enforces this with privacy-loss-based quotas on impression sites and conversion sites, plus a small cap on how many new quotas one user action can create, all checked atomically per epoch. The guarantee (Thm. 4.1) is conditional: it assumes an attacker can't simultaneously scale domains, user actions, and distinct users, and ecosystem-level governance is still needed to keep Sybil registration and user-activation abuse in check.

The payoff: under a 25-domain Sybil attack on real Criteo ad-tech data, an unprotected global budget lets median measurement error balloon from 5.2% to 48.5% (a 9.3× hit). Big Bird, with default quotas, holds it to 5.5% — within noise of the no-attack baseline — while still proving rigorous global device-epoch IDP (Thm. 4.2) and a formal resilience bound (Thm. 4.1).

🐦 Key takeaways

  • Per-querier IDP is unsound without collusion, via cross-querier data adaptivity (Thm. 3.1) or shared safety limits (Thm. 3.2) — excess leakage grows linearly in the number of queriers.
  • The fix is scope, not accounting: enforce IDP globally (Thm. 4.2), and Big Bird proves this remains sound under a comprehensive system model.
  • Global budgets are vulnerable to Sybil DoS depletion — the new problem global enforcement introduces.
  • Legitimate workloads have a stock-and-flow shape: impressions (stock) then conversions (flow), tied to real user actions across distinct sites.
  • Big Bird enforces this with impression-site quotas, conversion-site quotas, and a per-action domain cap (κ), checked atomically (Thm. 4.1).
  • On Criteo data under a 25-Sybil attack: unprotected global budget → 48.5% median error; Big Bird → 5.5% (baseline is 5.2%).
  • Both results are now part of the W3C Attribution draft standard, resolving a blocker to its Wide-Area Review milestone.
Seven everyday pictures

How to think about Big Bird

Read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the picture stops being true.

The core idea · Stock and flow

A rain barrel and a tap

Rain (impressions) fills a barrel — that's potential, not yet spent. Nothing happens until someone opens the tap (a conversion): only then does water actually leave. Budget should only drain when both a barrel has rain and a tap gets opened — never just because someone claims to have a barrel.

Where it breaks: a real barrel can only be filled once; a device can accumulate many small, low-value impressions across many sites in one epoch, so Big Bird still needs a per-site cap on how much any one barrel can hold (the impression-site quota).
Failure mode 1 · Cross-querier adaptivity

Notes passed through a shared notebook

Two students each glance at a shared notebook and write a hint based on what they see, without ever talking to each other. A third student, reading the notebook later, can piece together information neither of the first two "colluded" to share — the notebook itself was the leak. Attribution's data stream can play this role across queriers: one querier's past results shape new data, and a later querier reads more than its own budget should allow (Thm. 3.1).

Where it breaks: no student intends to leak anything — that's the point. The analogy fails if you assume leakage requires intent; DP's worst-case analysis has to account for it regardless.
Failure mode 2 · Shared safety limits

A "sold out" sign that leaks who's shopping

A shop keeps one shared "sold out" sign for a popular item, watched by many separate customers with their own accounts. Whether your order goes through depends on whether earlier shoppers — acting on their own information — already tripped the sign. Even without talking to them, you can infer something about what they saw. Attribution's shared safety limit acts the same way: whether your query is accepted leaks information about other queriers' results (Thm. 3.2).

Where it breaks: under Attribution's IDP, rejections aren't directly visible (no explicit "sold out" signal) — the paper's proof for this case is subtler, inferring the leak through which contributions silently vanish rather than an overt rejection.
Sybils · Why rate-limiting API calls fails

A thousand loyalty cards, one shopper

Rate-limiting how many times a domain can call the API is like capping how many purchases a loyalty card can log — but a determined shopper just prints a thousand cards. What actually matters is how much that shopper spends from their one wallet (their real budget), not how many cards they're carrying. Big Bird caps privacy-loss consumption, not API calls, precisely so minting more "cards" (domains) doesn't help.

Where it breaks: a wallet has one fixed balance; a device's global budget resets every epoch, so the shopper (attacker) gets a fresh wallet each week — bounding damage per-epoch, not forever.
Enforcement · The per-action domain cap

A boarding pass covers two connecting flights

A single trip might reasonably involve one layover — two flights, two airlines, tracked under one itinerary. It doesn't reasonably involve fifty airlines. Big Bird's per-action domain cap (κ) works the same way: one intentional user action (a click, a page visit) may legitimately touch a couple of distinct sites via a redirect, but a benign user action is not expected to traverse dozens of domains — so new quotas beyond κ simply aren't created.

Where it breaks: a real itinerary is booked once, in full, upfront; Big Bird's cap is enforced incrementally, per epoch, as sites happen to be visited — it can't know in advance which domains a "trip" will touch.
Utility trade-off · Atomic 2PC

Splitting a restaurant bill

Four friends share one bill: querier, global, conversion-site, and impression-site budgets. Big Bird checks that all four can cover their share before anyone pays — if one friend's card is declined, no one's card gets charged for that item. This is why a single depleted impression-site quota can zero out an otherwise-fine report: the transaction is all-or-nothing by design.

Where it breaks: real diners can renegotiate who pays what; Big Bird can't finely reallocate — if multiple relevant impression sites are involved and just one is out of quota, the whole epoch's contribution is dropped even though other sites still had room (a known utility cost, left to future work).
Device-epoch IDP · Why caps can't cross epochs

Sealed envelopes, one per week

Imagine your weekly allowance arrives in a sealed envelope: what you do with week 1's money can't reach into week 3's envelope. A DoS cap that instead tracks "total spending across all your envelopes so far" secretly lets week 1's activity decide whether week 3's contribution counts at all — breaking the seal. Big Bird's per-action domain cap is tracked separately for every epoch, precisely to keep each envelope sealed.

Where it breaks: real envelopes are handed out on a fixed schedule; here the "cap exceeded" check inside one epoch can still zero out just that epoch's data without penalizing others — it's a per-epoch failure, not a per-user one.
The full explanation

How it actually works

From the unsoundness proofs to the deployed algorithm, with the paper's key structures rebuilt as diagrams.

1 · Why per-querier IDP is unsound

The paper models a generic multi-querier DP system (Alg. 1): K queriers, each with its own per-querier filter, plus an optional shared limit L, operating over data that a process G can generate adaptively from all queriers' past results. Two conditions matter: (A) does new data depend on other queriers' past outputs, and (B) is a shared limit enforced on top of per-querier filters?

2 · Global device-epoch IDP: sound, but now a DoS target

A global budget has no analogous outside channel — cross-querier information is already inside the quantity being bounded. Theorem 4.2 proves Big Bird's full design satisfies global device-epoch εglobal-DP under a comprehensive model (individual DP, on-device report generation, adaptive queries and data, multiple epochs and queriers, atomic multi-budget transactions). But an open system means any domain can call the API — so the global budget itself becomes a denial-of-service target for Sybil domains (§4.1).

⚠
Naïve defenses fail. Rate-limiting API calls ignores that most calls cost zero privacy loss under IDP (§4.2). Rate-limiting total consumption blocks bursts but not paced, slow-drip attacks. Capping consumption per user action helps but still lets one popular site drain budget over many interactions. Capping consumption across epochs is the most tempting fix — and it silently violates device-epoch IDP (Appendix C).

3 · The stock-and-flow insight and Big Bird's quota architecture

Legitimate Attribution use has structure: a user visits an impression site (creating stock), then later visits a conversion site (triggering flow). Non-zero privacy loss requires both steps, on distinct sites, from real user actions. Attacks break this pattern by automating stock creation and flow triggering — many Sybil "sites" acting as both, from a single click.

4 · The algorithm, atomically

Each measureConversion() call processes every relevant epoch separately. For each epoch: check the per-action domain cap; if exceeded, drop only that epoch's data. Otherwise, match impressions, compute the device-epoch privacy loss and the finer device-epoch-impression-site loss, then attempt to deduct from the querier filter, the global filter, the conversion-site quota, and every relevant impression-site quota — all as one atomic transaction. If any one deduction would fail, none commit, and that epoch's data is dropped with zero budget impact.

# simplified from Alg. 2
def measureConversion(uaCtx, impSites, querier, convSite, params):
    for e in params.attributionWindow:
        # per-action domain cap, checked per epoch
        if |accessedSites[uaCtx][e] ∪ {convSite}| > κ_action:
            I_e ← ∅; continue          # drop only this epoch
        accessedSites[uaCtx][e] ∪= {convSite}

        I_e ← MatchingFn(uaCtx, ImpressionStore[e], params)
        ε_e ← computeEpochLoss(I_e, params)            # flow charge
        ε_ie ← {computeEpochImpSiteLoss(I_e[i]) for i}  # stock charge

        # atomic 2PC across all four budgets
        if querierBudget.tryConsume(ε_e) and globalBudget.tryConsume(ε_e)
           and convQuota[convSite].tryConsume(ε_e)
           and all(impQuota[i].tryConsume(ε_ie[i]) for i):
            commit()
        else:
            abort(); I_e ← ∅                            # all-or-nothing
    return AttributionFn(I_e1, ..., I_ew)
💡
Why per-epoch, never cross-epoch? If the per-action cap instead aggregated across the whole report's attribution window, one epoch's outcome would shape another epoch's privacy accounting — exactly the device-epoch IDP violation in Appendix C. Big Bird's cap check and its consequence (zeroing data) both live strictly inside a single epoch.

5 · Sizing the quotas from real workload data

Capacities are derived, not guessed, from four utility constraints: a conversion site must spend its full querier budget (ε_conv-quota ≥ (1+r)·ε_querier); no single conversion site should drain the global budget (ε_global ≥ N·ε_conv-quota); no single impression site should either (ε_global ≥ M·ε_imp-quota); and fan-out across n conversion sites bounds impression-site quotas (ε_imp-quota ≥ n·ε_conv-quota). Minimizing ε_global under these constraints gives closed-form capacities from three inputs: ε_querier, r (intermediary fraction), and conservative per-device estimates of N, M, n from production data.

6 · Resilience, in three steps (Theorem 4.1)

(i) Even with unlimited user actions, an attacker's consumption from the global budget on one device-epoch is capped by how many quotas they've managed to create: εadvglobal ≤ min(Madv·ε_imp-quota, Nadv·ε_conv-quota). (ii) Quota creation itself is capped by genuine user actions through κ_action, giving εadvglobal ≤ (1+r)·ε_querier·κ_action·Uadv. (iii) Because Attribution reports have bounded sensitivity Δ, even fully depleting k device-epochs only moves any aggregate query by at most m·k·Δ, where m bounds reports per device.

Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four traces using the paper's real parameters — a device-epoch charge, a quota-sizing calculation, a resilience bound, and a utility trade-off.

① Charging a single conversion (Table 1's example)

A conversion on shoes.ex requests a report with ε=0.2, value=75, maxValue=150. Relevant impressions sit in epochs e1 (news.ex) and e2 (blog.ex); the conversion itself is in e3.

  1. Per-epoch charge = (value / maxValue) × ε = (75/150) × 0.2 = 0.1, for each epoch that has a relevant impression.
  2. e1 and e2 each have exactly one relevant impression (news.ex, blog.ex respectively) — each charges 0.1 to the querier filter, the global filter, and shoes.ex's conv-quota.
  3. Impression-site quotas: only news.ex's imp-quota is charged 0.1 in e1; only blog.ex's imp-quota is charged 0.1 in e2 — books.ex, social.ex, etc. are untouched since they had nothing relevant here.
  4. e3 has no relevant impressions, so it charges 0 everywhere — this is the sparsity benefit IDP gives Attribution.
  5. Total drawn from the global budget for this report: 0.1 + 0.1 + 0 = 0.2, out of a starting capacity of 4.
🐦
Insight: the same computation runs again, independently, if adtech.ex requests its own report (j) on the same conversion — deducting 0.2 more from the global budget and from adtech.ex's own querier filter, but not from shoes.ex's.

② Sizing quotas from Criteo's p85 workload

Step (2) of quota configuration estimates N=4 conversion sites, M=2 impression sites, n=4 fan-out per device at the 85th percentile, with ε_querier=1 and r=0 (single-advertiser evaluation).

  1. ε_conv-quota = (1+r)·ε_querier = (1+0)×1 = 1.
  2. ε_imp-quota = n·(1+r)·ε_querier = 4×1×1 = 4.
  3. ε_global = max(N, n·M)·(1+r)·ε_querier = max(4, 4×2)×1 = max(4, 8) = 8.
  4. Check it's tight: a single conversion site can consume at most N·ε_conv-quota = 4×1 = 4 ≤ 8 ✓; a single impression site can consume at most M·ε_imp-quota = 2×4 = 8 ≤ 8 ✓ (this constraint is the binding one).
💡
The evaluation actually deploys ε_imp-quota = 2 (tighter than this table's 4) — Fig. 5 shows this halves resilience-relevant slack in ε_global at a negligible utility cost, since real devices' impression-site fan-out is usually well below the p85 upper bound.

③ How many user actions does it take to drain a global budget?

Default config: ε_querier=1, r=0, ε_imp-quota=2, ε_conv-quota=1, ε_global=8, κ_action=2. How many genuine-looking user actions must an attacker collect before they can fully drain one device-epoch's global budget?

  1. Per-action new quotas: each user action can activate at most κ_action=2 new conversion-site quotas (Thm. 4.1(ii)).
  2. Budget unlocked per action = (1+r)·ε_querier·κ_action = 1×1×2 = 2 (using the conversion-site bound, the tighter of the two in this config since ε_conv-quota=1 < ε_imp-quota=2).
  3. Actions needed to reach ε_global = 8: 8 ÷ 2 = 4 user actions, i.e. Uadv ≥ 4 — this matches Thm. 4.1(ii)'s bound exactly, since ε_global = (1+r)ε_querier·κ_action·Uadv at Uadv=4.
  4. Compare to the naïve attack (Fig. 2a): without Big Bird, x.ex could exhaust the same 8-unit budget from a single visit using 8/ε_querier = 8 Sybils, no real user actions required at all.
🐦
Insight: Big Bird doesn't make depletion impossible — it makes depletion cost real, distinct user visits, which is exactly the resource an ad-tech attacker can't cheaply mint the way they can mint domain names.

④ Reading the quota-size vs. utility trade-off (Fig. 5)

Sweeping ε_imp-quota at fixed ε_global=8 reveals a U-shaped trade-off — too small hurts benign utility, too large hurts resilience.

  1. ε_imp-quota = 0.5 (too tight): even with no attacker, 16.2% of benign reports get zeroed by the impression-site quota — median error jumps from the 5.2% baseline to 14.0%, a 2.7× increase.
  2. ε_imp-quota = 2 (the sweet spot): benign error returns to baseline (5.2%, no attack); under the random attacker, error stays near baseline (5.5%); fewer than 1% of benign reports are blocked by global depletion.
  3. ε_imp-quota = 3 (too loose): no benign cost, but now an attacker can route enough traffic through Sybils to matter — median error under attack rises to 12.5%, with ~11% of benign reports blocked by global depletion instead of quotas.
  4. ε_imp-quota = 5: under attack, error plateaus near 20.1%, with ~19%+ of reports blocked by global-budget exhaustion — the quota is now loose enough that it barely constrains the attacker at all.
💡
The lesson generalizes: a quota is a dial, not a fixed constant. Too tight throttles honest workload — as this sweep shows, "no attack" error is only 5.2% for a well-chosen quota, not for any defense — while too loose gives quotas back their pre-Big-Bird failure mode.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
Two advertisers never share data, code, or infrastructure, and neither knows the other exists. Can per-querier IDP still be unsound for them?
Show answer
Yes. Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 3.2 construct excess leakage that requires no collusion at all — it comes from a shared data stream (adaptivity) or a shared safety-limit filter, mechanisms that exist purely at the system level, independent of what the advertisers intend or know about each other.Misconception: that "unsound without collusion" must mean a modeling error in the proof. It's the opposite — the earlier proofs were sound for an incomplete model; adding realistic behaviors (adaptivity, shared limits) is what reveals the leak.
Problem 2 · medium
A conversion requests ε=0.4, value=30, maxValue=120. Two epochs have relevant impressions; a third does not. What's the total privacy loss charged to the global budget?
Show answer
Per-epoch charge = (value/maxValue)×ε = (30/120)×0.4 = 0.1 for each epoch with a relevant impression. Two such epochs → 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.2 total. The third epoch, with no relevant impression, charges 0 regardless of the requested ε.Misconception: that ε=0.4 is charged per conversion regardless of relevance. IDP charges only epochs that actually contain matching data — that's the sparsity optimization Cookie Monster introduced.
Problem 3 · hard
Why can't Big Bird's per-action domain cap simply track "total quotas activated by this user action across its whole attribution window" instead of tracking it separately per epoch?
Show answer
Because a report can span multiple epochs, and any check that reasons jointly about those epochs couples their outcomes — and therefore their privacy losses. Device-epoch IDP requires that epoch e's privacy loss depend only on epoch e's data. A cross-epoch cap check means epoch A's activity (many sites touched) can cause epoch B's contribution to be silently dropped, which makes epoch B's outcome depend on epoch A's data — exactly what Appendix C shows is unsound.Misconception: that any reasonable-looking DoS defense is automatically privacy-safe. Coupling checks or consequences across epochs is an easy, natural-looking way to accidentally violate device-epoch IDP.
Problem 4 · medium
Using the p90 row of the quota table (N=4, M=3, n=4, ε_querier=1, r=0), verify that ε_global=12 and ε_imp-quota=4.
Show answer
ε_imp-quota = n(1+r)ε_querier = 4×1×1 = 4 ✓. ε_global = max(N, nM)(1+r)ε_querier = max(4, 4×3)×1 = max(4,12) = 12 ✓. Here the impression-side constraint (n·M) is the binding one, not N — meaning a single popular impression site, fanned out across 4 conversion sites, is the tighter risk to the global budget at this percentile.Misconception: that ε_global is always set by the number of conversion sites N. The formula takes a max — whichever side (conversion fan-in or impression fan-out) is larger determines the bound.
Problem 5 · hard
An attacker with unlimited Sybil domains but only 3 genuine user actions faces κ_action=2 and ε_conv-quota=1 (r=0, ε_querier=1). What is the maximum global budget they can consume on one device-epoch, per Thm. 4.1(ii)?
Show answer
εadvglobal ≤ (1+r)·ε_querier·κ_action·Uadv = 1×1×2×3 = 6. Even with infinite Sybil domains registered, only 3×2=6 conversion-site quotas can ever be activated, because activation requires a genuine user action and each action unlocks at most κ_action=2 new ones.Misconception: that owning more domains always helps an attacker. Past the per-action cap, extra domains sit idle — they were never admitted into a quota in the first place.
Problem 6 · medium
Fig. 6b shows the random attacker's error barely moves (5.2%→6.4%) from 5 to 100 Sybil domains, while the omniscient attacker saturates by 10 domains and stays flat through 100. Why do both curves flatten, for different reasons?
Show answer
The random attacker flattens because the tight ε_imp-quota=2 sharply limits how much any one domain — random or not — can contribute; adding domains without tracking which ones still have quota mostly adds domains that get rejected. The omniscient attacker flattens because, with perfect knowledge of remaining quotas, ~10 domains already exhaust the available per-device quota headroom under ε_global=8; once the global budget itself is the bottleneck, additional Sybils have nothing left to consume.Misconception: that resilience against a weak attacker and resilience against a strong attacker come from the same mechanism. Here the quota size protects against the random attacker, while the global-budget bound (Thm. 4.1(i)) protects against the omniscient one.
Problem 7 · easy
Why does "Attribution w/o global budget" (Cookie Monster's original per-querier-only design) show 5.2% error even under a heavy Sybil attack — is it more resilient than Big Bird?
Show answer
It's unaffected because there's nothing shared to deplete — each querier only has its own private budget, so a Sybil conversion site draining its own filter has zero effect on any other querier. But this "resilience" is really just the absence of a global privacy guarantee at all: it's exactly the per-querier-only design that §3 proves is unsound. It's a utility ceiling, not a safe design to deploy.Misconception: that low error under attack always means a better system. Here it means the system was never protecting a global invariant to begin with — there's nothing for the attacker to break.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Tap any to expand.

Q1Is the problem that Cookie Monster's IDP accounting is wrong?▾
No. Device-epoch IDP itself is sound and is the accounting Attribution actually uses. The unsoundness is entirely about scope — enforcing that accounting separately per querier, rather than jointly across all queriers, is what breaks under realistic multi-querier system behavior.
Q2Doesn't Attribution already have global "safety limits" for this?▾
It has global safety limits as defense-in-depth against collusion, with per-querier budgets as the primary enforcement. The paper shows that this combination — per-querier budgets plus a shared limit — is itself unsound (Thm. 3.2): the shared limit becomes a cross-querier leakage channel. Big Bird instead makes the global budget the primary, sound enforcement mechanism.
Q3Why does adding a global budget create a new problem?▾
Because Attribution is an open API — any web domain can call it. A per-querier budget is naturally isolated per business; a shared global budget is a single resource anyone can draw from, including an attacker registering many throwaway domains. Fixing the privacy soundness problem (scope) directly creates an availability problem (Sybil depletion) that didn't exist before.
Q4What exactly counts as "stock" and "flow"?▾
Stock is the potential privacy loss created when an impression site records an ad view — nothing is spent yet. Flow is the realization of that potential when a conversion site requests a report and attribution actually runs. Big Bird's quotas cap each side separately (impression-site quota for stock, conversion-site quota for flow), so an attacker must both create stock and trigger flow, at bounded rates, to consume the global budget.
Q5Why not just rate-limit how often a site can call the API?▾
Because under IDP, most API calls cost zero privacy loss (most impressions never convert; most conversions have no matching impression). A call-count limit can't tell zero-cost calls from expensive ones, so it either throttles legitimate sparse usage or is set so loose an attacker operates beneath it. Big Bird limits privacy-loss consumption directly, which tracks the real resource.
Q6How does the per-action domain cap avoid breaking device-epoch IDP?▾
It's tracked and enforced strictly within a single epoch: the count of activated quotas is maintained per (user action, epoch) pair, and if the cap is exceeded in epoch e, only epoch e's data is zeroed. No check ever aggregates activity across epochs, and no failure in one epoch propagates to another — preserving the requirement that epoch e's privacy loss depends only on epoch e's data.
Q7Why is the impression-site and conversion-site budget check done atomically with the global and querier budgets?▾
Atomicity means a report either passes every relevant budget or spends none of them. This has two effects: it prevents wasting global budget on a report that would fail elsewhere anyway, and — importantly for resilience — it means a single over-quota Sybil impression is enough to nullify an entire attacker request, forcing attackers to be highly selective about what they claim, at real cost to their own effectiveness.
Q8What does Theorem 4.1 not guarantee?▾
It assumes the attacker can't simultaneously max out all three strength dimensions (domain count, user actions, distinct users) — enforcing that fully requires ecosystem-level governance (domain registration trust, stricter user-activation definitions) beyond the budget layer. It also doesn't protect quota budgets themselves from being targeted by a malicious partner site, though that damage stays localized to sites interacting with the attacker.
Q9Is Big Bird just "a global budget with rate limits bolted on"?▾
No — the paper explicitly shows that candidate approaches resembling generic rate limits (on calls, on total consumption, on consumption per action alone) each fail against some realistic attack strategy. Big Bird's quotas are shaped specifically around the stock-and-flow structure of genuine Attribution use, which is what lets them bound Sybil impact without needing to recognize specific attack tactics.
Q10What did the W3C standard actually adopt?▾
Both contributions: the specification now records the semantic limitations of per-querier enforcement as a documented limitation, and it adopted the Big Bird algorithm as its global budget-management mechanism, resolving an open issue (Sybil DoS on safety limits) that editors had flagged as a blocker to reaching Wide-Area Review — which the draft has since reached.
Q11Does this apply beyond web advertising measurement?▾
The authors argue two lessons generalize to any DP system with untrusted, multi-granularity queriers: (1) enforcing DP at multiple, independently-analyzed granularities (per-querier and shared) is fundamentally unsound when analyzed jointly, and (2) any global budget shared among untrusted participants needs depletion defenses shaped by the structure of legitimate workloads, not generic rate limits. The prototype (pdslib) is built as a general IDP library, with mobile recommendation workloads suggested as a future instantiation.
Q12What's explicitly out of scope?▾
Two things: bot-driven data poisoning (fabricating fake impressions/conversions to skew aggregates — a data-quality problem, not a budget-depletion one) and attacks that exploit direct or transitive partnerships with malicious sites (which need ecosystem governance, not budget logic). Big Bird protects the availability of measurement for genuine-user data against Sybil depletion specifically.

Glossary, results, and provenance

Every term in one place, then where the numbers came from.

Core terms
Device-epoch IDPIndividual differential privacy accounted separately for each (device, epoch) pair — the privacy unit Attribution uses.
Per-querier enforcementGiving each querier (e.g. advertiser) its own privacy budget, enforced in isolation from other queriers' budgets.
Global enforcementA single privacy budget shared across all queriers on a device-epoch, bounding total joint leakage.
Stock and flowBig Bird's structural model: impressions create potential privacy loss (stock); conversions realize it (flow).
Impression-site / conversion-site quotaPrivacy-loss-based budgets, separate from the global budget, capping how much stock or flow any one domain can contribute per epoch.
Per-action domain cap (κ_action)The maximum number of new quota-activating domains one intentional user action may unlock, enforced per epoch.
Atomic (2PC) budget checkChecking and deducting from every relevant budget (querier, global, quotas) as one all-or-nothing transaction.
Sybil depletion / DoS on a budgetUsing many cheaply-registered domains to exhaust a shared privacy budget, denying legitimate measurement — an availability attack, not a privacy attack.
Supporting terms
Privacy filterA stateful object tracking cumulative privacy loss against a fixed capacity; rejects further spend once exhausted.
Adaptive data generationNew data depending on past query results — inevitable in streaming systems, and the source of Theorem 3.1's leakage.
Shared safety limitA budget shared across queriers layered on top of per-querier budgets as defense-in-depth — the source of Theorem 3.2's leakage.
Siloing assumptionThe (unrealistic) condition under which per-querier DP would be sound: disjoint data streams and no binding shared limit.
Marginal weighted attack strengthThe paper's three-dimensional threat model: domains controlled, user actions induced, and distinct users reached.
Random / omniscient attackerEvaluation strategies: limited tracking (samples Sybils per conversion) vs. perfect knowledge of every device's remaining quota — a worst-case ceiling.
RMSREτRoot mean squared relative error across histogram buckets, clipping small counts below threshold τ; the paper's utility metric.
pdslibThe general-purpose Rust IDP library implementing Cookie Monster's and Big Bird's accounting, used as the reference implementation.
