HotStorage 2026 · A Field Guide

Every read ages the archive and spends a limited ticket

DNA can hold data for centuries — until someone reads it. This is the plain-English story of the first system model that treats pools, hydration, and read limits as first-class storage costs.

📄 "Towards A System Model for DNA-based Storage" · Schustermann et al. 🎓 No biology background assumed — the primers cover it ⏱ ~25 min read
🧬

Every read that actually reaches DNA is secretly two costs at once: it hydrates the whole pool (degrading it) and spends one of its few remaining reads. A durable digital cache can absorb reads for free — but every cache miss pays both. Policies that optimize for only one will quietly lose data.

Before you begin

Quick glossary, then six primers

The paper's vocabulary first (one line each), then the concepts behind it. Primers are collapsed — read in order, or jump to what's unfamiliar.

PoolA small container of dry DNA holding strands from many objects — the unit of storage.
PrimerA short sequence tagging an object's strands, used to selectively amplify/read just that object.
PCRPolymerase chain reaction — amplifies DNA so it can be sequenced; also the mechanism that erodes it.
HydrationDissolving dry DNA in liquid to read it; hydrated DNA decays far faster than dry DNA.
Read limitThe small number of reads a pool can serve before further PCR cycles make it undecodable.
ExpendedA pool that has hit its read limit — its remaining objects can no longer be retrieved.
EndangeredAn object whose every replica pool is expended — it can't be served until re-synthesized from content recovered via bulk-read or reserved read capacity.
ReplenishRe-synthesizing a hydrated or expended pool's objects to restore durability.
Bulk-readRetrieving an entire pool's contents at once (e.g. for replenishment) instead of one object at a time.
r-pool / r-hashReplication by duplicating whole pools (r-pool) vs. scattering each object's replicas via hashing (r-hash).

1Why DNA storage at all▾

DNA can theoretically pack hundreds of exabytes per gram and survive centuries at room temperature — orders of magnitude denser and more durable than tape or disk. That's why it's a leading candidate for cold archival storage: data written once, rarely accessed.

Now you're ready: this paper asks what happens the moment that "rarely" read actually occurs — and shows it's more costly than it looks.
2Strands, synthesis, and sequencing▾

Digital data is encoded into short DNA strands. Synthesis manufactures those strands (writing); sequencing reads them back. Both are error-prone, so encoding schemes add heavy redundancy — one reason a pool's usable capacity (~1TB) is far below the theoretical EB/gram limit.

Now you're ready: the paper abstracts all this churn into one number — "maximum logical pool capacity" — so the model stays valid as the biology improves.
3Pools and primers — the read granularity▾

Many objects' strands are stored together, dry, in one pool. Each object carries a primer tag, letting PCR selectively amplify just that object's strands for reading — but hydrating the pool to do so exposes and ages every object's strands in it, not just the one being amplified. The number of distinct primers a pool can support caps how many objects it can hold.

Now you're ready: "max objects per pool" is the model's stand-in for primer-library size, regardless of how primers are actually built.
4Why reads are destructive▾

Reading means hydrating the dry pool, extracting a sample, and discarding the rest of that sample. The leftover hydrated solution decays fast (down to a few years, even refrigerated). Repeated PCR cycles on it also accumulate errors. So: hydration ages the whole pool, and PCR cycling eventually makes it undecodable — the pool is expended.

Now you're ready: every read has two separate, compounding costs. The paper's whole model is built to track both.
5Load factor and bucket overflow▾

If objects map to pools uniformly at random, reading resembles inserting into hash buckets — a pool "overflows" (expends) once requests exceed its read limit. Classic queueing/hashing theory (Poisson arrivals) predicts this: overflow risk rises sharply once the load factor (requests ÷ total read capacity) passes 1.

Now you're ready: the paper reuses this exact intuition to explain why ~50% of pools expend right at load factor 1.
6Replication and skewed workloads▾

Replication spreads load across copies — either by duplicating whole pools (r-pool) or scattering each object's copies across different pools via hashing (r-hash), mirroring datacenter load-balancing. Workloads also matter: Uniform access spreads reads evenly; Zipf concentrates them on a few popular objects — which behave very differently under both pool expenditure and caching.

Now you're ready: the paper tests these exact variables (pool size, read limit, replication, caching) against exactly these two access patterns.
The whole story in one place

A system model for DNA storage, in a nutshell

The gap the paper fills, the model it builds, and what falls out of simulating it at scale.

DNA storage research has mostly lived at two extremes: molecular biologists improving synthesis/sequencing, and coding theorists squeezing more bits per strand. Almost nobody has modeled what happens when you manage many pools as a storage system — the way systems researchers model tape, disk, or flash.

This paper builds that model around three biologically-grounded parameters: max objects per pool (primer-library size), max pool capacity (storage density), and read limit (how many reads a pool survives before PCR errors make it undecodable). It then tracks three costs as a workload runs: how many pools get hydrated (durability degraded), how many become expended (read limit hit), and how many objects end up endangered (all replicas expended).

An event-driven simulator (100M objects, 15M requests) sweeps pool size, read limit, replication strategy, and caching across Uniform, Zipf, and Zipf-pool workloads. The headline result: today's push to improve storage density and expand primer libraries — letting pools hold more objects — makes the hydration and read-limit bottlenecks worse, not better, unless matched by proportionally larger read limits, cheap bulk-retrieval for replenishment, and DNA-aware (not just LRU) management policies.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • #1 Bigger pools reduce sensitivity to access skew, but increase replenishment cost and hydration-driven expiration risk.
  • #2 Raising the read limit is highly effective — it delays endangerment roughly in proportion to the increase.
  • #3 Load-balancing replication (r-hash) cuts near-term expenditure but spreads the "blast radius" of each expended pool across more pools, increasing long-term hydration.
  • #4 Plain LRU caching helps under skew but cannot prevent eventual replenishment, and is blind to which pools are already physically degraded.
  • #5 A system-aware cache — one that knows pool hydration/expenditure state — could do much better than LRU alone.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about DNA storage costs

Each stands alone. Every card ends with where the picture stops being true — and a few connect directly to tiering/CXL intuition.

The core cost · Hydration

A sealed jar vs. an opened one

Dry DNA is a sealed preserve jar — stable for years on a shelf. The moment you open it (read it), it starts spoiling, even refrigerated. You don't get to "read and reseal"; opening itself is the damage. That's why hydration is tracked as a cost separate from whether the read even succeeds.

Where it breaks: a jar spoils at a fixed rate once opened; a hydrated pool's decay rate also depends on how many more PCR cycles it endures, so repeated reads compound the damage further.
The second cost · Read limit = endurance

An odometer with a fixed lifespan

Each pool ships with a fixed number of "miles" (reads) before PCR noise makes it unreadable — much like NVM cells or flash have a fixed P/E-cycle endurance before bits become unreliable. A random-placement policy that doesn't track per-pool wear is like a filesystem that never load-balances writes across SSD blocks.

Where it breaks: flash wear is roughly linear and predictable; DNA read limits here are currently just "several hundred reads," with no fine-grained wear model yet — this paper's three-parameter abstraction is a first cut, not a precise curve.
Fig. 3's math · Bucket overflow

Load factor 1 is the tipping point

Pools of 100 objects with a 10%-of-size read limit behave exactly like hash buckets with capacity 10: once total requests equal total capacity (load factor = 1), roughly half the buckets have already overflowed — because arrivals aren't evenly spread, some buckets get hit much harder than average, just like Poisson arrivals.

Where it breaks: under skewed (Zipf) access the "buckets" aren't hit uniformly at all — a few pools overflow almost instantly while others stay untouched for a long time, so the clean Poisson overflow curve only applies to the Uniform workload.
Replication tradeoff · r-hash

Spreading shrapnel, not containing it

r-hash scatters each object's replicas across many pools to balance load — like spraying shrapnel instead of letting one pool absorb a hit. It works: each additional replica cuts endangerment by an order of magnitude. But when a pool does expend, its "blast radius" (how many other pools must now be hydrated to serve its displaced reads) is as wide as the scatter itself.

Where it breaks: in datacenters, blast radius is a probabilistic risk (an extra failure might compound). In DNA, it's deterministic and immediate — every newly-hydrated pool starts decaying the moment it's touched, no "if" about it.
The caching gap · Physical blindness

An elevator operator who can't see the fire alarm

LRU keeps the "popular" items near the top — but it has no idea which pool downstairs is already expended and smoking. A cold, rarely-used object whose pool just got hydrated by someone else's read is just as likely to be evicted as any other cold object, even though keeping it cached a bit longer could have shielded that fragile pool.

Where it breaks: this is exactly the gap tiering researchers know well — a page-replacement policy tuned purely for recency/frequency (like plain LRU, or even hotness-based schemes such as TPP/HeMem) is similarly blind to medium-specific wear unless explicitly made wear-aware.
Fig. 3's surprise · Zipf-pool

Sorting the crowd doesn't thin it

You'd expect grouping popular items into their own pools ("ideal placement") to spread load better — like sorting a crowd into separate lines. But applying a Zipf distribution to a small population (pools) just makes a heavier-tailed pileup: a few pools absorb even more concentrated traffic than before.

Where it breaks: the analogy suggests placement never helps — but the paper shows it does delay hydration considerably (Fig. 4), even while barely helping endangerment (Fig. 3). The two metrics can disagree.
The full explanation

How the model and simulator actually work

The abstraction, the pool lifecycle, the request-handling logic, and the policy knobs it sweeps.

1 · Three parameters stand in for all the biology

Rather than modeling synthesis chemistry or sequencing error rates directly — which change too fast to pin down — the paper picks three durable, system-visible parameters: max objects per pool (primer library size), max logical pool capacity (storage density), and read limit (durability under repeated PCR). Everything else — cost, latency, throughput of the physical processes — is explicitly out of scope "at this stage."

2 · Request-handling logic (what the simulator does per read)

on user_request(object):
    if object in cache:               # served for free, no DNA touched
        return cache.get(object)
    replica_pools = object.replica_pools
    if any(p.is_hydrated and not p.is_expended for p in replica_pools):
        pool = pick_hydrated(replica_pools)       # prefer an already-open, non-expended pool
    elif any(not p.is_expended for p in replica_pools):
        pool = hydrate(pick_dry(replica_pools))   # new hydration event
    else:
        mark_endangered(object); return FAIL     # all replicas expended
    pool.reads_used += 1
    if pool.reads_used == pool.read_limit:
        pool.expended = True                    # objects in it → endangered
    cache.insert(object)  # LRU eviction if full

Follow the numbers

Worked examples

Four calculations straight from the paper's reported numbers.

① Load factor 1 → ~50% of pools expended

n = 100M objects, pool size = 100, read limit = 10% of size. At reqs = 10% of n, why does the paper call this "load factor 1," and does the arithmetic check out?

  1. Pools in the system: n / size = 100M / 100 = 1,000,000 pools.
  2. Read limit per pool: 10% of 100 = 10 reads.
  3. Total system read capacity: 1,000,000 pools × 10 = 10,000,000 reads.
  4. Requests issued at reqs=10%: 10% of 100M = 10,000,000 requests.
  5. Load factor = requests ÷ capacity = 10M / 10M = 1. Matches the paper's claim exactly, and at that point ~50% of pools are already expended (Fig. 3).
💡
This is the bucket-overflow intuition made precise: at load factor 1 the system is "full" only on average — Poisson variance means many pools overflow well before every pool is individually full.

② The invisible 90%+ of replenishment

The paper states that under Uniform, Zipf, and Zipf-pool, less than 10%, 8%, and 5% of an expended pool's objects were ever actually read. What does that imply for replenishment?

  1. Take a pool of 100 objects that just expended under Zipf-pool (≤5% read).
  2. At most 5 of its 100 objects were ever requested — the system "knows" these were wanted.
  3. The other ≥95 objects were never touched, yet must still be re-sequenced to avoid losing them.
  4. Even a perfect cache that retained every object ever read would only cover that same ≤5% — >90% of the pool must be blindly bulk-read for replenishment.
⚠
This is why the paper calls efficient bulk retrieval essential: replenishment cost is dominated by objects nobody asked for, not by popular ones a cache could have saved.

③ Read limit scales inversely with tolerable request rate

The paper notes system state at limit=1%, reqs=0.1% looks like limit=10%, reqs=1% (pool size 1,000). Verify this is the same load factor.

  1. Case A: limit = 1% of 1,000 = 10 reads/pool; reqs = 0.1% of n.
  2. Case B: limit = 10% of 1,000 = 100 reads/pool; reqs = 1% of n.
  3. Ratio reqs:limit in A = 0.1 : 1 = 0.1. Ratio in B = 1 : 10 = 0.1. Identical load factor.
  4. Conclusion: a 10× larger read limit buys you roughly 10× more requests before reaching the same endangerment state — Takeaway #2's "proportional" scaling, confirmed by arithmetic.

④ One cached object can shield an entire pool — but only under skew

With a 1%-size cache, Zipf reaches a hit rate of only ~4.5% yet drives endangered objects to zero, while Uniform's hit rate of ~0.1% does nothing. Why such different leverage from similar-looking hit rates?

  1. Under Zipf, the same few hot objects are requested repeatedly — caching just one of them keeps its pool from accumulating more hydration-triggering reads, protecting every other (cold) object sharing that pool too.
  2. Under Uniform, no object is special — a 1% cache catches ~1% of distinct objects, each used once, so caching one doesn't reduce pressure on any particular pool meaningfully.
  3. Leverage factor ≈ pool size: shielding one hot object can save up to size neighbors from a cascade of forced hydration — e.g. up to 1,000 objects per cache slot at pool size 1,000.
🍯
Hit rate alone is a misleading success metric here — what matters is whether a hit removes pressure from a specific pool, which only happens under skew.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one names the misconception it checks.

Problem 1 · easy
A vendor announces a new encoding that doubles both pool capacity (storage density) and the primer library, so each pool now holds twice as many unit-sized objects. Per Takeaway #1, is this purely good news for system cost?
Show answer
Not by itself. The paper treats storage density and objects-per-pool (primer count) as separate parameters, but here both scale together, so twice as many objects now share the same (unchanged) read limit — making expenditure and hydration cascades affect more data at once, and increasing replenishment (re-synthesis) cost per event. It helps only if the read limit scales up proportionately.Misconception: that storage density is the main lever on system cost, and that "more capacity" automatically means "more objects per pool." The paper keeps these as distinct parameters; read limit often matters more than either.
Problem 2 · medium
Pool size=10,000, and Figure 4 shows 100% of such pools are hydrated after only reqs=0.1% of requests. Explain why large pools hydrate almost instantly even at tiny request volume.
Show answer
With only ~n/10,000 pools total and random placement, even a small fraction of requests is likely to touch every pool at least once — hydration happens on first access regardless of the read limit. More objects per pool means fewer, "bigger" pools, so the birthday-paradox-like coverage of "every pool touched once" happens very fast.Misconception: that hydration tracks the read limit. Hydration is triggered by the very first read; the read limit only affects when a pool later expends.
Problem 3 · medium
Why does "ideal" Zipf-pool placement (grouping popular objects into their own pools) fail to reduce endangered objects compared to plain Zipf, even though it clearly delays hydration?
Show answer
Applying a Zipf distribution over a small population (pools instead of objects) produces an even heavier-tailed concentration — a few pools absorb disproportionately more reads, so they still expend at a similar or faster rate. Delaying when pools get touched (hydration) is a different effect than how hard the ones that do get touched get hammered (expenditure).Misconception: that "smarter placement" uniformly helps all metrics. Hydration and endangerment can move in opposite directions.
Problem 4 · hard
r-hash reduces endangered objects by an order of magnitude per added replica, better than r-pool under Zipf. Why does r-hash then cause more pools to become hydrated when a pool expends?
Show answer
r-hash scatters each object's replicas across many different pools (good for load balancing), so when one pool expends, its displaced reads must now be served from as many different pools as there were distinct objects in it — each of those must be hydrated if still dry. r-pool, by contrast, keeps all of a pool's replicas together, so an expended pool's traffic falls on just a few sibling pools.Misconception: that a replication strategy good for one metric (endangerment) is automatically good for all (hydration). It's an explicit tradeoff (Takeaway #3).
Problem 5 · medium
A 1% cache eliminates all endangered objects under Zipf. Does this mean caching has solved DNA storage's durability problem?
Show answer
No. The cache has no effect on hydration (which happens on the first, compulsory cache miss), so pools still get hydrated and will still eventually need replenishment regardless of caching. Also, at most 10% of endangered objects (in the no-endangerment-prevented cases) could even have been served from cache — most replenishment must come from bulk-reading the pool itself.Misconception: that "zero endangered objects" means "problem solved." Hydration-driven decay and replenishment cost persist even when nothing is technically lost yet.
Problem 6 · easy
Why does plain LRU underperform on this workload even under high skew, compared to its reputation in conventional storage caching?
Show answer
LRU only tracks recency/frequency — it has no notion of which pools are already hydrated or close to their read limit. A less-popular object can be evicted and, once its pool later expends, can never re-enter the cache to be "saved." An LRU cache aware of pool physical state could prioritize keeping objects from fragile pools.Misconception: that any reasonably effective cache is good enough here. Takeaway #5 argues the cache itself needs to be DNA-aware.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Tap any to expand.

Q1What exactly does "hydration" cost, in concrete terms?▾
It converts a pool from dry (stable for decades/centuries at room temperature) to hydrated (durable for only a few years, even refrigerated), and it incurs ongoing refrigeration cost. It happens the first time any object in the pool is read, independent of the read limit.
Q2Is a "read limit" a hard biological wall, or an engineering choice?▾
It's a practical consequence of cheaper, more error-prone nanopore sequencing (vs. the far more accurate but costly Illumina) combined with the fact that each PCR amplification cycle adds decoding errors. Today it's "several hundred reads"; it could improve with better error correction or sequencing accuracy, which the paper's model treats as a single tunable parameter.
Q3Why doesn't the model account for the dollar cost of synthesis/sequencing?▾
Those costs are currently changing rapidly and unpredictably as the underlying technology matures, so pinning numbers to them would make the model obsolete quickly. The paper deliberately works one abstraction level above cost, using hydration/expenditure/endangerment as cost proxies instead.
Q4What's the difference between a pool being "expended" and an object being "endangered"?▾
Expended describes a single pool that has hit its read limit. Endangered describes an object whose every replica's pool is expended — it can no longer be served by a normal read. The model assumes its pool's content can still be recovered (via bulk-read or reserved read-limit capacity) and re-synthesized; only if that recovery path is unavailable is the object considered truly lost. One expended pool doesn't necessarily endanger anything, if replicas exist elsewhere.
Q5Why does Zipf-pool behave worse than expected for endangerment?▾
Applying the same Zipf skew to a much smaller population (pools, not objects) produces a heavier relative tail — a few pools get hit disproportionately hard, offsetting the benefit of "ideal" hot/cold separation. It still helps hydration timing, just not endangerment.
Q6How is this different from prior caching-for-DNA work (Agliamzanov & Tuck)?▾
That work analyzed caching over 10-100 pools under one high-access-rate workload and did not model hydration at all. This paper generalizes across access distributions, placement strategies, and explicitly tracks hydration alongside expenditure — showing the two can trade off against each other.
Q7Does bigger always mean worse for pools?▾
Not strictly — larger pools are less sensitive to access-pattern variance (fewer, "smoother" overflow events) and delay hydration less dramatically than they might seem, under skew. The downside is that when they do expend, far more data must be re-synthesized at once, and today's read limits don't scale with pool size.
Q8What would a "DNA-aware" cache or placement policy actually look like?▾
The paper doesn't fully design one — it's future work — but motivates it: a cache that avoids evicting objects whose pool is already hydrated/near its read limit, or a placement policy that balances popularity against replication's blast-radius tradeoff (something between r-pool and r-hash), rather than ignoring pool physical state entirely.
