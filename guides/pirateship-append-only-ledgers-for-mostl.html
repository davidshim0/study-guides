SOSP 2026 · A Field Guide

Sail fast on a trusted deck, keep a Byzantine lookout astern

A distributed ledger assumes its TEEs are honest — until, rarely, one isn't. Pirateship commits at CFT speed, then quietly re-derives the same decisions under full Byzantine suspicion, a few batches behind, so a compromise can only ever cost you the still-unaudited tail.

📄 "Pirateship: Append-Only Ledgers for (Mostly) Trusted Execution Environments" · Mishra et al. 🎓 Assumes familiarity with distributed systems generally, not BFT specifics ⏱ ~30 min read
🏴‍☠️

Commit under the CFT assumption that TEEs behave — then, off the critical path, re-derive the identical log under the BFT assumption that they might not, using votes you already collected.
No extra messages. A bounded, constant-size blast radius.

Before you begin

Quick glossary, then the concepts it rests on

The acronyms first — skim them once, then come back as needed. Below, the conceptual primers that make the rest of the paper legible; open the ones you're unsure of.

1Why "mostly trusted" instead of "trusted" or "untrusted"?▾

Two extremes already exist. Trust TEEs fully, and you get a cheap CFT protocol (2f+1 nodes, one phase) — fast, but a single TEE break can silently fork the whole log. Trust TEEs not at all, and you need a full BFT protocol (3f+1 nodes, extra phases, signatures on everything) — safe against any TEE, but permanently paying BFT's overhead for an event that's rare.

Pirateship's premise: TEE compromise is rare but catastrophic, not routine. So spend BFT-level suspicion only on catching and undoing rare bad events, not on every single commit.

Now you're ready: Pirateship runs CFT for speed and a hidden BFT for insurance — the whole paper is about making that combination cost nothing extra.
2Quorums, and why size matters▾

Consensus works by collecting votes from enough replicas (a quorum) that any two quorums must share at least one honest replica — that shared replica is what prevents two conflicting decisions from both being accepted. CFT only needs to survive crashes, so a bare majority (⌊N/2⌋+1) suffices. BFT must also survive replicas that actively lie, so it needs a larger quorum (N−u, with u accounting for both crashes and Byzantine no-shows) to still guarantee overlap once you discount for liars.

Now you're ready: Pirateship's commitQC uses the small CFT quorum; its auditQC uses the larger BFT-style quorum — same votes, different thresholds.
3Signed vs. unsigned votes▾

A crash-only setting doesn't need signatures — nobody is assumed to fabricate messages, so a plain vote is trustworthy. Once you admit a node might lie, you need cryptographic signatures so a forged vote can be caught and a quorum can be proven to a third party (like a future leader) without re-asking every voter.

Now you're ready: commitQCs in Pirateship don't require signatures (cheap, matches CFT); auditQCs do (expensive but necessary — they're the thing that must survive a lying leader).
4Hash chaining as "votes that count twice"▾

If every batch cryptographically points to its parent, then voting for batch 10 is also implicitly vouching for batches 1–9 (you can't vote for a batch without having seen its whole history). This lets a leader "top up" an old, smaller commit quorum into a larger audit quorum using votes that arrived later, or votes cast for a completely different, later batch — no one has to be asked to vote twice.

Now you're ready: this is the trick that lets Pirateship harden CFT quorums into BFT quorums with zero extra messages.
5Pipelining▾

Rather than wait for phase 1 of a batch to finish before starting phase 2, a pipelined protocol layers phase 2 of an old batch onto the message carrying phase 1 of a new one — one round trip serves two purposes. This is what keeps a two-phase (BFT-like) audit from taking twice as long as a one-phase (CFT-like) commit.

Now you're ready: Pirateship's audit needs two QCs (a QC on a QC) but, thanks to pipelining, never falls more than a small constant number of batches behind commit.
6View change, briefly▾

When the current leader seems to have stalled or misbehaved, replicas time out, elect a new leader, and that leader must figure out which branch of the (possibly forked) log to continue. Standard BFT view changes only guarantee that already-audited state survives this — anything only "committed" in the weaker sense can, in principle, be lost.

Now you're ready: Pirateship needs a modified view change (with an extra "view stabilization" step) precisely to also protect committed-but-not-yet-audited transactions.
7Correlated vs. independent failures▾

Classic fault models assume each replica fails independently. TEE vulnerabilities don't work that way: a bug in a chip generation or a shared library can compromise every node running that stack at once, regardless of how many replicas you deployed. Traditional models have no vocabulary for "these five nodes will fail together, those five will fail independently."

Now you're ready: this gap is exactly what the paper's new failure model, platform-fault-tolerance, is built to express.
8Separating safety from liveness▾

Safety: nothing incorrect is ever agreed upon. Liveness: the system eventually makes progress. Many cloud ledger deployments deliberately give up on liveness under attack (a malicious host can trivially stall you by withholding messages) while insisting safety never breaks — any answer you do get must be correct, even if you sometimes get no answer at all.

Now you're ready: Pirateship's failure model lets an operator size for safety and for liveness independently, instead of one bundled threshold.
The whole story in one place

Pirateship in a nutshell

The problem, the refactoring trick that fixes it, and how the pieces connect.

Distributed ledgers built on TEEs run a lightweight CFT protocol atop hardware that's assumed never to lie. That assumption is usually true — but when a TEE vulnerability lands, an attacker can potentially fork or roll back the entire log with no warning, on every affected node at once. The alternative, a full BFT protocol that trusts nothing, pays for that safety permanently: 3f+1 nodes instead of 2f+1, extra phases, signatures on every message.

Pirateship's move is to run both, cheaply, at the same time. It commits transactions exactly as a CFT-on-TEE system would — same quorum size, same phase count, same speed. In the background, using the same votes, it incrementally assembles the larger, signed quorums a BFT protocol would require, and uses them to audit the CFT decisions. If TEEs never misbehave, audit is a formality that silently confirms commit was right. If a TEE is compromised, audit is what catches the divergence and reconciles it — before more than a small, bounded amount of committed-but-unaudited log is at risk.

Making this work with zero extra messages requires three techniques working together: hash chaining (a vote for a later batch also counts as a vote for all its ancestors, so late or later votes can upgrade an old commit quorum into an audit quorum), pipelining (the audit's second phase rides along on the next batch's first phase, so audit never falls more than a constant number of batches behind commit), and a fast audit path (when all N replicas respond, one round trip suffices instead of two).

The payoff: Pirateship's commit throughput and latency track existing TEE-only ledgers (87% of signed Raft's throughput at N=7, up to 2.6× lower commit latency than a no-trust BFT approach), while its audit throughput tracks a state-of-the-art BFT protocol (88.6% of Autobahn's throughput, 15% lower latency) — for the price of one protocol's worth of messages.

🏴‍☠️ Key takeaways

  • TEEs raise the bar against attack but don't eliminate it — treat compromise as rare, not impossible or routine.
  • PFT separates crash failures (node-independent) from platform compromises (correlated), and safety from liveness thresholds.
  • N ≥ 2u + f_safe + 1, where u = f_live + c — strictly less replication than plain BFT's 3f+1 whenever liveness is sacrificed.
  • Hash-chained batches make every vote also a vote for all its ancestors — commit quorums harden into audit quorums for free.
  • Pipelining + a fast audit path keep audit within a constant, bounded lag behind commit — the "blast radius" of a TEE compromise.
  • A new view stabilization step protects committed-but-unaudited transactions across leader changes, which standard BFT view change does not.
  • Result: commit performance ≈ TEE-only ledgers; audit performance ≈ state-of-the-art BFT — both, from one protocol.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about Pirateship

Each stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the picture stops being true.

The core idea · Cautious trust

The captain, and the navigator double-checking the charts

The captain steers by instinct and calls out headings immediately — fast, and almost always right. A navigator, a few minutes behind, cross-checks each heading against the stars using the same sightings the captain used. Most voyages, the navigator only ever confirms. The one time the captain is compromised (drugged, deceived), the navigator's slower, independently-verified log is what the crew falls back on — and only the last few minutes of heading are ever in doubt.

Where it breaks: the navigator isn't a separate person with separate senses — Pirateship's "double check" reuses the very same votes the captain collected, which is the whole trick (no second observation needed).
The mechanism · Hash chaining

Signing a logbook page that also stamps every earlier page

Imagine a ship's logbook where signing today's page cryptographically re-affirms every page before it — you can't sign page 30 without having read and endorsed pages 1–29. A crewmate who signs page 32 late, or a different crewmate who signs page 33, has — without knowing it — also just re-affirmed page 30. Old, thin approvals get "topped up" by later signatures for free.

Where it breaks: this only works because batches are strictly chained (each has exactly one parent); it wouldn't work if the log could branch arbitrarily without conflict-checking.
The failure model · Platforms vs. nodes

Three ships built in the same shipyard

Three ships crewed independently can each sink on their own (a "crash" — a storm, a broken mast) without the others being affected. But if all three were built by the same shipyard using the same flawed hull design, a design flaw sinks all three at once, no matter how independently they're crewed. That shared hull design is a platform: nodes fail independently, platforms fail together.

Where it breaks: what counts as "the same shipyard" is a belief, not a physical fact — the paper is explicit that platform boundaries are set by the operator's judgment of fault independence, and can be wrong.
The API · Commit vs. audit

A provisional customs stamp, and a notarized deed

A provisional customs stamp is issued the moment your goods pass inspection — fast, trusted in the ordinary course of business, but revocable if the inspector is later found corrupt. A notarized deed takes longer to produce (it requires cross-checked signatures) but, once issued, cannot be undone. Most of your daily business runs fine on customs stamps; you only pay for notarization on your house.

Where it breaks: in Pirateship the "notarization" isn't slower paperwork for the same event — it's a structurally stronger quorum computed from votes you already have, so it costs latency but not extra client effort.
The fix · View stabilization

The new captain re-reads the log before setting a new course

When a captain is deposed mid-voyage, a hasty successor who immediately charts a new course risks contradicting orders the old captain had already given and the crew had started to follow. A careful successor first collects the crew's logbooks, confirms which heading was actually being followed, and only then proceeds — guaranteeing the new course never silently erases an order that was already underway.

Where it breaks: "confirms which heading" isn't a vague diligence step — it's a precise extra protocol phase (an auditQC on the chosen branch) that blocks new batches until it completes.
Why it's cheap · Overlapping quorums

Two search parties combing the same overlapping stretch of coast

If two search parties are each assigned a large enough stretch of coast, their areas are guaranteed to overlap — so anything one party found, the other's report will corroborate, even without the parties talking to each other. Pirateship sizes commit and audit quorums (and the intersection argument behind Theorem 1) exactly so that any two quorums — old or new, commit or audit — are guaranteed to share at least one honest witness.

Where it breaks: the overlap guarantee depends on precise counting (N ≥ 2u+f_safe+1) — shrink the "coastline" (replica count) below that bound and overlap, and the guarantee, disappears.
The full explanation

How it actually works

From the failure model, to the on-the-wire protocol, to view changes — with the paper's key mechanisms rebuilt as diagrams.

1 · Platform-Fault-Tolerance — the failure model

PFT splits N nodes into Π platforms — correlated failure domains. It then separates two independent guarantees: safety (a single, agreed log) holds up to πsafe platform compromises (fsafe total compromised nodes); liveness (the log keeps growing) holds up to πlive platform compromises and c independent crashes. Writing u = flive + c for total liveness-relevant failures, the paper proves the tight bound:

N ≥ 2(c + f_live) + f_safe + 1  =  2u + f_safe + 1

Plug in the classic corners and you get the classic bounds back: with f_safe = f_live = 0, N ≥ 2c+1 (plain CFT). With u = f_safe = f, N ≥ 3f+1 (plain BFT). PFT never needs more replicas than BFT already required — but it lets an operator spend far fewer when liveness is intentionally sacrificed.

2 · Batches, quorum certificates, and the two thresholds

Each batch hash-chains to its parent and carries the highest commit/audit QCs the leader knows about. Two quorum types coexist over the same replica set: a commitQC needs only ⌊N/2⌋+1 votes, unsigned; an auditQC needs N−u signed votes. A batch is audited only once an auditQC forms for it (or a descendant) and a second QC forms over that fact — two hops, mirroring how BFT protocols need a prepare and a commit phase to rule out equivocation and guarantee durability across views.

3 · Confirmation rules

Each replica applies these rules locally as QCs arrive, piggy-backed on ordinary Append-Entry messages — no separate round trips:

  1. Commit: a majority commitQC forms. Unsigned, so a malicious leader could in principle spoof one — acceptable, since committed state isn't promised to survive a compromise.
  2. Slow audit: an auditQC (N−u signed votes) forms for the batch or a descendant, and a second QC forms over that fact in the same view — two hops close the door on both equivocation and losing the decision across a future view change.
  3. Fast audit: all N replicas vote (signed) for the batch or a descendant — a single, maximal quorum, which directly guarantees durability without needing the second hop.
💡
Why does fast audit need every vote? Any future leader running a view change will see at least N−u−f_safe of those N votes — for fast audit's single QC to still be provably unforgeable to that future leader, it must start from the maximal quorum, since a smaller one leaves room for f_safe liars to manufacture a matching fake.

4 · View change and view stabilization

Standard BFT view change: (i) replicas time out and broadcast view-change, (ii) the new leader gathers N−u of these and picks a branch to extend, (iii) it disseminates a new-view message. That alone only guarantees audited state survives. Pirateship adds a fourth step, view stabilization: before the new leader accepts any new client batches, it must itself collect an auditQC on its chosen branch. This closes the exact gap where a committed-but-not-yet-audited batch could otherwise be silently dropped by an unlucky view change.

Branch selection, in order: (1) prefer any branch with the highest-view auditQC — any truly audited branch is guaranteed to appear here; (2) also prefer any branch that could have gone the fast-audit path (appeared N−(u+f_safe) times); (3) among what's left, prefer the highest view; (4) then the highest sequence number. Rules 3–4 preserve committed (not just audited) branches whenever no node is actually malicious.

5 · Receipts

Clients get a signed commit receipt (a Merkle inclusion proof) as soon as their transaction commits — identical in spirit to what TEE-only ledgers offer today. An audit receipt additionally bundles one or more auditQCs plus the short hash-chain segment linking the transaction to the audited batch — bounded in size, and independently verifiable offline, no live query needed.

Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four traces carried through with the paper's own numbers: sizing a fleet, walking a five-node quorum, backing out a hidden baseline, and timing the blast radius.

① Sizing a fleet: from platform threat to node count

Using N ≥ 2(c+f_live) + f_safe + 1, walk the paper's own Fig. 1 progression.

  1. Safety only, single-node platforms, π_safe=1: f_safe=1, c=f_live=0 → N ≥ 0+1+1 = 2 platforms.
  2. Add liveness under the same threshold, π_live=1: f_live=1, c=0, f_safe=1 → N ≥ 2(1)+1+1 = 4. Liveness costs twice as much as safety — the asymmetry comes straight from the "2u" term.
  3. Unequal platform sizes, safety only: platforms of size {3,1}; compromising the biggest platform gives f_safe=3, c=f_live=0 → N ≥ 0+3+1 = 4 nodes, matching the {3,1} split exactly.
  4. Add liveness (π_live=1, same worst-case sizing → f_live=3) and one crash (c=1): N ≥ 2(1+3)+3+1 = 8+3+1 = 12 nodes — exactly the paper's number.
🏴‍☠️
Insight: every extra unit of liveness tolerance costs 2 nodes; every extra unit of safety tolerance costs 1. That 2:1 ratio is why many cloud deployments deliberately give up liveness and keep safety.

② Quorums for a five-node fleet

N=5, u=f_safe=1 (the paper's Fig. 3 worked example). What are the three quorum sizes, and how do batches A–D progress?

  1. Commit = ⌊5/2⌋+1 = 3 votes, unsigned.
  2. Slow audit = N−u = 5−1 = 4 signed votes, plus a second QC over that fact.
  3. Fast audit = N = 5 signed votes, single hop.
  4. Leader R5 broadcasts A; R1, R2, R5 vote → 3 votes → A commits. B follows the same way.
  5. A vote on B from R3 (a fourth, distinct voter across A and B) upgrades A's quorum to 4 signed votes → auditQC on A forms. This QC rides along with batch C.
  6. C collects its own 4 votes, forming a QC that also covers the earlier auditQC on A → two hops complete → A is (slow-path) audited.
  7. D collects votes from all 5 replicas at once → D is fast-audited in a single hop.
💡
Notice steps 5–6 needed no extra round trips — the "4th vote" and the "QC on C" were both going to be sent anyway as part of normal pipelined progress.

③ Backing out the hidden baseline

The paper reports ps(sig=10) commit throughput as 345k txn/s, stated to be 87% of signed_raft(n=7)'s throughput, and roughly 1.9× engraft's throughput. Recover both baselines.

  1. signed_raft(n=7): 345,000 ÷ 0.87 ≈ 396,550 txn/s.
  2. engraft: 345,000 ÷ 1.9 ≈ 181,600 txn/s — consistent with the paper's framing that engraft's extra 2PC round trips for rollback-resistance roughly halve its throughput relative to Pirateship.
  3. Sanity check against signed_raft(n=5)=520k txn/s: going from 5 to 7 nodes (more messages fanned out per round) plausibly explains signed_raft(n=7)'s drop to ≈397k — replication factor, not protocol logic, is the main cost driver here.
🏴‍☠️
Insight: Pirateship's 35% gap versus signed_raft(n=5) is attributed entirely to running at N=7 instead of N=5 (needed for auditing) — not to any inherent audit-logic overhead on the commit path.

④ The blast radius, timed

Using the paper's equivocation-recovery timeline (Fig. 6b): equivocation begins ~30.3s; audit throughput visibly craters by 32.7s; followers time out after a fixed 4s of no audit progress; the new leader steps in around 35s; the view stabilizes at 37.61s; steady state resumes ~40s. How long is a TEE compromise's actual "blast radius" window?

  1. Detection lag ≈ 32.7 − 30.3 = 2.4s — how quickly audit throughput visibly stalls once equivocation starts.
  2. Fixed timeout = 4s — the protocol constant before a follower gives up on the stalled leader.
  3. New leader → stabilized view ≈ 37.61 − 35 = 2.61s — collecting the view-change quorum, selecting the correct branch, and completing view stabilization's extra audit round.
  4. Settle back to steady state ≈ 40 − 37.61 ≈ 2.4s — rolled-back replicas replay/re-audit the reconciled branch.
  5. Total window ≈ 40 − 30.3 ≈ 9.7 seconds, end to end — and by construction, only the batches committed but not yet audited within that window were ever at risk.
💡
Compare this to a pure TEE-CFT ledger: there, an undetected compromise could roll back the entire log, with no automatic timeout to bound the damage at all.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the common misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
Why doesn't Pirateship need the classic BFT bound of 3f+1 nodes?
Show answer
Because it doesn't insist on liveness under Byzantine attack the way classic BFT does. Its general bound is N ≥ 2u+f_safe+1 where u=f_live+c; setting f_live=0 (giving up liveness under compromise, a common deployment choice) drops the "2u" term's Byzantine contribution, letting N shrink well below 3f+1 while safety (f_safe) is still fully protected.Misconception: that Pirateship "cheats" BFT's lower bound. It doesn't — it simply targets a configuration (safety-only under attack) that classic BFT's bound wasn't optimized for.
Problem 2 · medium
A deployment wants π_safe=2 (worst-case platform size 4 nodes, so f_safe=4), π_live=1 (worst-case platform size 4, f_live=4), and tolerates c=2 independent crashes. How many total nodes N are required?
Show answer
N ≥ 2(c+f_live) + f_safe + 1 = 2(2+4) + 4 + 1 = 12 + 4 + 1 = 17 nodes.Misconception: adding f_safe and f_live directly (getting 8+1=9). They enter the formula asymmetrically — f_live is doubled (via u), f_safe is not — because liveness thresholds need larger quorums to guarantee overlap while still tolerating no-shows.
Problem 3 · medium
Why don't commitQCs need signatures, while auditQCs do?
Show answer
Commit only needs to survive crashes — no replica is assumed to fabricate a vote, so an unsigned tally is trustworthy, and even if a malicious leader spoofed one, that's acceptable because committed state was never promised to survive a compromise. Audit must survive a lying leader and lying replicas, so it needs signatures: proof that must convince a future leader (who wasn't there) that N−u distinct honest replicas really did vote this way.Misconception: that skipping signatures on commitQCs is a shortcut/bug. It's deliberate — signing every message would cost real throughput for a guarantee (surviving TEE compromise) that commit isn't meant to provide.
Problem 4 · hard
A malicious leader spoofs a commitQC for a batch that fewer than a majority of replicas actually saw. Does this violate Pirateship's guarantees?
Show answer
No — not for the commit guarantee. Definition 2 ("committed transactions") is explicitly scoped to no platform compromises. Once a leader is malicious, committed state can legitimately be rolled back; that's exactly the gap audit exists to close. What must never happen is a spoofed auditQC — that would violate Definition 3, which holds up to π_safe compromises. Audit's larger, signed quorum is precisely what's hard to spoof.Misconception: expecting commit to be tamper-proof on its own. Commit's promise is CFT-strength only; audit is where the Byzantine-strength promise lives.
Problem 5 · medium
Under what condition does the fast audit path become impossible to use safely, and why?
Show answer
When N−u−f_safe ≤ f_safe, i.e. the configuration has been tuned to optimize liveness so aggressively that f_safe malicious replicas could, on their own, manufacture enough matching fake votes (≥ N−u−f_safe) to counterfeit what looks like a legitimate fast-audit quorum. In that regime, only the two-hop slow audit path is safe.Misconception: that fast audit is always strictly better since it's faster. It's only safe when the deployment has enough of a safety margin (f_safe is small relative to N−u) that liars can't fabricate a full-N-vote illusion.
Problem 6 · hard
Why does Pirateship need a whole extra "view stabilization" phase — isn't the standard BFT view change (trigger, branch select, disseminate) enough?
Show answer
Standard BFT view change only guarantees that audited branches survive — audit quorums (N−u signed) are large enough that any view-change quorum (also N−u) is guaranteed to intersect them. Commit quorums are smaller (⌊N/2⌋+1, unsigned) and were never designed with that same overlap guarantee against view-change quorums. Without an extra step, a new leader could legitimately pick a branch that drops committed-but-unaudited batches. View stabilization forces the new leader to first collect its own auditQC on the chosen branch before accepting new work, which re-establishes the missing overlap guarantee going forward.Misconception: thinking committed and audited state need identical protection. They don't — audited state is the durability floor; committed state is a weaker, revocable convenience layer, and it's exactly the asymmetry between their quorum sizes that creates the gap view stabilization closes.
Problem 7 · easy
Two platforms use the exact same TEE chip generation but are deployed in different Azure regions. Does adding a third replica in a third region always improve platform-fault-tolerance?
Show answer
Not necessarily. If all three regions share the same vulnerable TEE chip generation, an operator who believes "different region = different platform" may be wrong — a single chip-level vulnerability could compromise all three regions' replicas simultaneously, i.e. they're really one platform, not three. PFT's guarantees only hold relative to the operator's platform boundaries; if those boundaries don't reflect true fault independence, the nominal f_safe/f_live thresholds overstate real protection.Misconception: that more replicas (or more regions) automatically means more fault tolerance. It only helps against the failure types your platform boundaries actually capture as independent.
Problem 8 · medium
In the paper's C1 (1,1,1,1 nodes/platform) vs. C2 (2,2,2,1) configurations, both with u=f_safe matching their respective sizes, C2 has lower commit latency than C1 despite having more total nodes. Why?
Show answer
Commit latency is dominated by how many distinct platforms (i.e. network round trips to a distinct region) the leader must hear from to reach quorum, not raw node count. In C1, every node is its own platform, so the commit quorum spans more distinct regions. In C2, several nodes share a platform (and typically a region), so the leader can satisfy commit quorum by hearing from fewer distinct, geographically-separated regions — cutting the slowest cross-region round trips out of the critical path.Misconception: that latency scales with total replica count. It scales with the number of distinct network hops (platforms/regions) a quorum has to span.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Ten questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What's the actual difference between "committed" and "audited"?▾
Committed = durable assuming no TEE compromise (a CFT-strength promise, identical to what today's TEE-only ledgers already offer). Audited = durable up to π_safe platform compromises (a BFT-strength promise). Clients pick which one to wait for; both are produced by the same protocol run.
Q2Does auditing add extra network messages?▾
No. Hash chaining makes every vote implicitly a vote for all ancestor batches, so audit quorums are assembled from votes that were already flowing for other reasons (either late arrivals or votes on subsequent batches). Pipelining lets the audit's second QC-of-a-QC hop ride on the very next batch's normal Append-Entry message.
Q3If audit always trails commit, how bad can a TEE compromise get?▾
Bounded. Pirateship guarantees audit never trails commit by more than a constant number of batches (via pipelining). So the "blast radius" of any compromise is at most that small, fixed unaudited tail — never the whole log, unlike a pure TEE-trusting ledger.
Q4What's a "platform" concretely — is it a machine, a region, a chip?▾
Whatever the operator believes constitutes a correlated failure domain — could be a (TEE type, cloud region) pair, a whole cloud provider, or a hardware stack. It's a modeling choice, not a fixed physical unit, and the paper is explicit that it can be revised via reconfiguration as beliefs about shared vulnerabilities change.
Q5Why would anyone deliberately give up liveness (π_live=0)?▾
Because a malicious host can trivially attack liveness anyway (just withhold messages) — so paying 2× the replication cost to formally tolerate it often isn't worth it, especially given the scarcity of independent cloud/TEE platforms. Many production ledgers (Signal's SVR3, Azure Confidential Ledger) already make this trade explicitly.
Q6How does Pirateship compare in raw performance to existing systems?▾
Its commit path performs like existing TEE-only ledgers (87% of signed Raft's throughput at N=7, matched latency, up to 2.6× lower latency than a no-trust BFT approach). Its audit path performs like state-of-the-art BFT (88.6% of Autobahn's throughput with 15% lower latency; beats HotStuff by 20–46% and PeerReview by 39%).
Q7What exactly does a "receipt" prove to a client?▾
A commit receipt is a signed Merkle inclusion proof that a transaction is in a specific batch — transferable evidence a client can show a third party. An audit receipt adds the auditQC(s) and hash-chain segment proving that batch has been audited — stronger, still bounded in size, and verifiable entirely offline.
Q8Is Pirateship a blockchain?▾
It shares mechanisms with modern BFT/blockchain consensus (hash-chained batches, quorum certificates, pipelining, a fast path), but it's a permissioned distributed ledger for a known set of replicas within/across specific clouds — not a permissionless, proof-of-work or proof-of-stake system.
Q9How do real applications decide when to wait for audit vs. just commit?▾
Per-transaction policy, tuned to sensitivity: a banking app audits only transfers above a dollar threshold; a key-recovery service (Signal-style) throttles a user once it sees multiple committed-but-unaudited password guesses in a row, to avoid a rollback letting an attacker exceed their guess limit; a key transparency service can switch its whole workload to audit-only temporarily when a new TEE vulnerability is disclosed, then switch back once patched.
Q10What did the paper leave for the extended/future version?▾
The full Theorem 1 proof, the TLA+ specification, a generalized reconfiguration algorithm for evolving platform boundaries over time, and further implementation detail on PirateshipCTS — all pointed to the extended paper (arXiv:2602.05346).

Glossary & further reading

Every term in one place, results at a glance, then a path to go deeper.

Core terms
PirateshipThe paper's PFT ledger: commits at CFT speed, audits in the background at BFT strength, using the same votes and no extra messages.
PFT (Platform Fault Tolerance)Failure model distinguishing independent node crashes from correlated platform (TEE/cloud/hardware) compromises, with separate safety and liveness thresholds.
π_safe / f_safeMax platform compromises / total compromised nodes tolerated while still guaranteeing safety (a single agreed log).
π_live / f_live / c / uMax platform compromises, resulting compromised nodes, independent crashes, and their sum (u=f_live+c) tolerated while still guaranteeing liveness.
commitQC / auditQCQuorum certificates for the two confirmation tiers: small & unsigned (commit, CFT-strength) vs. larger & signed (audit, BFT-strength).
Hash chainingEach batch cryptographically points to its parent, so a vote for a batch also votes for all its ancestors — lets late/later votes harden old quorums for free.
Fast / slow auditFast: single QC of all N votes. Slow: a QC-of-a-QC across two hops, needed whenever fewer than all N replicas respond.
View stabilizationPirateship's added view-change phase: a new leader must form an auditQC on its chosen branch before accepting new batches, protecting committed-but-unaudited state.
Supporting terms
TEETrusted Execution Environment (AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX, ARM CCA) — hardware attestation that code runs as specified, until the hardware itself is broken.
CFT / BFTCrash- vs. Byzantine-Fault-Tolerant consensus; 2f+1 vs. 3f+1 replicas, one phase vs. at least two.
Platform / nodeA correlated failure domain (platform) vs. an individually-crashing replica (node) within it.
Batch / branchA hash-chained bundle of transactions (batch); a maximal chain of batches (branch) — conflicting branches don't extend each other.
PipeliningLayering a later protocol phase for an old batch onto the message carrying an earlier phase for a new one, so extra phases don't cost extra round trips.
ReceiptSigned, client-portable, offline-verifiable proof of commit (Merkle proof) or audit (+ auditQCs + hash-chain segment).
EquivocationA Byzantine leader broadcasting conflicting batches to different partitions of replicas — the commission attack Pirateship's audit is designed to catch.
signed_raft / engraft / autobahn / hotstuff / peer_reviewBaselines: Raft + periodic signatures; Raft + 2PC rollback-resistance; a DAG-based SOTA BFT protocol; chained BFT with blocking pipelining; accountability via signed witnesses.
