FHE Acceleration · A Field Guide

It's not the math that's slow — it's moving the bytes

Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) lets a cloud compute on data it can never see. Everyone assumed the bottleneck was the heavyweight number-theoretic transform (NTT). Anaheim measures real GPUs and finds the opposite: boring element-wise arithmetic, starved of DRAM bandwidth, is what actually eats bootstrapping time — so it moves exactly those ops into the DRAM itself.

🔐 FHE CKKS on GPUs + processing-in-memory (PIM) · HPCA 2025 🎓 Assumes DRAM/CXL memory-systems background; CKKS terms defined in Start Here ⏱ ~40 min read
🔀

Send only the element-wise ops to PIM units living inside DRAM — leave NTT, BConv, and automorphism on the GPU — because DRAM bandwidth, not compute throughput or cache size, is what actually gates FHE on GPUs.
GPUs have plenty of TOPS. What they don't have is a 512MB ASIC-style cache — and that single fact rewrites which ops matter.

Before you begin

Eight ideas Anaheim builds on

You know DRAM banks, bandwidth, and the general shape of PIM already. What's new here is CKKS — the FHE scheme this paper accelerates — and the specific way its op sequences interact with GPU hardware limits. Tap any open; read in order the first time.

1Polynomials, RNS limbs, and the L×N matrix▾

CKKS encrypts vectors as polynomials in a ring R_Q = Z_Q[X]/(X^N+1), with N typically 2^16 and a huge modulus Q ~ 2^1300. To make arithmetic on such a large modulus tractable, CKKS uses the residue number system (RNS): pick L smaller primes Q₀…Q_{L-1} whose product is Q, and represent a polynomial as L limbs — one residue per prime. Any op on the polynomial can then be done limb-wise, in parallel, with ordinary machine-word arithmetic.

The mental model that matters for everything downstream: a polynomial is an L × N matrix — L rows (limbs), N columns (coefficients) — and most FHE ops are either "do this per-row" (NTT, modular arithmetic) or "combine rows" (basis conversion). Anaheim's whole data layout story (Tab 4) is about how you place that matrix in DRAM.

Now you're ready: whenever you see "L limbs," picture L independent rows of N numbers that can all be processed at once — that's where the parallelism (and the DRAM-layout decisions) come from.
2Ciphertexts and the four basic ops▾

A plaintext ⟨u⟩ is an encoded (not yet encrypted) polynomial. Encrypting it with a secret s gives a ciphertext [⟨u⟩] = (b, a) — a pair of polynomials in R_Q. Four ops cover most computation: HADD (ciphertext+ciphertext, cheap), PMULT (ciphertext×plaintext, cheap), HMULT (ciphertext×ciphertext, expensive), and HROT (cyclic rotation of the encrypted vector's slots, expensive).

HADD and PMULT are "low computational complexity" per the paper — essentially element-wise. HMULT and HROT are the expensive ones, and they're expensive for a specific, structural reason covered next.

Now you're ready: the paper's central finding is really about where the time inside HMULT/HROT/bootstrapping goes — not that they're expensive (everyone knew that), but which sub-operations inside them dominate on real GPUs.
3Evaluation keys — why HMULT/HROT need extra, huge objects▾

HMULT and HROT can't be done with the ciphertext alone — they need an evaluation key (evk), computed once from the secret and reused across many ops. An evk lives in an extended modulus PQ (P = extra primes P₀…P_{α-1}) and is composed of 2·D polynomials, where D = L/α is the "decomposition number." With the paper's default D = 4, that's 8 polynomials per evk, each carrying L+α limbs (more than a plain ciphertext's L).

This is why evks are enormous: the paper states a single polynomial can be ~17MB while a full evk is ~136MB — and 136/17 ≈ 8, matching 2·D = 8 almost exactly (the extended-modulus limbs add a bit more). Every HROT with a distinct rotation amount needs its own evk unless you're clever about reuse — which is exactly the fork in Primer 6.

Now you're ready: evks are the paper's "expensive furniture" — too big to keep resident, and moving them is a large fraction of what a GPU implementation spends its DRAM bandwidth on.
4NTT, BConv, ModSwitch — the compute-heavy trio▾

NTT (an integer FFT) turns an O(N²) polynomial multiplication into O(N log N) element-wise multiplication — indispensable, and the operation almost every prior FHE accelerator paper optimized hardest. BConv (basis conversion) switches a polynomial's limb-basis from Q to the extended PQ (or back) via a dense matrix-multiply-like operation. ModSwitch is the sequence INTT → BConv → NTT that appears constantly whenever moduli change — which is most of the time in HMULT/HROT.

All three are genuinely compute-bound: more arithmetic per byte moved than the element-wise ops in Primer 2. That's exactly why they were the traditional target for custom silicon — and exactly why the paper's discovery (Primer 7) is surprising.

Now you're ready: NTT/BConv/ModSwitch = honey-colored, GPU-resident, compute-bound. Keep this trio mentally separate from the element-wise ops — the paper's whole architecture hinges on that separation.
5Bootstrapping and T_boot,eff — the metric this paper optimizes▾

Every HMULT/rescale consumes one "level" (reduces L). Levels can't go negative, so FHE periodically runs bootstrapping to refresh L — a defining, expensive feature of the whole field. Because bootstrapping dominates real workload time, the paper (following prior work) tracks T_boot,eff = (bootstrapping time) / L_eff, where L_eff is how many more multiplications you get to do before the next bootstrap. This normalizes across parameter choices so architectures can be compared fairly.

Bootstrapping itself is mostly homomorphic linear transforms (>60% of bootstrapping time) plus polynomial function evaluation. A linear transform is built from K parallel HROT+PMULT pairs summed together — the paper uses D=4, K=8 as its running example throughout.

Now you're ready: when the paper says "we improve T_boot,eff," read it as "we make FHE workloads cheaper per useful multiplication," which is the real currency of practical FHE.
6Hoisting vs. MinKS — one optimization forecloses the other▾

A linear transform needs K HROT evaluations that all start with the same expensive ModSwitch step (ModUp). Hoisting exploits this: do ModUp once instead of K times, and similarly batch the back-half (ModDown) after accumulating. This cuts (I)NTT op count dramatically (2.47× in the paper's CoeffToSlot example) — at the price of slightly larger intermediate plaintexts, because the batched ops now run in the extended modulus.

MinKS (minimum key-switching) attacks the other cost — evk memory — by reusing a single evk₁ iteratively (HROT by 2 = HROT by 1, applied twice) instead of holding K distinct evks. It cuts evk footprint ~4× but does not reduce computation, and it only pays off if you can keep that one evk (plus a few ciphertexts, ~217MB total) resident in cache. The paper is explicit: you cannot use both hoisting and MinKS together — they optimize incompatible op orderings.

Now you're ready: this is a real fork, not a footnote. Which one wins depends entirely on your hardware's cache size — which is exactly the GPU-vs-ASIC gap in the next primer.
7GPUs vs. ASICs: same math, wildly different cache budgets▾

The paper frames three concrete hardware gaps (D1–D3) between GPUs and prior custom FHE ASICs. D1, the crucial one: ASIC proposals assumed 180–512MB of on-chip cache and 72–92TB/s of effective cache bandwidth; a real A100 80GB has only 40MB of L2 and under 2TB/s of DRAM bandwidth. D2: ASICs assumed 25–170 TOPS of dedicated modular-multiply throughput vs. an A100's 19.5 TOPS of general integer multiply (each modular mult costs several GPU instructions, so the effective gap exceeds 40×). D3: ASICs have datapaths custom-built for NTT/BConv's data-exchange patterns; GPUs don't (though newer ones, like H100's NoC, are closing this).

D1 is why MinKS (which needs hundreds of MB of cache to actually reuse evks) works on ASICs but not GPUs — GPU libraries (100×, Cheddar) use hoisting instead, because trading more computation for less DRAM traffic is the right call when you have compute to spare but no cache.

Now you're ready: "GPUs have less cache" isn't a throwaway detail — it's the reason the whole rest of the paper exists. Every algorithm choice downstream traces back to D1.
8DRAM banks, ACT/PRE, and where PIM sits▾

You already know this shape: a DRAM bank holds an array plus row/column decoders and I/O sense amplifiers (IOSAs); an ACT copies a row into the IOSAs, RD/WR pulls chunks out via the column decoder, and PRE restores the row for the next access. Normally different banks hide each other's ACT/PRE behind a shared I/O channel. Realized PIM products (UPMEM, HBM-PIM, GDDR6-AiM) place small compute units right next to each bank to exploit the bank's own internal bandwidth instead of shipping data out over that shared channel.

The one wrinkle that matters for Anaheim specifically: when every bank fires a PIM op simultaneously ("all-bank" operation, which Anaheim uses for parallelism), there's no longer another bank's transfer to hide ACT/PRE behind — that overhead becomes fully exposed. Anaheim's data-layout contribution (Tab 4) exists specifically to amortize this.

Now you're ready: you already understand PIM's mechanism; the new part is that Anaheim's all-bank parallelism turns ACT/PRE from "usually hidden" into "the thing you now have to engineer around."
The whole story in one place

Anaheim in a nutshell

The false assumption everyone shared, the measurement that broke it, and the narrow, surgical fix that follows once you believe the measurement.

For years, FHE hardware research assumed the bottleneck was NTT/BConv — the compute-heavy transforms behind every polynomial multiplication — and built ASICs with hundreds of megabytes of cache and tens of TB/s of internal bandwidth to feed them. GPUs can't match that: an A100 80GB has 40MB of L2 versus the 180–512MB ASIC designs assumed, and under 2TB/s of DRAM bandwidth versus 72–92TB/s of assumed cache bandwidth. So GPU libraries reach for hoisting instead of the cache-hungry MinKS, trading more computation for less data movement — a reasonable, well-understood adaptation.

Except that adaptation has a side effect nobody had measured carefully: hoisting shrinks ModSwitch time so much, and pushes the remaining element-wise ops (HADD, PMULT, accumulation) into the wider extended modulus, that those "trivial" ops balloon to 45–48% of bootstrapping time on an A100 and 68–69% on an RTX 4090 — dwarfing NTT and BConv. These ops have under 2 ops/byte of arithmetic intensity (GPUs want 10–40), so they are flatly DRAM-bandwidth-bound, and no amount of extra compute throughput or kernel fusion fixes that.

Anaheim's fix is narrow by design. Rather than accelerating everything, it offloads only the element-wise ops into PIM units placed next to each DRAM bank (or on HBM's logic die, in a custom-HBM variant), where internal bandwidth is far higher than the external DRAM interface. NTT, BConv, and automorphism — the parts that are actually compute-bound or need complex data movement — stay on the GPU. A software framework (the "glue") reorders op sequences, fuses automorphism with accumulation, and exposes a CUDA-style API so PIM kernels slot into an existing GPU pipeline with only microseconds of transition overhead.

The upshot: across six real FHE/DNN workloads, Anaheim delivers 1.06–1.74× workload speedup and 1.62–3.14× energy-delay-product improvement, using PIM units that add only ≈7.6–9.9% area overhead per DRAM die — no new DRAM process, no custom ASIC, and GPU hardware modifications the paper argues are "manageable."

🍯 Key takeaways

  • The debunked assumption: FHE-on-GPU is not primarily bottlenecked by NTT/BConv — it's bottlenecked by element-wise ops starved of DRAM bandwidth.
  • The mechanism: hoisting (the right GPU-side algorithm given limited cache, D1) shrinks ModSwitch time and grows the element-wise share to 45–69% of bootstrapping.
  • The fix is surgical: offload only element-wise ops to PIM; keep NTT/BConv/automorphism on the GPU, where they're already efficient.
  • Software makes it work: reordering + automorphism fusion + a CUDA-style PIM-launch API, with microsecond (not millisecond) transition overhead.
  • A data layout (column partitioning / PolyGroups) is required to amortize ACT/PRE once every bank fires a PIM op at once — without it, gains vanish (2.1–2.2× slower, per the paper's ablation).
  • Two microarchitecture variants: near-bank PIM (like HBM-PIM/GDDR6-AiM) and custom-HBM (PIM on the logic die, avoiding DRAM-process constraints).
  • Net results: 1.06–1.74× speedup, 1.38–2.05× energy reduction, 1.62–3.14× EDP improvement, at ≈7.6–9.9% die-area overhead.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about Anaheim

Each picture stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the analogy stops being true.

The core discovery · where the time goes

The highway vs. the errands

NTT and BConv are the highway: dense, fast, high-throughput — a truck moving a lot of cargo per mile of road (per byte fetched). Element-wise ops are the local errands: dozens of tiny stops, each one paying the full cost of getting the truck moving again. Nobody times the errands because the highway looks like the hard part — until you clock a real delivery day and find the errands ate the afternoon.

Where it breaks: the errands aren't slow because there are many of them — they're slow because each one is starved of bandwidth (<2 ops/byte). A faster truck (more GPU compute) doesn't help; you need more/faster roads to each stop (more effective bandwidth), which is exactly what PIM supplies.
The constraint · D1 (cache gap)

The studio apartment vs. the mansion

An ASIC accelerator is a custom-built mansion with a walk-in pantry the size of a small house (180–512MB of cache) — you can stockpile the evk and never restock. A GPU is a studio apartment (40MB L2 on an A100): you simply cannot hoard 136MB evks and 27MB ciphertexts on the shelf. So GPU "cooking" (algorithm choice) has to change — smaller trips, more prep — not because GPUs are worse chefs, but because the pantry is smaller.

Where it breaks: the studio-apartment dweller can't just "buy a bigger fridge" — DRAM bandwidth itself is power-limited (the paper cites this explicitly), so brute-force scaling isn't on the table the way it might be for a literal apartment upgrade.
The fix · PIM offload

Sending the sous-chef into the pantry

Instead of hauling ingredients (evks, plaintexts) across the kitchen to one central chef every time, Anaheim puts a small sous-chef (a PIM unit with 8 modular multiply-accumulate units) right inside the pantry (next to each DRAM bank). The sous-chef only does the simple prep work — chopping, mixing (element-wise ops) — while the head chef (GPU) still handles the technique-heavy dishes (NTT/BConv) at the main station.

Where it breaks: the sous-chef can't do everything in the pantry — complex plating (automorphism's data shuffling) still has to go to the head chef, and every pantry-chef needs the shelf organized a specific way (column partitioning) or they spend more time hunting for ingredients than cooking.
The fork · hoisting vs. MinKS

The shared shuttle vs. the reused key card

Hoisting is a shared airport shuttle: instead of K separate cars each driving the same first leg (ModUp) to the terminal, one shuttle does that leg once carrying all K passengers, then they split up. MinKS is a reused key card: instead of issuing K different master keys (evks), you issue one and swipe it K times in sequence — cheaper to mint, but you now do K sequential swipes instead of one parallel batch.

Where it breaks: you can't run both systems at once — the shuttle needs everyone traveling together (batched, extended-modulus ops); the reused key needs sequential access. The paper is explicit that hoisting and MinKS are mutually exclusive within one linear transform.
The layout · column partitioning & PolyGroup

Organizing the warehouse shelf by order, not by arrival

If a warehouse shelves items in the order they arrived, filling one order means walking the whole aisle. Anaheim's PolyGroup layout instead pre-plans the shelf: related polynomials (the ones a PIM instruction will touch together) sit in the same row, so one aisle visit (one DRAM row activation) serves many chunks of the order at once — amortizing the walk (ACT/PRE) instead of paying it per item.

Where it breaks: this only works because FHE's computation pattern is entirely static and known in advance — the warehouse can pre-plan because every "order" (op sequence) is identical every time. A workload with unpredictable access patterns couldn't pre-organize the shelf this way.

The waste eliminated · one-time-use data

Packing material that never needs to leave the warehouse

evks and the plaintexts p̂ᵢ are "one-time-use data that never leaves DRAM" — like packing peanuts used once inside the warehouse and thrown away on the spot. A GPU-only pipeline still trucks that packing material to the loading dock (GPU cache/registers) and back, even though it was never meant to travel. PIM lets the peanuts stay exactly where they were used: Anaheim eliminates 1.20GB of such DRAM traffic in its D=4, K=8 running example alone.

Where it breaks: it's not entirely free — some data (like ModUp(a), up to 68MB in the running example) still has to be written back from GPU cache to DRAM first, so PIM can find it. The saving is large, not total.
Section by section

How Anaheim actually works

The hardware gap that forces the algorithm choice → the measurement that finds the real bottleneck → the software framework that offloads it → the PIM microarchitecture and data layout that make offloading pay off.

1 · The hardware gap that decides the algorithm (§III)

Table III (below, reconstructed from the paper) makes D1 concrete: an A100 80GB has 1802GB/s of DRAM bandwidth and 40MB of L2 cache; an RTX 4090 has 939GB/s and even less cache headroom. Prior ASIC proposals assumed 180–512MB of cache and 72–92TB/s of effective cache bandwidth — orders of magnitude beyond what any GPU offers. Given that gap, GPU FHE libraries (100×, Cheddar) correctly choose hoisting over MinKS: hoisting trades more computation for less DRAM traffic, which is the right trade when compute is relatively cheap and DRAM bandwidth is the scarce resource.

2 · Hoisting vs. MinKS, quantified (§III-B/C)

The paper's running example (D=4, K=8, CoeffToSlot linear transform inside bootstrapping) gives exact figures for the trade:

AlgorithmevksPlaintexts# (I)NTT (limbs)Cache needed for reuse
Baseline4.03GB1.14GB12,792—
Hoisting4.03GB1.46GB5,188>8MB
MinKS1.02GB1.14GB12,792>217MB

Hoisting cuts (I)NTT limb-ops from 12,792 to 5,188 — a 2.47× reduction — at the cost of larger plaintexts (1.14→1.46GB) but needs only ~8MB of cache to realize (well within GPU L2). MinKS cuts evk memory 4.03→1.02GB (~4×) but needs >217MB resident to actually reuse that single evk — squarely in ASIC territory, not GPU. This single table is why GPU libraries hoist and ASICs (with MinKS) go a different direction.

3 · The measurement that changes everything (§IV)

Using Cheddar (a fast, modern GPU FHE library), the paper measures T_boot,eff while sweeping the decomposition number D on both A100 80GB and RTX 4090. The result: element-wise ops consume 45–48% of bootstrapping time on A100, 68–69% on RTX 4090 — regardless of D. Switching from hoisting to MinKS on the same GPU drops the element-wise share to ~28% on A100 (comparable to a normal HMULT/HROT's 20–27%), confirming hoisting itself is the mechanism inflating this share. Kernel fusion (already state-of-the-art in Cheddar) and increasing fftIter (decomposing linear-transform matrices further) both fail to meaningfully help — fusion is limited because evks/b/p̂ᵢ must still be loaded from DRAM, and higher fftIter trades element-wise time for a worse L_eff.

◆
Why this is genuinely surprising. Every architectural paper before this one (BTS, ARK, SHARP, CraterLake, F1) built custom datapaths for NTT/BConv. On a real GPU running the algorithm real libraries actually use, that's optimizing the minority of the time. Compute-throughput improvements (RTX 4090's 2.1× higher integer TOPS over A100) do speed up NTT/BConv (2.0×) and BConv (1.4×) — confirming they're compute-bound — but do nothing for element-wise ops, which need bandwidth, not FLOPs.

4 · Offloading element-wise ops to PIM (§V-A/B)

The paper first tries a "cheat": measure a hoisted linear transform, then simulate 4× higher DRAM bandwidth by underclocking the GPU core to 1/4 (which also cuts cache bandwidth 1/4) while holding the DRAM clock fixed, then dividing execution time by 4. Result: element-wise ops get 2.84× faster and automorphism 2.54× faster — but ModSwitch variants (NTT/BConv-bound) barely move, confirming those are compute-, not bandwidth-, bound. Quadrupling real external DRAM bandwidth is power-infeasible, so Anaheim instead exploits DRAM's much higher internal bandwidth via PIM — reaching similar per-op speedups without touching the external interface.

Crucially, Anaheim restricts PIM to only element-wise ops: NTT/BConv (inside ModSwitch) are compute-bound and gain little from more bandwidth, and automorphism's complex data permutation is hard for PIM to execute cheaply. Element-wise ops, by contrast, are "embarrassingly parallel" — an op over L limbs × N coefficients decomposes into L×N independent scalar ops — which is exactly the shape a highly parallel, simple PIM unit wants.

5 · The software framework (§V-C)

Four design goals shape it: minimize GPU hardware changes, keep the PIM device abstract (chip designers need not know its microarchitecture), minimize PIM↔GPU transition overhead, and make PIM effortless for programmers. Concretely: a new CUDA Runtime API call (cudaLaunchPIMKernel) queues PIM kernels asynchronously in the existing stream-ordered execution model — transitions cost only microseconds against PIM kernels that run hundreds of microseconds. Because FHE's computation graph is entirely static, memory addresses can be predetermined ahead of time. A software-inserted, clflush-like write-back (without cache invalidation) keeps DRAM coherent with GPU L2 before PIM reads it. A high-level library hides all of this behind ordinary-looking code (LinearTransform lt(matrix, context); lt.Evaluate(...)).

6 · PIM microarchitecture (§VI-A)

Each PIM unit sits beside a DRAM bank (near-bank variant) or on HBM's logic die serving several banks (custom-HBM variant). It contains an instruction decoder, 8 modular multiply-accumulate (MMAC) units — matching the 256-bit DRAM global I/O datapath at 32 bits/word — a 224-bit-wide data buffer, and control logic. The MMAC units are general-purpose modular arithmetic (not fixed per-op logic, since logic on a DRAM process node is costly), support Montgomery reduction (exploiting that eligible primes satisfy Qᵢ ≡ 1 mod 2N), and use small 28-bit primes with double-prime scaling to keep area/energy low without sacrificing precision. Table II's instruction set spans basic ops (Add, Mult, MAC), constant-embedded ops (CMAC), and compound ops (PAccum⟨K⟩, CAccum⟨K⟩, Tensor) that fuse an entire accumulation chain into one instruction.

7 · Column partitioning & PolyGroups (§VI-B/C)

When every bank fires a PIM op simultaneously (all-bank operation), no other bank's transfer is available to hide ACT/PRE behind — so Anaheim's data layout is designed to amortize that cost across many chunk accesses per row activation. Data is partitioned into die groups (e.g., one HBM stack per group on A100, each handling L/5 limbs), then within a bank into column groups holding several 256-bit chunks each, with limbs wrapped across adjacent rows to form a row group. A programmer-visible PolyGroup allocation places the polynomials one PIM instruction will touch together in the same row/column groups, so accessing 8 chunks at a time (for example) never triggers an extra ACT/PRE. Skipping this (allocating contiguously instead) makes element-wise ops 2.24× (A100) and 2.11× (RTX 4090) slower in the paper's ablation — enough to erase most of the benefit.

8 · Two realizations: near-bank PIM vs. custom-HBM (§V-D/§VI-D)

Near-bank PIM follows real products (HBM-PIM, GDDR6-AiM): the PIM unit sits directly beside each DRAM bank, inside the DRAM die. Custom-HBM instead places all PIM units on HBM's logic die, connected to multiple banks via additional through-silicon vias (TSVs), exploiting advanced packaging (hybrid bonding) to add TSV density cheaply. The paper considers custom-HBM "especially practical" because it avoids modifying the restrictive DRAM process node at all — only the logic die, which can use a normal logic process (from HBM4 onward).

Put real numbers on it

Worked examples

Four calculations recomputed from the paper's own stated figures, then an interactive estimator for the gap between a naive bandwidth argument and Anaheim's actual reported speedups.

Example 1 — sizing an evk from first principles

The paper states a polynomial can be as large as 17MB and a full evk 136MB, with D = 4 by default and evk = 2·D polynomials in the extended modulus.

  1. Polynomial count: 2·D = 2×4 = 8 polynomials per evk.
  2. Naive check: 8 × 17MB = 136MB — matches the paper's stated evk size exactly, even though evk polynomials technically carry L+α limbs (slightly more than a plain L-limb polynomial), which the 17MB figure evidently already reflects as a "large" polynomial size.
  3. Takeaway: every evk-heavy op (HMULT, HROT) moves ~8× the data of a single polynomial op — which is exactly why evk traffic, not raw polynomial size, dominates FHE's memory footprint.
◆
Takeaway: D isn't just an algorithm knob — it's a direct multiplier on evk size (2·D polynomials) and, per Primer 6, also on L_eff. Every D you increase buys more multiplications between bootstraps at the cost of proportionally larger evks.

Example 2 — hoisting's and MinKS's numbers, reconciled

From the D=4, K=8 CoeffToSlot table (Tab 4, section 2): Baseline 12,792 (I)NTT limb-ops, Hoisting 5,188; Baseline 4.03GB evks, MinKS 1.02GB.

  1. Hoisting's (I)NTT reduction: 12,792 / 5,188 = 2.4657…× ≈ 2.47× — matches the paper's claim exactly.
  2. MinKS's evk reduction: 4.03GB / 1.02GB = 3.95× ≈ "4× fewer evks" as stated (K=8 rotations sharing one evk instead of eight explains the near-exact 4× — the paper's 8 rotations reduce to roughly 2 effective evk-equivalents of traffic due to reuse patterns not fully detailed in the text; treat "~4×" as the paper's own summary figure).
  3. MinKS's cache requirement, checked: the paper says MinKS needs ">217MB [1 evk + 3 ciphertexts]." One evk = 136MB; three ciphertexts at 27MB each = 81MB. 136 + 81 = 217MB — exact match.
▲
Misconception it kills: "MinKS is strictly better since it needs 4× less evk memory." It needs 4× less total evk memory, but only pays off if 217MB of it stays resident simultaneously — a GPU's 40MB L2 can't hold that, so MinKS's smaller footprint is irrelevant without ASIC-scale cache.

Example 3 — how much DRAM traffic PIM actually removes

For the D=4, K=8 running example, the paper states PIM eliminates 1.20GB of evk/plaintext DRAM access while adding back up to 68MB of ModUp(a) write-back. At the full bootstrapping-workload level (simulated), PIM cuts GPU-side DRAM access to 6.03GB, a stated 6.15× reduction versus the no-PIM baseline, and only 1.86× above an idealized no-PIM case (MinKS + unlimited cache).

  1. Net win in the running example: 1.20GB eliminated vs. 68MB added back → a ~17.6× ratio of traffic removed to traffic added (1200MB / 68MB ≈ 17.6), so the trade is heavily favorable even before counting the compute-side benefits.
  2. Recomputing the baseline: if PIM's 6.03GB is 6.15× lower than the no-PIM baseline, the baseline must be 6.03 × 6.15 ≈ 37.1GB of GPU-side DRAM access for the full bootstrapping workload.
  3. Recomputing the ideal case: if PIM's 6.03GB is only 1.86× above the ideal (MinKS, unlimited cache) case, the ideal must be 6.03 / 1.86 ≈ 3.24GB.
  4. Energy: despite element-wise-op DRAM access converting to PIM-side access (and even growing slightly in raw amount), the much shorter physical distance data travels yields a 2.87× overall DRAM-access energy reduction.
◆
Takeaway: PIM doesn't just move the bottleneck — it lands close to the theoretical ideal (1.86× above a cache-unlimited fantasy) without requiring any actual cache expansion, because it exploits internal DRAM bandwidth instead.

Example 4 — a naive bandwidth estimate vs. what Anaheim actually delivers

The paper's own "cheat" experiment (§V-A) quadruples effective DRAM bandwidth by underclocking the GPU core to 1/4 and dividing execution time by 4: element-wise ops speed up 2.84×, automorphism 2.54×. Real Anaheim (PIM) achieves an effective internal-bandwidth increase of 16× (A100, near-bank) or 8× (RTX 4090, near-bank) per Table III — but the overall workload speedup is only 1.24–1.74× (A100) or 1.06–1.49× (RTX 4090), because element-wise ops are only part of total time and PIM adds its own overheads (write-backs, exposed ACT/PRE, kernel-launch transitions).

A colleague says "just add more on-chip cache to the GPU and MinKS will work great, since it needs less evk memory overall." Using D1 and the >217MB cache figure, explain why this doesn't fix the GPU case the way it does for ASICs.
Show answer
MinKS's benefit requires holding one evk (136MB) plus several ciphertexts (~217MB total) resident simultaneously to actually reuse it across K sequential rotations. A100's L2 is 40MB — even a large cache bump wouldn't close a 5×+ gap without approaching ASIC-scale (180–512MB) cache, which changes the chip's economics entirely (die area, cost, and yield), not just a parameter tweak. Meanwhile hoisting achieves its benefit with only ~8MB of cache — already within reach. Catches: treating "needs less total memory" as equivalent to "needs less cache." MinKS's memory savings are in total DRAM footprint; its performance benefit specifically requires on-chip residency, which is the actually scarce resource.
Diagnose
Why does hoisting — an optimization that reduces the amount of computation and (I)NTT ops — end up increasing the fraction of time spent on element-wise ops, rather than decreasing overall time spent everywhere proportionally?
Show answer
Hoisting shrinks ModSwitch (INTT/BConv/NTT) time by a large factor (2.47× fewer (I)NTT limb-ops in the example) by batching ModUp/ModDown once instead of K times. But it also moves the PMULT and accumulation ops into the extended modulus PQ (larger plaintexts, 1.14→1.46GB in the example) to make that batching possible. So the numerator (element-wise op time) grows somewhat while the denominator (total time) shrinks a lot — the ratio swings hard toward element-wise, even though hoisting is an unambiguous net win in absolute time. Catches: assuming "the fraction went up" means "that category got worse." Here the category's absolute cost is roughly flat-to-slightly-up while everything else dropped sharply — a classic Amdahl's-law illusion.
Design
Anaheim reports PIM cuts GPU-side DRAM access to 6.03GB, stated as 6.15× lower than the no-PIM baseline and 1.86× above an idealized (MinKS, unlimited cache) case. Recompute the baseline and ideal figures in GB, and explain why the ideal case still isn't zero.
Show answer
Baseline = 6.03GB × 6.15 ≈ 37.1GB. Ideal = 6.03GB / 1.86 ≈ 3.24GB. The ideal case isn't zero because even with unlimited cache and MinKS's reduced evk footprint, compulsory cache misses still occur the first time each evk/plaintext is fetched from DRAM — an infinite cache eliminates repeated fetches, not the first one. PIM's 6.03GB sits much closer to this compulsory-miss floor (1.86×) than to the realistic no-PIM baseline (6.15× away), which is the paper's real claim: PIM gets most of the theoretical benefit without needing the cache that would be required to get there conventionally. Catches: assuming "ideal" means literally zero DRAM traffic. Even a perfect cache still pays for compulsory misses — the ideal case is a floor, not an absence of cost.
Design
RTX 4090 has a higher element-wise-op share of bootstrapping time (68–69%) than A100 (45–48%), yet Anaheim's measured speedup on RTX 4090 (1.06–1.49×) is lower than on A100 (1.24–1.74×). Using Table III's numbers, explain the apparent contradiction.
Show answer
A higher memory-bound fraction only translates into a bigger PIM speedup if PIM can also deliver a large effective bandwidth multiplier on that fraction. Table III gives A100 near-bank PIM a 16× effective bandwidth increase (5 HBM2E stacks, 64 banks/die, one PIM die group per 8-die stack) versus only 8× for RTX 4090 near-bank PIM (12 GDDR6X dies, 32 banks/die, one PIM die group per 4 dies) — GDDR6X's architecture simply offers less internal bandwidth headroom to exploit per die than HBM2E's. So RTX 4090 has "more of the pie" to attack but a smaller lever to attack it with, and the paper explicitly attributes its lower speedup to this lower BW-increase factor, not to the element-wise share. Catches: assuming the bottleneck's size alone determines the achievable speedup. The size of the fix's lever (here, the internal-vs-external bandwidth ratio a given DRAM technology and PIM placement can expose) matters just as much as the size of the problem.
Loose ends

Frequently asked

The questions that come up once the mechanism clicks.

QIs Anaheim an ASIC that replaces GPUs for FHE?▾
No — it's an augmentation. The GPU keeps doing NTT, BConv, and automorphism exactly as before; only element-wise ops move to PIM units living inside DRAM. The paper is explicit that this "requires only manageable hardware modifications to the GPU," pointing to Samsung's MI100-PIM (a real AMD MI100 GPU integrated with HBM-PIM stacks) as an existence proof that GPU+PIM integration is practical today.
QIf NTT isn't the bottleneck, why did every prior FHE accelerator paper target it?▾
Because on custom ASICs with hundreds of MB of cache, NTT/BConv genuinely can dominate — those designs can afford MinKS and keep evks resident, so the remaining time really is compute-bound. The paper's finding is specific to GPUs running the algorithm GPU libraries actually use (hoisting); it doesn't claim NTT was never a bottleneck anywhere, only that the field's dominant hardware target (GPUs, as a practical near-term solution) has a different bottleneck than assumed.
QWhat exactly gets sent to PIM vs. stays on the GPU?▾
Element-wise ops only: HADD, PMULT, MAC, and the compound accumulation instructions (PAccum, CAccum, Tensor). NTT, INTT, BConv (all inside ModSwitch, compute-bound) and automorphism (complex data permutation, hard for PIM) stay on the GPU. The one exception the paper adds is automorphism fusion — automorphism itself runs on GPU, but gets algebraically reordered and fused with the accumulation step around it to cut redundant DRAM traffic.
QWhy propose two different PIM microarchitectures (near-bank and custom-HBM)?▾
Near-bank PIM mirrors existing shipped products (HBM-PIM, GDDR6-AiM), so it's the lower-risk, precedented option. Custom-HBM avoids modifying the DRAM die's process node at all — a genuinely hard, expensive thing to do — by moving all PIM logic to HBM's logic die (which can use an ordinary logic process) and adding more through-silicon vias for internal bandwidth. The paper finds custom-HBM "especially practical" for exactly this reason, even though it shows a slightly lower assumed bandwidth increase (4× vs. 16×) in the paper's configuration.
QDoes using 28-bit primes and PIM offloading hurt precision?▾
The paper states they checked that all optimizations (reordering, fusion, small-prime PIM arithmetic) "do not damage the precision." Small 28-bit primes are used with double-prime scaling — a known technique (also used by Cheddar) for maintaining high precision despite a smaller word size — and data is still stored at 32-bit granularity in DRAM, truncated to 28 bits only when actually fed into a PIM unit.
QHow much silicon does this actually cost?▾
Per Table III, near-bank PIM adds about 9.69% of die area on A100 (10.7mm² per die) and 7.58% on RTX 4090 (7.26mm² per die); the custom-HBM variant adds 9.94% to HBM's logic die. All were deliberately kept within roughly 10% of die area as a design target.
QDoes DRAM capacity, not just bandwidth, become a problem?▾
Yes — the paper flags this as an emerging limiter that prior architectural studies didn't elaborate on. RTX 4090's 24GB DRAM hit out-of-memory failures even on ResNet20, and ResNet18-AESPA (ImageNet-scale) needs over 40GB. Prior ASIC designs with only two HBM stacks, or FPGA boards with 8GB HBM2, face the same wall — capacity, not just bandwidth, will matter more as FHE workloads scale up.
QDoes this generalize beyond the CKKS scheme studied here?▾
Explicitly flagged as future work in the paper, not demonstrated. The authors note BGV and BFV (other FHE schemes) share the same KeyMult operation structure, and FHEW/TFHE require a similar parallel-multiplication process for their evks — so a direct extension seems plausible — but they state that the same detailed hardware/algorithm analysis (§III–IV) would need to be redone for those schemes before concluding the same bottleneck applies.

Reference

Glossary, a dated summary card, and the honest provenance notes.

Core terms
CKKSThe FHE scheme this paper accelerates — handles real/complex numbers, ideal for private ML. Operates on polynomials in a ring R_Q via RNS limbs.
Limb / RNSA polynomial's modulus Q is split into L smaller primes; each limb is the polynomial reduced mod one prime, enabling parallel, machine-word arithmetic.
Evaluation key (evk)Extra data (2·D polynomials in extended modulus PQ) required for HMULT and HROT; ~136MB with the paper's default D=4.
HROT / linear transformCiphertext rotation; K parallel HROT+PMULT pairs, summed, form a homomorphic linear transform (this paper's D=4, K=8 running example).
ModSwitchINTT → BConv → NTT sequence for changing a polynomial's modulus; compute-bound, dominant in prior ASIC designs.
Bootstrapping / L_eff / T_boot,effPeriodic level-refresh required by FHE; L_eff = mults available before the next bootstrap; T_boot,eff = bootstrap time / L_eff, the paper's core efficiency metric.
HoistingBatches ModUp once across K rotations instead of K times; cuts (I)NTT ops 2.47× in the paper's example but grows plaintext size and element-wise-op share.
MinKSMinimum key-switching; reuses one evk iteratively instead of K distinct evks; cuts evk memory ~4× but needs >217MB resident cache to pay off — ASIC-only in practice.
Supporting terms
Decomposition number (D)D = L/α; controls evk size (2·D polynomials) and L_eff; the paper defaults to D=4.
NTT / INTT / BConvNumber-theoretic transform (integer FFT) and its inverse; basis conversion between moduli Q and PQ — the compute-bound trio inside ModSwitch.
AutomorphismCoefficient-permutation step specific to HROT; complex data movement, kept on the GPU rather than offloaded to PIM.
Near-bank PIMPIM units placed directly beside each DRAM bank inside the DRAM die (as in HBM-PIM, GDDR6-AiM); one of Anaheim's two microarchitecture variants.
Custom-HBMAnaheim's alternative: PIM units on HBM's logic die (not the DRAM die), connected via added TSVs — avoids modifying the DRAM process node.
MMAC unitModular multiply-accumulate unit; 8 per PIM unit, matching the 256-bit DRAM I/O datapath at 28-bit (Montgomery-reducible) prime granularity.
PolyGroup / column partitioningAnaheim's DRAM data layout: places co-accessed polynomials/limbs in the same row/column groups so PIM's all-bank operation amortizes ACT/PRE overhead.
EDPEnergy-delay product; Anaheim's headline result is a 1.62–3.14× EDP improvement across workloads and PIM architectures.

📌 Summary card · as of 2026-08-30 · HPCA 2025 paper

  • What: Anaheim offloads only the DRAM-bandwidth-bound element-wise ops of FHE CKKS to PIM units inside DRAM, leaving compute-bound NTT/BConv/automorphism on the GPU.
  • Why: real GPU measurement (Cheddar) shows element-wise ops are 45–48% (A100) to 68–69% (RTX 4090) of bootstrapping time — not NTT/BConv, which prior architecture work targeted.
  • Root cause: GPUs lack ASIC-scale cache (40MB vs. 180–512MB assumed), forcing hoisting over MinKS — and hoisting is precisely what inflates the element-wise share.
  • The fix: a software framework (reordering, automorphism fusion, CUDA-style PIM launch API) plus a near-bank or custom-HBM PIM unit (8 MMAC units, 28-bit primes) and a column-partitioned/PolyGroup data layout.
  • Results: 1.06–1.74× workload speedup, 1.38–2.05× energy reduction, 1.62–3.14× EDP improvement, at ≈7.6–9.9% die-area overhead; PIM-instruction-level speedups of 1.65–10.33×.
  • Limits acknowledged: DRAM capacity (not just bandwidth) becomes limiting at scale (RTX 4090 OoM on ResNet20/ResNet18-AESPA); ASICs with MinKS + massive cache (e.g., SHARP) still outperform Anaheim by 8.9–17.2× on raw latency.
  • Not yet shown: extension to BGV/BFV/TFHE (structurally plausible, explicitly left as future work); pipelining PIM and GPU kernels (judged low-value given how much time PIM already removes).
