SOSP 2026 · A Field Guide

Deciding not how to split the work, but where it runs on the wafer

A million-core wafer turns every compilation decision into a physical one: communication cost depends on distance, not just data volume. This is the plain-English story of Wavel, the system that makes placement itself the object the compiler searches over.

Before you begin

Quick glossary, then the primers

You already know memory hierarchies and interconnect scaling — these primers skip that and focus on the compiler- and wafer-specific pieces the paper needs.

1What a wafer-scale accelerator physically is▾

Instead of a handful of large dies connected by off-chip links, a wafer-scale chip etches up to a million small cores directly onto one silicon wafer. Each core has a private memory and a router; cores talk only to their mesh neighbors, hop by hop — there is no shared bus and no off-chip hop for most traffic.

The WSE-3 used in this paper packs ~950,000 cores and 44 GB of on-chip memory into one device, with tens of PB/s of aggregate on-chip bandwidth.

Now you're ready: "compiling for the wafer" means deciding exactly which rectangle of that mesh each operator runs on — not just how much of it.
2The sharding-oriented view — and why GPUs got away with it▾

GPU/TPU compilers (Roller, Welder, Ladder) and multi-GPU planners (Alpa, nnScaler, GShard) mostly search for a logical shard plan: how to split tensors and ops across devices. Physical realization — where a shard actually lands relative to its neighbors — barely matters, because a shared-memory system (or a small number of coarse communication tiers) absorbs the difference.

Wavel's claim is that this assumption silently breaks at wafer scale.

Now you're ready: the paper's whole argument is that "finish the shard plan, then map it physically" is the wrong order of operations once physical location determines cost this directly.
3GEMM, GEMV, and operator graphs (fast recap)▾

A transformer layer is a small dataflow graph of operators: GEMV/GEMM for projections and attention, elementwise ops, reductions like allreduce. Wavel's compiler operates on this graph, deciding where each operator's rectangle of cores sits and when it may run relative to its neighbors.

Now you're ready: "MeshKernel" in the next section is just one of these operators, bound to a concrete physical rectangle instead of an abstract shard.
4Regions — the wafer's analog of "ways"▾

A Region is a rectangular block of cores: top-left coordinate (x, y) plus width and height. Every operator is bound to one (or, for irregular MoE layouts, a composition of several) Regions. Cache partitioning allocates in units of ways; Wavel allocates in units of Regions — physical area with a fixed location, not just a count.

Now you're ready: "partitioning" here means literally drawing rectangles on the mesh, not counting abstract shares.
5MLIR and where Wavel plugs in▾

MLIR is a compiler-infrastructure framework for building layered IRs. Wavel lowers high-level tensor programs through MLIR first, reusing its device-agnostic simplification and canonicalization passes, and only takes over once placement, spatial alignment, and resource conflicts must be made explicit — i.e., once the problem becomes physical.

Now you're ready: Wavel is not a whole new compiler stack; it's a targeted spatial-scheduling layer inserted at exactly the point where sharding-style compilers stop being sufficient.
6Cost models and search — A*, ternary search, memoization▾

Because every candidate schedule is fully placed, its cost (compute + communication cycles) can be estimated directly from region sizes and hop distances — no guessing about missing physical detail. Wavel exploits three algorithmic tricks: ternary search for operators whose cost-vs-size curve has a single minimum (unimodal), A* with heuristics to order which candidate schedule to expand next, and memoization keyed by structure rather than absolute position.

Now you're ready: none of this changes what states are reachable — CUT and TUNE still define that — it only changes the order and cost of exploring them.
7MoE routing in one paragraph▾

Mixture-of-Experts models route each token through only a handful of "expert" sub-networks (top-k gating) instead of the whole network. Individual expert GEMV/GEMM operations are therefore small — spreading a small operator across the entire wafer wastes cores and inflates communication overhead relative to giving it a right-sized Region.

Now you're ready: this is exactly why Wavel's biggest calibrated gain (2.97×) shows up on MoE decode — right-sizing small expert regions matters most where the naive "use the whole chip" baseline is most wasteful.
8Measured, simulated, and calibrated — read the evidence type carefully▾

The paper mixes three kinds of evidence, and it matters which one backs a given number: (1) real WSE-3 hardware — dense-LLM throughput and the WaferLLM comparison; (2) the official Cerebras SDK cycle-level simulator — the one-layer case study, the schedule ablation, and the HPC/vector-search results; (3) a calibrated cost model — MoE speedup predictions (up to 2.97×), validated at 95–99% accuracy against hardware but not itself a hardware measurement.

Now you're ready: when a number sounds too clean, check which of these three categories it came from — the paper itself is careful to separate them, and this guide keeps that distinction throughout.
The whole story in one place

Wavel in a nutshell

The problem, the reframing that fixes it, and how the pieces connect.

Wafer-scale accelerators replace shared-memory hierarchies with a single large 2D mesh of private-memory cores. This is great for scaling — interconnect cost grows quadratically for shared memory but favorably for on-chip meshes — but it changes what a compiler must decide. Communication cost now depends sharply on where things are placed, not just how work is divided.

Existing DNN compilers (GPU-oriented: Roller, Welder, Ladder; distributed: Alpa, nnScaler, GShard; vendor: Cerebras Torch, Tenstorrent Torch) all inherit a sharding-oriented view: search for a good logical partition, then let a later lowering stage sort out placement. On a wafer, that later stage is where everything that matters — layout alignment, region overlap, memory fit — actually lives, and leaving it implicit means the compiler never really optimizes it.

Wavel's fix is to make the executable schedule itself the search object. MeshIR represents a schedule as explicit Regions, MeshTensors, and MeshKernels bound to physical rectangles. Two legal, locality-preserving refinement rules — CUT (split a shared region into two adjacent sub-regions) and TUNE (resize one operator's region around a fixed anchor) — expand one valid schedule into another, so the search never wastes effort on states that need physical repair later. A compositional cost model plus structural pruning (unimodal-curve search, translation-invariant memoization) then keeps this enormous space tractable.

The payoff: on WSE-3 hardware, Wavel delivers 1.31×–1.35× higher mean per-setting throughput than the hand-optimized WaferLLM, and up to 1.85× over a Full-Chip baseline on dense models; a calibrated cost model predicts up to 2.97× on MoE workloads over the same baseline — while compiling 28×–70× faster than a vendor pipeline on a smaller mesh accelerator (Tenstorrent Blackhole).

🧭 Key takeaways

  • A sharding-oriented compiler searches a logical shard plan; Wavel searches an executable schedule — placement, alignment, ordering fixed together.
  • MeshIR makes that schedule explicit: Region (rectangle) → MeshTensor (tensor bound to a Region) → MeshKernel (op bound to a Region) → MeshSchedule (the whole graph, placed).
  • CUT and TUNE are the only two ways to move between valid schedules — both are locality-preserving by construction.
  • Search stays practical thanks to compositional cost evaluation, unimodal per-operator cost curves (ternary search), and translation-invariant memoization.
  • Result: 1.31×–1.35× over WaferLLM, up to 1.85× (dense) / 2.97× (calibrated, MoE) over Full-Chip, and 28×–70× faster compilation on Tenstorrent Blackhole.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about Wavel

Read in any order — each card ends with an honest note about where the picture breaks down.

The core reframing · Plan vs schedule

The floor plan vs the square footage

A leasing agent quoting "1,200 sq ft" tells you nothing about where the kitchen or the load-bearing wall is. A logical shard plan is the square footage: it says how much work each device gets, not where anything actually sits relative to its neighbors. Wavel insists on the full floor plan — exact rooms, exact doors — before calling the space usable.

Where it breaks: square footage really is all you need on a GPU, where the "walls" (memory hierarchy) are shared and cheap to cross — the analogy only bites once crossing a wall costs real, distance-dependent time.
CUT · adjacency constraint

Cutting the cake along one straight line

You can slice a cake anywhere, but if you want both halves edible, cut it along one straight line through the middle — not into scattered scraps. CUT does the same: it only ever splits a shared region into two sub-regions along a full boundary edge, so x_B + x_C = N and the two pieces stay adjacent. A small alignment kernel (S) sits right at the cut, handling the handoff.

Where it breaks: a cake can be cut into oddly-shaped scraps if you don't mind mess; MeshIR's Regions must stay rectangular, so CUT can't carve out arbitrary shapes — MoE's non-rectangular expert layouts are handled instead by composing several rectangles per kernel.
TUNE · fixed anchor

The tailor's pinned corner

A tailor lets out a jacket by pinning one shoulder seam in place and letting the fabric grow from there — the seam that matters (the one facing the collar) never moves. TUNE resizes one operator's Region the same way: it pins the corner nearest the operator's producer and lets width and height grow or shrink from that fixed point, so the short data path to the producer is never disturbed.

Where it breaks: a tailor can also move the seam if needed; TUNE cannot relocate the anchor corner mid-refinement — a genuinely different placement requires a different schedule, not a TUNE step.
Unimodal cost · the sizing sweet spot

The Goldilocks office size

Too few desks and the team is compute-starved, waiting on each other; too many desks spread across too many floors and everyone burns time walking between them. GEMM/GEMV operators on the wafer show exactly this shape: too few cores and compute dominates, too many and communication overhead dominates, with one sweet spot in between. It's the same trade-off you already reason about when a tier gets over-provisioned with far memory that then costs interconnect bandwidth instead of buying real capacity headroom.

Where it breaks: not every operator is unimodal — elementwise and allgather ops are monotonic in region size — so Wavel only applies the fast ternary search where the curve shape has actually been confirmed to have one minimum.
Translation invariance · memoization

Reusing the same stencil

A stonemason cuts one stencil for a decorative tile pattern, then stamps it at every location on the wall — the pattern's quality depends only on its shape, not on which patch of wall it lands on. The best schedule for a set of operators in a fixed-size Region depends only on that Region's dimensions, not its absolute (x, y) — so Wavel solves each shape once, hashes it, and stamps the answer wherever the same shape recurs.

Where it breaks: this assumes a uniform mesh fabric; a wafer with faulty tiles or non-uniform routing at the edges would break the "same stencil everywhere" assumption — fault-map-aware placement is explicitly left to future work.
Locality · hop distance

The block you're actually zoned into

Just as a CXL-attached memory pool has a fixed round-trip latency budget regardless of what data you place in it, a wafer Region has a fixed hop-distance to its neighbors regardless of which operator you put there. Distance is a property of the location, and it's paid whether or not you plan for it — the only choice is whether the compiler accounts for it up front (Wavel) or discovers it as a penalty after the fact (sharding-oriented lowering).

Where it breaks: CXL/NUMA tiers are usually a handful of discrete latency classes; a wafer mesh has up to ~1000 graduated locality levels (Table 1), so the "tiering" here is far finer-grained than anything in a typical memory hierarchy.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Section by section, with the paper's key mechanisms rebuilt as diagrams you can read at a glance.

1 · MeshIR — making the schedule explicit

A Region is a rectangle R = ((x,y),(w,h)): a top-left coordinate and a width/height in cores. A MeshTensor binds a logical tensor to a Region. A MeshKernel binds a compute function (GEMM, GEMV, allreduce, …) to input/output MeshTensors and a region_list — the enclosing physical area the kernel occupies. A MeshSchedule pairs the compute graph with a concrete Region for every MeshKernel in it. Nothing in this object is left implicit.

2 · CUT and TUNE — the only two moves

CUT applies when two operators share a region: it partitions along one axis into two adjacent sub-regions (x_B + x_C = N), inserting alignment kernels at the new boundary. TUNE resizes a single operator's region around a fixed anchor corner chosen to minimize distance to its producer. Both are legal by construction — every resulting state is already an executable MeshSchedule, so search never has to backtrack out of an illegal state.

3 · Making search practical

Three structural properties keep the huge schedule space tractable: compositional cost evaluation (every MeshKernel is fully placed, so its cost is unambiguous and per-operator costs compose into a faithful whole-graph estimate); unimodal cost structure for GEMM/GEMV (too few cores → compute-bound, too many → communication-bound, one minimum in between — exploitable with ternary search); and decomposition with translation-invariant reuse (CUT shrinks each subproblem, and a solved layout is memoized by shape, not absolute position, so it's reused wherever the same shape recurs).

4 · Search engine — A*, DUPLICATE, PLACE

On top of CUT/TUNE, Wavel extends A* with domain heuristics that leave the reachable state space unchanged: they only reorder which legal state gets expanded next. Two composite shortcuts — DUPLICATE (replicate an operator across regions) and PLACE (pin a region) — collapse common CUT/TUNE sequences. Candidates exceeding SRAM capacity are discarded immediately; only dependency-ready groups advance; states are memoized by a translation-invariant structural key so isomorphic layouts at different (x, y) are never re-explored.

5 · Backend portability — one contract, two lowerings

MeshSchedule and the CUT/TUNE semantics are shared across targets; only the lowering is backend-specific. On Cerebras, a "Move" becomes a statically routed CSL program over colored wavelet routes. On Tenstorrent, the same logical Move expands into explicit reader/writer kernels, circular buffers, and runtime dispatch arguments — a controlled dataflow model rather than pure dataflow. Porting to a new mesh accelerator means writing a new lowering and profiling operator costs, not redefining the search object.

Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four traces using the paper's real measurements — a multiplicative ablation, a cycle-level case study, a search-space magnitude estimate, and a portability comparison.

① Why the ablation percentages multiply, not add

Table 3 reports, for Qwen3-30B-A3B decode at K=128: Uniform removes 55.4% of latency, Per-op removes 0.3% more, +CUT removes 9.9% more, +TUNE removes 24.5% more, for an overall 3.30× speedup. How does that arithmetic actually work?

  1. Start with baseline latency L₀ = 100 (Full-Chip serial execution).
  2. After Uniform (one shared rectangle instead of the whole chip): L₁ = 100 × (1 − 0.554) = 44.6.
  3. After Per-op (independently right-sized operators, still serial): L₂ = 44.6 × (1 − 0.003) = 44.47.
  4. After +CUT (cross-operator parallelism unlocked): L₃ = 44.47 × (1 − 0.099) = 40.07.
  5. After +TUNE (geometric refinement restored): L₄ = 40.07 × (1 − 0.245) = 30.25.
  6. Overall speedup = L₀ / L₄ = 100 / 30.25 = 3.31× — matching the paper's reported 3.30×.
💡
Each stage's percentage is a reduction of what's left, not of the original baseline — that's why they compound multiplicatively into a speedup rather than adding to 90.1% and stopping there.

② The one-layer LLaMA3-8B case: CUT's 1.52× in the simulator

On a 360×360 WSE-3 submesh in the Cerebras SDK simulator, WaferLLM runs Q, K, V serially on the whole region (its whole-layer discipline). Wavel instead applies CUT to place Q, K, V on three adjacent 120×360 regions running concurrently.

  1. WaferLLM baseline: the simulator measures 11,800 cycles for the full attention phase (Q, K, V run one after another on 360×360).
  2. Wavel's schedule: CUT splits the 360×360 region into three 120×360 bands; Q, K, V execute concurrently. The simulator measures 7,804 cycles.
  3. Speedup = 11,800 / 7,804 = 1.512× ≈ the paper's reported 1.52×.
  4. Same mechanism, second instance: later in the layer, Gate and Up run concurrently on two 180×360 regions rather than serially on the full 360×360 — the same CUT idea, applied again.
📐
This number is a Cerebras SDK simulator measurement, not silicon — it isolates the one degree of freedom WaferLLM's whole-layer mapping doesn't expose: partitioning within a layer.

③ How big is the search space Wavel actually avoids?

Table 4 gives an analytical candidate count before and after successive structural restrictions, for an 8-op MLP and for LLaMA3-8B.

  1. 8-op MLP, brute force: 4.10×10⁵¹ candidate schedules. After every restriction (executable expansion, unimodal search, structural hashing, specialized subproblems): 3.53×10².
  2. Orders-of-magnitude reduction = log₁₀(4.10×10⁵¹) − log₁₀(3.53×10²) = 51.61 − 2.55 ≈ 49.1 orders of magnitude — matching the paper's "about 49."
  3. LLaMA3-8B, brute force: 1.74×10²⁹⁶³ candidates. After restrictions: 3.68×10¹².
  4. Reduction = log₁₀(1.74×10²⁹⁶³) − log₁₀(3.68×10¹²) ≈ 2963.24 − 12.57 ≈ 2,951 orders of magnitude — matching the paper exactly.
🍯
Insight: these are derived counts of the space CUT/TUNE plus pruning removes from consideration — not a trace of states Wavel actually visited at runtime. The gap between "space avoided" and "iterations actually run" is exactly what makes ~500-iteration budgets practical.

④ Tenstorrent Blackhole: compilation speed vs. hand-tuned kernels

Table 5 reports host-side compilation time and device-side decode latency for two models against the vendor (vLLM/TT-Torch) and TTNN paths.

  1. Compilation, LLaMA3.1-8B: vLLM (TT-Torch) takes 668.7 s; Wavel takes 24.2 s → speedup = 668.7 / 24.2 = 27.6×.
  2. Compilation, Qwen3.5-4B: vLLM takes 682.0 s; Wavel takes 9.8 s → speedup = 682.0 / 9.8 = 69.6×.
  3. Execution, Qwen3.5-4B (no hand-tuned kernel library exists for this model): TTNN 181.7 ms vs Wavel 26.4 ms → 181.7 / 26.4 = 6.88× ≈ the paper's 6.9×.
  4. Execution, LLaMA3.1-8B (a hand-tuned kernel library does exist): TTNN 35.3 ms beats Wavel's 46.2 ms — Wavel is 0.76× as fast here, i.e., TTNN wins.
⚠
Honest reading: Wavel's advantage is compile time and generalization to models without hand-tuned libraries — not universal device-side dominance. Where a vendor has already hand-fused compound operators for a specific model (LLaMA on TTNN), that fusion still beats Wavel's current automatic backend.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
The Full-Chip baseline assigns every operator to the entire wafer. Why doesn't that maximize performance, given it uses the most hardware?
Show answer
Because GEMM/GEMV cost is unimodal in region size: too few cores makes an operator compute-bound, but too many makes it communication-bound (data now travels across a much larger, sparser region). Full-Chip always sits at the far "too many cores" end of that curve, especially for small operators like individual MoE experts.Misconception: that more allocated hardware always means more throughput. Past the curve's minimum, more cores actively hurts by inflating communication overhead.
Problem 2 · medium
A three-stage ablation reports latency reductions of 40%, 10%, and 20% relative to what remains at each stage. What is the overall speedup — and why isn't it simply 40+10+20 = 70%?
Show answer
Multiply the remaining fractions: (1−0.40)×(1−0.10)×(1−0.20) = 0.60×0.90×0.80 = 0.432. Overall speedup = 1/0.432 ≈ 2.31× (a 56.8% total reduction, not 70%). Each percentage removes a share of what's currently left, not a fixed share of the original baseline.Misconception: that sequential percentage reductions add up. They compound multiplicatively — exactly as worked out for Wavel's own Table 3 ablation.
Problem 3 · medium
Operator A's output MeshTensor occupies a 60×60 region; its logical consumer B expects a 40×90 input region at a different location. A's output correctly satisfies the data dependency. Is the schedule executable as-is?
Show answer
No. Correct logical dependence doesn't guarantee physical executability — the regions' sizes and locations differ, so Wavel must insert an explicit alignment MeshKernel: Resize A's output layout to 40×90, then Move it to B's region. Only after that alignment kernel is inserted is the schedule actually executable.Misconception: that a valid dataflow graph is automatically a valid physical schedule. On a mesh, layout and location are separate constraints from data dependency.
Problem 4 · hard
Why does CUT require the two resulting sub-regions to share a full boundary edge, rather than allowing any partition that conserves total area?
Show answer
Adjacency is what keeps producer–consumer communication across the new boundary short-range (one hop) instead of crossing arbitrary distance on the mesh. If CUT allowed disconnected or non-adjacent sub-regions, the resulting schedule could still be area-correct but would reintroduce exactly the long-hop communication cost Wavel's locality-preserving refinements are designed to avoid.Misconception: that any area-conserving split is equally good. On a mesh, where the split falls matters as much as how much area each side gets.
Problem 5 · medium
Wavel memoizes the best schedule found for a set of operators in a region of a given size, keyed by shape rather than absolute (x, y). Why is this safe on a wafer mesh, and when would it stop being safe?
Show answer
It's safe because the fabric is (assumed) uniform: a 200×200 region behaves the same wherever it sits on the mesh, so the optimal internal schedule depends only on its dimensions. It would stop being safe on a mesh with faulty tiles, non-uniform routing near the edges, or heterogeneous tile types — cases the paper explicitly flags as needing fault-map-aware placement in future work.Misconception: that position never matters. It doesn't for cost within a uniform region — but it can matter the moment the fabric itself isn't uniform.
Problem 6 · hard
Wavel is slower than TTNN's hand-tuned path on LLaMA3.1-8B (46.2 ms vs 35.3 ms) but 6.9× faster than TTNN on Qwen3.5-4B. What single fact about these two cases explains the reversal?
Show answer
Whether a hand-tuned, model-specific fused-kernel library already exists. TTNN's trace-replay path for LLaMA uses hand-fused compound operators that cut dispatch overhead below what Wavel's current automatic backend generates. Qwen3.5-4B has no such hand-tuned library, so TTNN falls back to a slower generic path, and Wavel's automatic scheduling wins decisively.Misconception: that an automatic compiler should always beat a hand path, or always lose to one. The comparison is model-dependent on whether hand engineering exists for that specific model.
Problem 7 · easy
Wavel's gains over WaferLLM are larger for decode than for prefill. Why?
Show answer
Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, so where each operator's region sits and how it overlaps with neighbors has a large effect on latency — exactly what CUT/TUNE optimize. Prefill (especially at large batch) is compute-bound and tensors often need nearly the full chip just to fit on-chip, leaving little room left for schedule search to improve on.Misconception: that a scheduling improvement should help every phase equally. Its impact depends on whether that phase is bound by placement-sensitive communication or by raw compute/capacity.
Problem 8 · medium
The paper reports "up to 2.97× speedup on MoE workloads" using a calibrated cost model rather than hardware measurement. What would you need to see before trusting that number as much as the hardware-measured 1.31×–1.35× dense result?
Show answer
Evidence that the cost model's predictions track real WSE-3 hardware closely across the relevant operator types and region sizes — which the paper does provide (Figure 13: 95–99% prediction accuracy for GEMM, GEMV, Move, and Resize). That closes most, but not all, of the gap: it's still a model extrapolation for a workload Wavel doesn't yet fully code-generate end-to-end (MoE router/dispatch logic remains a manual-tuning edge case per §8).Misconception: that a calibrated model result is interchangeable with a hardware measurement. It's a validated prediction, not a replacement for actually running the workload.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Twelve questions newcomers ask most.

Q1What's the one-sentence difference between a shard plan and an executable schedule?▾
A shard plan says how work is divided among devices; an executable schedule additionally fixes exactly where each piece sits physically, how mismatched layouts are aligned, and in what order operators may legally run relative to each other.
Q2Why can't GPU compilers like Roller, Welder, or Ladder just be pointed at a wafer-scale chip?▾
They optimize within a sharding-oriented view that assumes a shared-memory system absorbs physical placement differences. A wafer has no shared memory — communication cost is hop-distance-dependent — so the physical details those compilers leave implicit are exactly the details that dominate wafer-scale performance.
Q3In one line each, what do CUT and TUNE actually do?▾
CUT splits a region shared by two operators into two adjacent sub-regions along one axis. TUNE resizes a single operator's region around a fixed anchor corner, without changing the schedule's topology.
Q4How does Wavel judge a candidate schedule without actually running it?▾
Because every MeshKernel in a MeshSchedule is bound to a concrete Region, its cost can be estimated directly from region size, memory usage, and hop distances using a trained per-backend performance model (95–99% accurate against hardware for the tested operators), and per-operator costs compose into a whole-graph estimate.
Q5Does Wavel guarantee it finds the globally optimal schedule?▾
No. CUT only produces axis-aligned adjacent splits and TUNE only resizes around a fixed corner, so irregular or corner-relocating layouts aren't explored; unimodality is empirical rather than proven, so ternary search can settle on a local optimum; and bounded A* stops at a fixed iteration budget. The paper is explicit that it claims no global optimality guarantee.
Q6Why does hand-tuned TTNN beat Wavel on LLaMA3.1-8B?▾
TTNN's trace-replay path uses hand-fused compound operators that reduce dispatch overhead beyond what Wavel's current automatic backend generates for that specific, well-supported model. Wavel still wins decisively (6.9×) on Qwen3.5-4B, which has no such hand-tuned library.
Q7Is the 2.97× MoE number measured on real WSE-3 hardware?▾
No — it's a calibrated cost-model prediction, not a hardware measurement. The authors are explicit about this distinction: MoE router/gating and dynamic token dispatch aren't yet fully code-generated end to end, so this result reports automatic schedule generation plus calibrated prediction rather than measured executable code.
Q8How does MeshIR handle MoE's non-rectangular expert layouts if Regions must be rectangular?▾
A MeshKernel's region_list can compose multiple rectangular sub-regions to represent a T-shaped or L-shaped occupied area, which some expert-routing reduce operations require — the rectangle stays the atomic unit, but a kernel's footprint can be built from several of them.
Q9What is "translation-invariant reuse" and why does it matter for search speed?▾
The best internal schedule for a set of operators in a region depends only on the region's dimensions, not its absolute position on the mesh (a property of the uniform fabric). Wavel memoizes solved subproblems by shape, so structurally identical subproblems at different locations are solved once and reused everywhere — this is one of the key levers behind the ~49–2,951 orders-of-magnitude search-space reductions in Table 4.
Q10Does any of this generalize beyond wafer-scale chips?▾
Yes — the same MeshSchedule/CUT/TUNE design ports to Tenstorrent's much smaller Grayskull, Wormhole, and Blackhole mesh accelerators. On Blackhole, Wavel compiles 28×–70× faster than the vendor pipeline and is up to 6.9× faster at execution on a model lacking a hand-tuned kernel library.
Q11What's explicitly left for future work?▾
Fault-map-aware placement and routing, adaptive/online rescheduling as workload shapes (batch size, sequence length, active experts) change at runtime, more mature MoE code generation for router/dispatch logic, richer kernel fusion to close the gap with vendor hand-tuned libraries, and extension of Region/CUT/TUNE to higher-dimensional mesh topologies.
Q12How does this relate to OS-level memory tiering (DRAM/CXL/NVM page placement)?▾
Structurally similar problem, different regime and mechanism. Both are "place the right thing near where it will be used, given a cost that grows with distance" — OS tiering (TPP, HeMem, Memtis, Pond) tracks page hotness at runtime and migrates reactively across a handful of latency tiers; Wavel decides placement statically at compile time, using an explicit hop-distance cost model over up to ~1000 graduated locality levels on one chip, with no runtime migration once compiled. Wavel trades the adaptivity of runtime tiering for the precision of a fully known, offline-computed cost landscape.

Core terms
WavelThe paper's compilation system: search directly over executable schedules for wafer-scale accelerators.
Executable scheduleA MeshSchedule that already fixes placement, layout alignment, and execution ordering — Wavel's search object.
Sharding-oriented viewThe prevailing compiler assumption (GPU/TPU/distributed compilers) that finding a logical shard plan is the main job; physical realization is left to later lowering.
MeshIRWavel's IR: Region, MeshTensor, MeshKernel, MeshSchedule — the building blocks that make an executable schedule explicit.
RegionA rectangular block of cores R = ((x,y),(w,h)); the atomic unit of physical allocation.
CUTSplits a region shared by two operators into two adjacent sub-regions along one axis; inserts alignment kernels at the new boundary.
TUNEResizes one operator's region around a fixed anchor corner chosen to minimize distance to its producer.
Logical–physical locality synergyThe paper's term for structured, localized workloads (LLMs, HPC, vector search) whose logical dependencies already tend to align with physical mesh locality.
Supporting terms
Full-Chip baselineA no-search baseline that assigns every operator to the entire wafer, treating it as a single shared device.
WaferLLMState-of-the-art hand-optimized WSE inference system; partitions no finer than a whole transformer layer.
Unimodal cost structureA per-operator property: cost vs. region size has a single minimum (compute-bound below it, communication-bound above), enabling ternary search.
Translation invarianceThe property that a solved schedule for a region depends only on its dimensions, not its absolute mesh location — the basis of Wavel's memoization.
Bounded A*A*-with-heuristics search over the CUT/TUNE state space, extended with a fixed iteration budget and shortcut moves (DUPLICATE, PLACE).
MLIR boundaryThe point in the compilation stack where device-agnostic graph optimization (MLIR) hands off to Wavel's spatial-scheduling layer.
CSL / TTNN backendsWavel's two lowering targets: statically-routed Cerebras kernels vs. dispatch-oriented Tenstorrent reader/writer kernels — same MeshSchedule contract, different code generation.
Calibrated cost modelA trained per-backend performance model (95–99% accuracy on GEMM/GEMV/Move/Resize) used to predict results (e.g., MoE speedups) not yet measured on hardware.
