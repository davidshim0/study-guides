Cold at the top means cold all the way down

Tiered memory only pays off if the OS can tell which data is hot and which is cold — and every existing way of finding out breaks once an application's footprint reaches a terabyte. Scanning every page is too slow; sampling a few pages per region misses the hot ones; hardware counters drown in interrupts. This is the plain-English tour of Telescope, which finds hot and cold data by reading a bit the hardware was already setting — at every level of the page table, not just the bottom.

📄 "Telescope: Telemetry for Gargantuan Memory Footprint Applications" · Nair, Kumar, Prasad, Huang, Rudoff, Subramoney (Intel Labs / Intel Corp.) 🎓 A page-table primer is included — no prior tiered-memory reading required ⏱ ~30 min read
🔭

Don't scan every page to see if it's hot — check one bit near the root of the page table, and if it's clear, skip the whole subtree beneath it.

1Tiered memory: near (fast, small) vs. far (slow, huge)▾

A tiered-memory system pairs a small, fast near tier (DRAM) with a much larger, slower far tier (CXL-attached memory, NVM, or — in this paper's evaluation — Intel Optane DC Persistent Memory used as plain volatile RAM). The whole point is to keep frequently-used ("hot") data in the near tier and rarely-used ("cold") data in the far tier.

Now you're ready: that placement decision is only as good as the information it's based on — which is exactly the paper's subject: how do you find out what's hot without it costing more than it saves?
2The 4-level x86-64 page table▾

A virtual address is translated to a physical one by walking a 4-level radix tree: PGD (page global directory, root) → PUD (page upper directory) → PMD (page middle directory) → PTE (page table entry, the leaf pointing at an actual 4 KiB data page). Each level has 512 entries; each level of the virtual address supplies 9 bits used as an index into that level.

Now you're ready: a PGD entry effectively "covers" 512 GB of virtual address space; a PUD entry covers 1 GB; a PMD entry covers 2 MB — this is the granularity ladder Telescope climbs up and down.
3The ACCESSED bit, at every level▾

When a TLB miss forces the hardware to walk the page table, it sets an ACCESSED bit not just in the final PTE, but in the PGD, PUD, and PMD entries it passed through on the way there too. This is a normal, always-on part of the hardware page walk — no special mode required.

Now you're ready: this is the entire insight the paper is built on — a hot page implies hot entries at every level of its path, and a cold higher-level entry implies everything beneath it is cold.
4Linear scanning (kstaled, idle page tracking)▾

The simplest telemetry approach: periodically walk the entire virtual address space, checking (and resetting) the ACCESSED bit at the leaf level for every single page. It's exact — no false positives or negatives — but the scan time grows directly with the number of pages, which is fatal once footprints reach terabytes.

Now you're ready: this is "Problem 1" in the paper's taxonomy (Table 1) — precise but doesn't scale.
5Region-based sampling (DAMON)▾

DAMON (Data Access Monitor), already in the mainline Linux kernel, divides an application's address space into regions and randomly samples one or a few pages per region to estimate that whole region's hotness, splitting hot regions finer and merging cold ones over time.

Now you're ready: sampling trades precision for speed — and the paper shows that trade collapses entirely once regions are large enough that a random sample is very unlikely to land on the (relatively few) hot pages inside.
6Hardware counters / PEBS▾

Intel PEBS (Processor Event-Based Sampling) and similar PMU (performance monitoring unit) mechanisms sample hardware events — retired loads/stores, TLB misses, cache misses — and record the virtual address that caused each sampled event. Higher sampling rates catch more hot pages but generate more OS interrupts.

Now you're ready: Linux automatically throttles interrupt-heavy sampling, which is exactly what causes PEBS-based approaches to silently under-detect hot data at scale (Worked Example 1).
7Precision and recall, as used here▾

Precision is the fraction of pages a technique labeled "hot" that are actually hot (no false positives). Recall is the fraction of actually-hot pages the technique successfully found (no false negatives). A technique can have perfect precision while missing almost all the hot data — which is exactly PMU's failure mode at scale.

Now you're ready: these two numbers, measured against known "ground truth" access patterns in synthetic microbenchmarks, are the paper's main way of comparing techniques (Worked Examples 1–2).
8MGLRU: Linux's newer generational reclaim▾

MGLRU (Multi-Generational LRU) replaces the classic active/inactive page-reclaim split with several numbered "generations." Each scan checks the leaf-level ACCESSED bit for every page to decide whether to promote it to a newer generation — an exhaustive, linear-scanning process at heart.

Now you're ready: Telescope's insight applies here too — MGLRU-TS (How It Works, step 8) skips scanning entire subtrees whose higher-level ACCESSED bit is already clear.

The whole story in one place

Telescope in a nutshell

The problem, the mechanism that fixes it, and how it all connects.

The paper's framing is blunt: "memory tiering is only as good as the telemetry." Every existing hot/cold detection technique falls into one of three buckets, and all three degrade sharply as memory footprint scales into the terabytes. Linear scanning is exact but its scan time grows with the number of pages — orders of seconds at terabyte scale. Region-based sampling (DAMON) has a sampling probability of finding the actual hot pages that shrinks as regions get larger, and at 100 GB its precision and recall both collapse to zero. Hardware counters (PEBS) have no false positives but suffer high false negatives at scale, since higher sampling rates needed to catch rare hot pages generate so many interrupts that Linux automatically throttles them.

Telescope's insight: the hardware already sets the ACCESSED bit at every level of the page-table tree during a page walk, not just the leaf. A hot data page therefore has hot PTE, PMD, PUD, and PGD entries along its path — and conversely, a cold higher-level entry means the entire multi-gigabyte (or multi-hundred-gigabyte) subtree beneath it is cold, full stop, with no need to check a single page inside it. Telescope profiles top-down: start by checking coarse, high-level entries; only descend into a subtree if its bit is set; converge naturally onto the specific hot 4 KiB pages while instantly ruling out everything else. Two variants — bounded (never overshoots a region's boundary) and flex (allows a small, tunable overshoot for better coverage) — trade convergence speed against coverage accuracy. The same insight, applied to Linux's MGLRU scanner, becomes MGLRU-TS: skip scanning any subtree whose higher-level bit is already clear.

The payoff, as measured: 90%+ precision and recall at 5 TB, where DAMON's precision and recall are near 0% and PEBS's recall is under 10%; under 0.9% single-CPU utilization for the telemetry itself; 5.6% to 34.4% real-application throughput improvement across Memcached and Redis at 1–2 TB footprints; and, applied to MGLRU, a 12.38% faster scan with 8% higher Memcached throughput.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • All three existing telemetry approaches — linear scanning, region-based sampling, hardware counters — degrade sharply as footprint scales to terabytes, for three different underlying reasons (Table 1).
  • Telescope's insight: the hardware-set ACCESSED bit exists at every page-table level, not just the leaf — a cold higher-level entry proves an entire subtree beneath it is cold, with zero pages checked.
  • Two variants trade convergence speed against region-boundary accuracy: bounded (exact, may need multiple lower-level entries for odd-shaped regions) and flex (allows a tunable overshoot for better single-entry coverage).
  • The same top-down-skip insight applied to Linux's MGLRU scanner (MGLRU-TS) speeds up reclaim scanning itself, not just tiering telemetry.
  • Result: 90%+ precision/recall at 5 TB (vs. ~0% for DAMON), under 0.9% CPU overhead, up to 34.4% real-app throughput gain, and 12.38% faster MGLRU scans — accuracy and efficiency together, not a trade-off between them.
Six everyday pictures

What Telescope feels like

Every analogy below is useful for one part of the intuition and wrong for another — the "where it breaks" line is as important as the picture itself.

A building's master breaker panel

Check the whole floor's switch first

If a building's third-floor master breaker is off, you already know every room on that floor is dark — no need to walk the hallway checking each door. Telescope's PGD-level check works the same way: one bit rules out everything beneath it at once.

Where it breaks: a real breaker panel has no equivalent of "flex" — it's a strict on/off per floor, with no tunable overshoot the way Telescope's flex variant allows for an oddly-shaped region.
A library's "nothing checked out this month" sign

Skip the whole floor, not shelf by shelf

A library that tracks "was anything on this floor checked out this month?" as one aggregate fact can skip inspecting every individual shelf on a quiet floor. Region-based sampling, by contrast, is like spot-checking three random books per floor and guessing from there — much easier to miss the one popular book.

Where it breaks: a library's floor-level fact doesn't nest recursively the way page-table levels do (floor → shelf → book → page) — Telescope's real advantage is that the same trick applies at every level down.
Zooming a map app before zooming in

Country, then state, then street

You don't scan every street in a country to find one address — you zoom from country to state to city to street, only descending where the thing you're looking for actually is. Telescope's PGD→PUD→PMD→PTE descent is exactly this kind of progressive zoom.

Where it breaks: a map app's zoom levels are fixed visual convenience; Telescope's levels are dictated by real hardware structure (the page table itself), and skipping a level isn't optional the way jumping straight to street view can be.
A property line vs. a lenient easement

Bounded stays strictly inside the fence

A "bounded" survey never claims land beyond your actual lot line, even if that means describing your lot with several small parcels instead of one clean rectangle. A "flex" survey allows a little rounding beyond the fence if it makes the paperwork simpler — at the cost of a small, bounded inaccuracy.

Where it breaks: a real easement is a one-time legal allowance; Telescope's flex threshold (15% at PUD, 25% at PMD) is re-applied fresh every profiling window, not a permanent grant.
A warehouse motion-sensor audit

Skip the whole aisle if its sensor never fired

An inventory audit that checks one motion sensor per aisle can skip walking an entire quiet aisle end-to-end. MGLRU-TS does exactly this for reclaim scanning — a clear PGD-level bit for a subtree means skipping potentially hundreds of thousands of leaf-level checks underneath it.

Where it breaks: a warehouse aisle is a flat list; the page table is a nested tree, so MGLRU-TS's savings compound at every level it can skip, not just once.
A metal detector at the gate, not a full pat-down

Cheap enough to run on everyone, all the time

PMU-based sampling is like pulling aside 1-in-1000 people for a thorough (accurate) search — precise about who it catches, but bound to miss most people carrying something. Reading an always-updated hardware bit is like a metal detector every single person walks through — cheap enough to apply universally, so nothing structurally gets missed.

Where it breaks: the ACCESSED bit still only tells you "was this touched," not how many times or how recently within the window — Telescope's region hotness score (How It Works, step 3) needs repeated profiling windows to build that up.
Mechanism, step by step

From a region to a converged hot page

Ten steps, from the underlying hardware fact to the two tunable profiling variants and the MGLRU extension.

  1. The hardware fact this all rests on. During a TLB-miss page walk on x86-64, the hardware sets the ACCESSED bit not only in the final PTE but in every PGD, PUD, and PMD entry it traverses to get there. This happens on every ordinary memory access — no special profiling mode needed.
  2. What that implies, formally. If a data page was accessed, its entire path of table entries (PTE, PMD, PUD, PGD) has ACCESSED set. Conversely, if a PGD entry's ACCESSED bit is clear, none of the up-to-512 GB of address space beneath it has been accessed since the bit was last reset — the entire subtree is provably cold.
  3. Region management, borrowed from DAMON. Telescope divides the application's virtual address space into regions, each assigned a hotness score from an access count measured over a profiling window. Hot regions are split into smaller sub-regions for finer tracking; cold regions with similar scores are merged to reduce bookkeeping.
  4. Region profiling: pick one page-table level. For each region, in each profiling window, Telescope identifies a single page-table entry level (somewhere between PTE and PGD) to track. It resets that entry's ACCESSED bit at the window's start and checks it at the end — if set, one or more data pages under that entry were touched during the window.
  5. The bounded variant. Bounded picks the highest page-table level whose covered address range fits entirely within the region's own bounds — never spanning outside it. This maximizes convergence speed (a single bit can stand in for a huge range) but can force a fallback to several entries at a lower level when a region's size doesn't cleanly align with any single higher-level entry's range.
  6. The flex variant. Flex allows the picked entry to extend slightly beyond the region's bounds, within a per-level error threshold — 15% at PUD, 25% at PMD in the paper's tuning. This improves single-entry coverage for oddly-sized regions at a small, bounded accuracy cost; when even the threshold can't be satisfied, flex falls back to the same multi-entry approach bounded uses.
  7. Convergence in practice. Profiling proceeds top-down: check the identified PGD-level entries first; for any with ACCESSED set, profile that subtree's PUD entries next window; continue down through PMD to PTE. Every level with a clear bit ends that branch of the search immediately — cold regions are identified and left alone without ever touching a leaf-level bit.
  8. The same trick applied to scanning: MGLRU-TS.
    💡
    Stock MGLRU scans the leaf (PTE) level exhaustively to decide which pages to promote to a newer LRU generation. MGLRU-TS first checks the PGD-level ACCESSED bit for each subtree; if clear, it skips the entire subtree — potentially avoiding checking up to 262,144 PTE-level bits the paper's own worked example uses to illustrate the savings from skipping just one PUD-level subtree.
  9. Migration policy on top (not the paper's main contribution, but how it's used). Regions with an access count above a threshold (5, in a 120-second window) in the evaluation are considered hot; very large hot regions (≥4 GB) are left for later splitting into finer sub-regions rather than migrated whole; migration proceeds in descending hotness order until a per-step DRAM budget (10 GB in the paper) is reached.
  10. Portability. The mechanism only assumes a radix-tree page table with per-level access bits set during a hardware walk — true of x86-64, ARM, RISC-V, and POWER architectures alike, so nothing about the core idea is Intel- or x86-specific, even though the implementation and evaluation are done in Linux on x86-64.
Real numbers from the paper

Seven ways to see the difference

Every number below is from the paper's own evaluation (4-socket Intel Xeon Gold 6238M, 176 cores, 768 GB DRAM near tier + 6 TB Optane DC PMM far tier, Fedora 30 / Linux 5.18.19).

1 · Precision and recall at 5 TB (multi-phase microbenchmark)

Across all three phases of a 5 TB synthetic workload (MASIM) that relocates its hot region twice mid-run, DAMON's precision and recall are mostly 0 — at terabyte scale, the probability a random sample lands on the actual hot data is simply too low. PMU's precision stays near 1.0 (few false positives) but its recall is under 0.1 — it structurally can't generate enough samples to cover a large hot region. Both variants of Telescope stay above 0.9 precision and recall through every phase, including the moments right after the hot region relocates.

2 · Where DAMON and PMU actually start failing

At a 1 GB footprint with a 10% hot region, DAMON and PMU both achieve 90%+ precision and recall. At 10 GB, DAMON's recall already drops sharply. At 100 GB, DAMON's precision and recall both fall to zero — the whole region gets marked cold because sampling never lands on the hot pages. PMU's precision stays high, but its hot-data coverage keeps shrinking as the total number of hot pages grows. Both Telescope variants keep high precision and recall across all three sizes.

3 · CPU overhead: cycles and utilization

For the SubTB-10GB microbenchmark, DAMON-AGG (aggressive sampling) consumes 68.22 billion CPU cycles and 14.7% of a single core, while Telescope-BND uses only 3.20 billion cycles at 0.8% utilization — roughly a 20× reduction in cycles for a technique that's simultaneously far more accurate. Across every microbenchmark configuration, both Telescope variants stay under 0.9% single-core utilization; PMU is excluded from this comparison since its sampling is handled by hardware rather than a separate kernel thread.

4 · Real-application throughput: up to 34.4%

Running Memcached and Redis with YCSB and memtier load generators at 1–2 TB memory footprints on the full tiered system: Telescope-driven migration improved throughput by 5.6% to 34.4% over an untiered baseline. DAMON-driven migration could not identify a single hot data page at this scale, so its throughput improvement was essentially nil. PMU-driven migration found only "a few gigabytes" of the actual hot data set, producing marginal gains in some cases and none in others.

💡
5 · Not just throughput — tail latency too. For Memcached with YCSB, 95th-percentile latency was 867 ms (Telescope-BND) and 824 ms (Telescope-FLX) versus 881 ms (DAMON-MOD) and 976 ms (PMU-AGG). Telescope wins on both throughput and tail latency simultaneously — better telemetry means fewer far-memory accesses on the critical path, not a trade-off between speed and accuracy.

6 · MGLRU-TS: faster scans, real throughput gain

Applying Telescope's insight to Linux's MGLRU scanner on a 2-socket, 72-core, 1 TB DRAM system running Memcached with memtier (≈976 GB populated): a single scan took 12.77 s for MGLRU-TS versus 14.57 s for stock MGLRU — a 12.38% improvement — while consuming 15.5% fewer CPU cycles (46.85B vs. 53.15B over 100 iterations) and improving Memcached throughput by 8%.

7 · The honest limitation. For the real-world application benchmarks, there's no independently-known "ground truth" for which pages are actually hot — unlike the synthetic microbenchmarks, precision and recall can't be directly computed. The paper's own workaround is to visualize telemetry output as heatmaps and rely on measured throughput/latency as an indirect proxy for correctness, which is a real limit on how rigorously the real-app results can be verified.

Five questions, misconceptions flagged

Try answering before revealing — each answer names a specific misconception it's checking for.

Medium
Q1. Does Telescope eliminate the need to ever check the leaf-level (PTE) ACCESSED bit?
Show answer
No. It still checks PTE-level bits to find the actual hot 4 KiB pages — it just avoids doing so across the entire address space by ruling out cold subtrees at higher levels first. The savings come from skipping, not from never looking at all.Misconception checked: assuming "page-table profiling" means never touching the leaf level.
Medium
Q2. Is Telescope itself a page-migration policy?
Show answer
No — Telescope is a telemetry technique: it identifies which regions are hot or cold. The migration policy built on top (hotness threshold, skipping very large regions, a per-step DRAM budget) is a separate, comparatively simple layer, largely reusing ideas from prior tiered-memory work rather than being the paper's contribution.Misconception checked: conflating "detects hot data" with "decides what to do about it."
Hard
Q3. Does the bounded variant always cover a region with exactly one page-table entry?
Show answer
No — if a region's size and alignment don't match any single page-table level's coverage exactly, bounded falls back to using multiple entries at a finer level to stay strictly within the region's bounds. That's precisely the scenario the flex variant is designed to handle more efficiently, at the cost of a small, thresholded overshoot.Misconception checked: assuming "bounded" implies "always one entry, always fast."
Hard
Q4. Is Telescope's advantage purely about lower CPU overhead, with DAMON/PMU still being "correct, just slower"?
Show answer
No — the paper shows DAMON and PMU aren't just slower, they're actually wrong at terabyte scale: DAMON's precision and recall collapse to near 0% at 100 GB, and PMU's recall drops under 10% at 5 TB. Telescope is simultaneously cheaper (under 0.9% CPU) and more accurate (90%+ precision/recall) — not a speed/accuracy trade-off.Misconception checked: treating all telemetry techniques as differing only in overhead, not correctness.
Easy
Q5. Does MGLRU-TS still eventually check every PTE-level bit for a hot subtree?
Show answer
Yes, for subtrees that are actually hot — the savings come entirely from skipping cold subtrees. The paper's own example: checking one PGD-level bit and finding it clear substitutes for what would otherwise be up to 262,144 individual PTE-level ACCESSED-bit checks underneath that one entry.Misconception checked: assuming the speedup means hot data is scanned less thoroughly too.
Common questions

FAQ

Q·Where does the name "Telescope" come from?▾
The paper doesn't spell out the etymology explicitly, but it fits the mechanism well: profiling starts with a wide, coarse field of view (whole page-table subtrees covering hundreds of gigabytes) and progressively narrows — converges — down to individual hot 4 KiB pages, much like zooming a telescope from a wide sky view to one star.
Q·Is this specific to Intel hardware or Optane?▾
No — the evaluation happens to use Intel Optane DC Persistent Memory as the far-memory tier and an Intel Xeon platform, but the mechanism only assumes a radix-tree page table with per-level access bits set during a hardware walk. The paper explicitly notes this holds for x86-64, ARM, RISC-V, and POWER architectures.
Q·How is this different from DAMON, which is already in mainline Linux?▾
DAMON checks the ACCESSED bit only at the leaf (PTE) level and estimates a region's hotness by randomly sampling a few pages inside it. Telescope checks the ACCESSED bit at every page-table level and uses it to deterministically rule out entire cold subtrees, rather than statistically guessing from a small sample.
Q·Does Telescope need new hardware support?▾
No — it relies entirely on the ACCESSED bit that radix-page-table architectures already set during an ordinary hardware page walk. No new PMU events, no new instructions, no firmware changes.
Q·What's the honest catch?▾
Two things the paper is upfront about. First, the bounded/flex error thresholds (15% at PUD, 25% at PMD) were tuned for the paper's own evaluation workloads — a workload with a pathological address-space layout could still force many small-level fallback entries. Second, like DAMON, real-world application benchmarks have no independently-known "ground truth," so precision/recall can't be directly measured there — the paper relies on heatmaps and throughput/latency as an indirect proxy instead (Worked Example 7).
Q·Does this replace DAMON in mainline Linux?▾
Not as shipped. Telescope is implemented in Linux and evaluated head-to-head against DAMON and PEBS-based approaches, but it's a research prototype from the paper, not a merged replacement for DAMON in the mainline kernel.
