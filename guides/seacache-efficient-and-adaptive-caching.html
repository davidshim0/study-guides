MICRO 2025 · A Field Guide

Give the cache to whatever shape the fiber is — not the shape the block demands

Sparse accelerators cache rows and columns of ragged length in fixed-size blocks, and evict them with a policy that needs future knowledge no general cache can have. SeaCache gets both right, cheaply.

📄 "SeaCache" · Li, Jiang, Gao · Tsinghua 🎓 Written for readers who already know caches, tiering, and CXL ⏱ ~25 min read
🧵

PACK & SPLIT fibers to fill every block exactly — then replace by counting future reuse, not by chasing pointer chains.

Before you begin

What's domain-specific here

You already know sets, ways, LRU, and why caches beat scratchpads for irregular access — the primers below skip that and go straight to what's specific to sparse tensor caching.

Quick glossary
FiberA row or column of a sparse tensor in compressed form (CSR/CSC) — the natural unit of reuse, indexed by ID, not address.
SpMSpMSparse-sparse matrix multiplication, 𝐶=𝐴×𝐵 — the paper's main workload.
Gust / IP / OPGustavson's, Inner-Product, Outer-Product — the three loop orders (dataflows) for SpMSpM; SeaCache targets Gust by default.
Belady's OptimalEvict the block used farthest in the future — provably best, usually impossible because the future is unknown.
gLRU / gLFUGuided LRU / guided LFU — replacement guided by tensor 𝐴's structure, which reveals 𝐵's future reuse pattern.
Virtual tagA cache-set slot with a counter but no data block — extra room to track future-reuse info for fibers not yet cached.
P&SFiber packing & splitting — SeaCache's mapping fix for variable fiber lengths vs. fixed block size.
Meta-tagThe tag field storing a fiber's ID directly (no physical address translation), as in X-Cache.
1Why fiber IDs replace physical addresses▾

In CSR/CSC, finding an element's physical address requires reading row-pointer metadata first. X-Cache skips that: it indexes the cache directly by fiber ID (row/column number), so a fiber that's cached needs zero metadata lookups — about 40% lower load-to-use latency in SpMSpM.

Now you're ready: SeaCache's tag array literally stores fiber IDs (split as Tag-H/Tag-L), not addresses — everything in Section 4.1 builds on this.
2Why Belady's Optimal is achievable here at all▾

In Gust, which rows of 𝐵 get reused next is fully determined by the non-zero pattern of 𝐴 — walking column 𝑘 of 𝐴 tells you exactly when 𝐵𝑘 is needed again. That's "free" future knowledge general-purpose caches never get, and it's what lets a guided policy realize the true optimal.

Now you're ready: "guided" always means "guided by the other operand's structural metadata" — not a heuristic, but literal future information.
3Why realizing that optimal is expensive (gLRU's actual cost)▾

Two ways to get gLRU: (1) precompute 𝐴's transpose offline and read it as-is — but transposing a sparse matrix costs up to 126% of the actual computation, and doubles online accesses to 𝐴; or (2) build the transpose on the fly with per-column linked lists of ~20-bit pointers, sized linearly in 𝐴's dimension 𝐾 (not its non-zero count) — potentially hundreds of kB to a few MB.

Now you're ready: gLRU's cost isn't the idea, it's the pointer-chain bookkeeping — that's exactly what SeaCache's gLFU replaces.
4Why fixed cache blocks fight sparse fiber lengths▾

Real matrices (SuiteSparse) show fiber lengths varying wildly — both across matrices and within one matrix. A block sized for short fibers wastes almost nothing per fiber but overflows on long ones (X-Cache, 16 B); a block sized for long fibers (SpArch, 576 B) wastes most of its space on short fibers. No single fixed size wins everywhere.

Now you're ready: this — not the replacement policy — is design goal 1, and it's solved independently by fiber packing & splitting (Section 4.1).
5Why scratchpads struggle with sparse data specifically▾

A scratchpad's explicit, decoupled buffering is great when block sizes are predictable, but sparse fibers have irregular lengths that fragment scratchpad space, and — unlike a cache — a scratchpad has no cheap way to skip fetching a fiber it turns out never to need.

Now you're ready: SeaCache still beats an optimized scratchpad (Tailors) by 2.1× on average — caching's flexibility wins once its two weaknesses (mapping, replacement cost) are fixed.
The whole story in one place

SeaCache in a nutshell

Two problems, three fixes, and how they compose.

Sparse accelerators cache tensor fibers (rows/columns) by ID rather than address, which is fast but exposes two problems. First, fiber lengths vary drastically, so any single fixed cache block size either wastes space on short fibers or overflows on long ones. Second, the theoretically optimal replacement policy — guided LRU (gLRU) — is genuinely achievable in sparse tensor algebra (the structure of one operand predicts the other's future reuse), but implementing it needs either an expensive offline transpose or wide, dimension-sized pointer lists online.

SeaCache fixes both, independently. Fiber packing & splitting lets one fixed-size block hold several short fibers or one segment of a long one, so space utilization stays high regardless of length. Guided LFU (gLFU) swaps gLRU's wide recency pointers for narrow 4-bit reuse counters — nearly as accurate, far cheaper — and adds a few virtual tags per set (counter-only shadow slots) to track fibers not yet cached. Finally, because the counters need a prefetched window of the guiding tensor, a two-phase adaptive mechanism shares cache space between real data and this guide metadata, sized first from sparsity statistics, then tuned online with simulated annealing.

The payoff: integrated together, SeaCache beats state-of-the-art sparse caches (InnerSP, SpArch, X-Cache) by 2.8× on average, and an optimized scratchpad (Tailors) by 2.1× — for roughly 6% extra chip area and a 60.2% energy cut versus InnerSP.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • Fixed block size vs. variable fiber length is a mapping problem, separate from replacement — solved by packing short fibers together and splitting long ones across blocks.
  • Belady's Optimal is achievable in Gust-style SpMSpM (the other operand's structure is the future), but gLRU's pointer-chain implementation is what's expensive, not the idea itself.
  • gLFU replaces per-column linked lists (~20-bit pointers, ∝ dimension 𝐾) with per-column 4-bit counters (∝ 𝐾 too, but ~5× narrower) — and empirically performs as well or better.
  • Virtual tags (counter-only shadow ways) recover most of the accuracy lost from not storing every counter.
  • Guide metadata and real data share the same cache; the split ratio is picked offline from sparsity, then adapted online by simulated annealing.
  • Net result: 2.8× vs. SOTA caches, 2.1× vs. an optimized scratchpad, ~6% area, 60.2% less energy than InnerSP.
Six pictures, and where each snaps

How to think about SeaCache

Read in any order. Each ends with the point where the picture stops matching the hardware.

The mapping fix · Fiber packing & splitting

Packing a moving truck

Boxes come in wildly different sizes. Load them one-per-shelf and you waste most shelves on small boxes and can't fit the big ones at all. A good mover stacks several small boxes on one shelf and splits an oversized item across two — SeaCache does exactly this with fibers and 64-byte blocks (2 Cnt bits + 16 Extra bits of "packing list" per block, ~3.4% overhead).

Where it breaks: a mover can rearrange freely; SeaCache can only pack fibers with contiguous IDs sharing a set, and can only split a fiber into as many segments as the 16-bit Extra field can uniquely tag (~21,845 elements/fiber before limits kick in).
The replacement fix · gLRU → gLFU

A guest list vs. a headcount

To know who to seat last, you could keep a full ordered guest list per table (exact arrival order, gLRU's linked lists) — precise, but every list is as long as the invite pool. Or you could just keep a headcount per table of who's still expected (gLFU's 4-bit counters) — much smaller, and just as good for deciding who to bump.

Where it breaks: a headcount forgets order, only volume — that's literally why gLFU is no longer provably optimal like gLRU, even though it measures similarly well in practice (idealized miss rates of 15.9% vs. 16.5%).
Extra tracking · Virtual tags

A waitlist with no seats

A restaurant tracks not just seated parties but a waitlist — names and party sizes with no table assigned yet. That's a virtual tag: a slot in the cache set with a counter but no data block, tracking a fiber's expected future importance before it earns a real slot. Skipping this made miss rates 2.6× worse — most of what matters about the future concerns things not yet cached.

Where it breaks: a waitlist can be arbitrarily long; SeaCache only affords ~4 virtual tags per set — diminishing returns kick in fast (Fig. 5: 0→2→4→8→16 virtual tags), so more isn't free area for little extra accuracy.
Sharing space · Two-phase adaptive sizing

A shared kitchen counter

Prep space and plated food compete for the same counter. Too little prep space and you can't season the next dish (small prefetch → inaccurate future info); too much and there's nowhere to put finished plates (large prefetch → starves the data cache). SeaCache first eyeballs a starting split from how "busy" the recipe is (offline sparsity estimate), then nudges it while cooking based on what's actually going wrong (online simulated annealing).

Where it breaks: a cook adjusts continuously and intuitively; SeaCache only re-checks every 1/500 of total work and accepts worse splits early on purpose (high "temperature"), which is deliberate — annealing, not a thermostat.
Global search · Simulated annealing

Hiking downhill with occasional detours uphill

Figure 6's miss-rate-vs-prefetch-size curve has multiple local dips, so blindly walking to the nearest low point can strand you in a shallow one. Simulated annealing occasionally accepts an uphill (worse) move early on — cooling as it goes (𝑇(𝑘)=0.2×0.99^𝑘) — so it can escape shallow local minima and settle near the true best split, typically within 25 iterations and 95% of optimal.

Where it breaks: a hiker can backtrack any distance; SeaCache only nudges the prefetch size incrementally each round, so it explores the neighborhood of its current split, not the whole landscape at once.
Same footprint · No dedicated buffer

One drawer, two labels

Rather than build a second, separate SRAM structure to hold prefetched guide data (as an offline gLRU design effectively does by pinning it all on-chip), SeaCache partitions each bank's data array between actual 𝐵 fiber data and prefetched 𝐴 guide metadata, distinguished only by a bit of state — gLFU's reuse counters and virtual tags live separately, as extra bits alongside the tags.

Where it breaks: a labeled drawer never runs out mid-use; the shared cache genuinely trades one type of content for the other, which is exactly why getting the split ratio right (design goal 3) matters so much.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Section by section, with the paper's mechanisms rebuilt as diagrams.

1 · Fiber-ID mapping, and where it strains

X-Cache indexes the cache directly by fiber ID, skipping address translation. But a fixed block size can't track real fiber lengths: X-Cache's tiny 16 B blocks waste little on short fibers but truncate long ones; SpArch's 576 B blocks fit long fibers but leave short ones mostly empty. Neither adapts to a workload's actual (and internally varying) length distribution.

2 · Guided LFU — counting instead of pointing

Gust's dependency (which 𝐵𝑘 row is needed next depends on column 𝑘 of 𝐴) is exploited by prefetching a sliding window of 𝐴 elements into a guide buffer. gLRU reorganizes that window into per-column linked lists ordered by row ID — accurate, but each column needs head/tail pointers plus one next-pointer per buffered element, all ~20 bits wide and scaling with dimension 𝐾. gLFU instead keeps one small counter per column: incremented when an 𝐴 element enters the prefetch window, decremented when it's consumed. 4 bits per counter is enough — sparsity keeps counts low, and saturation on the rare high-count fiber is harmless since it's already unlikely to be evicted.

⚠
Without virtual tags, this degrades badly. A counter can only live where a fiber already has a cache slot — but the fibers that matter most for a future decision are often not yet cached. Skipping virtual tags entirely raised miss rates 2.6× over the idealized (infinite-metadata) gLFU. Adding just 4 virtual tags per 16-way set (160 extra bits/set) recovers most of that gap.

3 · Two-phase adaptive prefetch sizing

The guide buffer for 𝐴 shares space with the 𝐵 data cache, so its size is a real tradeoff: too small and many columns have no counter at all (zero-information); too large and it starves the data cache and exceeds what gLFU's counters (plus virtual tags) can hold, discarding useful increments.

// Offline: estimate from B's average non-zero row length
nzlB = nnz_B / (K × (J / T_J))
prefetch_size = max(1 / nzlB, 1/1024)      // floor: never below 0.1% of cache

// Online, every ~1/500 of total MACs:
if discard_rate > 20% and miss_rate < 20%: shrink prefetch window
elif discard_rate < 20% and miss_rate > 20%: grow prefetch window
else: perturb size; accept if P(k,ΔM) = exp(−ΔM / T(k)) > random()
// T(k) = 0.2 × 0.99^k — accepts worse moves early, converges within ~25 iterations
💡
Two independent signals, not one. The discard rate (dropped counter increments) says "too big"; the miss rate on real accesses lacking any counter says "too small." Neither alone would find the right size for both dense matrices (which want ~1/16 or less) and highly sparse ones (which want much more).

4 · Putting it together

A prefetch controller slides the guide window and runs the two-phase size selection; a fiber iterator per PE decodes packed/split blocks back into fibers; the cache itself carries the extra Cnt/Extra bits, per-block counters, virtual tags, and a second tag-array port. All three techniques are independent and orthogonal to the PE array — they apply equally to other dataflows (SpArch's condensed OP) and other tensor kernels (SpMM, MS-BFS), and compose with dynamic tiling schemes without change.

Follow the numbers

Worked examples

Four calculations straight from the paper's own figures — overhead, limits, and how the three techniques compose.

① Fiber packing/splitting overhead — 3.4%

Each 64-byte block gets 2 Cnt bits + 16 Extra bits, on top of a normal ~20-bit tag. What's the relative area cost?

  1. Baseline block cost = tag + data = 20 bits + 64 bytes × 8 bits = 20 + 512 = 532 bits.
  2. Added bits = 2 (Cnt) + 16 (Extra) = 18 bits.
  3. Overhead = 18 / (20 + 64×8) = 18 / 532 ≈ 3.4% — matching the paper's own figure exactly.
🍯
Insight: because block size can now grow (64 B, not X-Cache's tiny 16 B) without wasting space on short fibers, the 3.4% tax buys back both the packing gain (1.80×) and the ability to use fewer, larger banks.

② The segment limit for fiber splitting

The 16-bit Extra field disambiguates a split segment from a same-tag fiber by storing the low 16 bits of the original ID. How long can a fiber get before this aliases?

  1. Aliasing condition: two segments collide only if they differ by a multiple of 2¹⁶ = 65536 in ID, which (with 4-bit Tag-L shifts) requires ≥ 4096 blocks of separation.
  2. 4096 blocks × 64 bytes/block = 256 kB of fiber data before risk of collision.
  3. Each element = 64-bit value + 32-bit coordinate = 96 bits.
  4. Elements per fiber limit = 256×1024×8 bits / 96 bits ≈ 21,845 elements — comfortably above nearly every row/column in SuiteSparse, and moot anyway once tiling shortens fibers.
💡
This is a real but practically irrelevant limit: it only bites on single unsplit fibers longer than 21K non-zeros, which tiling naturally avoids by chopping long rows into shorter per-tile fibers.

③ Why virtual tags matter so much: the 2.6× gap

Figure 5 shows "gLFU w/ 0 vtag" with miss rates 2.6× worse than the idealized (unlimited-metadata) gLFU. Where does that gap come from?

  1. Idealized gLFU assumes every column's counter is tracked, cached or not — 15.9% average miss rate.
  2. With 0 virtual tags, a counter can only exist for a column whose fiber is already occupying a real cache way.
  3. But the columns that most need tracking are exactly those about to become important — i.e., not yet cached. Those counters simply vanish.
  4. Result: replacement decisions are made mostly blind to imminent future reuse, and miss rate jumps 2.6× — until 4 virtual tags per set recover most of the idealized accuracy at ~1.8% extra bits/set.
🍯
Insight: the "guided" part of gLFU is only as good as its ability to track fibers before they're cached — virtual tags are not an optimization, they're what makes gLFU's guidance real.

④ Composing the three techniques: 1.80× × 1.10× × 1.38×

The paper reports each technique's individual contribution on top of a "Base" design (fiber-ID mapping + plain LRU). Do they simply multiply to the reported 2.8× headline?

  1. Fiber packing & splitting alone: 1.80× over Base.
  2. + gLFU with virtual tags (adaptive size not yet applied): 1.10× further improvement over the P&S-only design.
  3. + Two-phase adaptive prefetch sizing: 1.38× further improvement, i.e. 1.12× over the best static size (1/16) alone.
  4. Compounding: 1.80 × 1.10 × 1.38 ≈ 2.73× over Base — in the same ballpark as, though not identical to, the headline "2.8× over SOTA cache designs," since the 2.8× figure compares against different (non-Base) prior designs (InnerSP/SpArch/X-Cache), not against SeaCache's own Base.
💡
Reading Figure 10 vs. Figure 7 carefully: the ablation (Fig. 10) isolates SeaCache's own techniques against its own baseline; the headline comparison (Fig. 7) is against three different prior accelerators. Both tell a consistent story, but they aren't the same denominator — worth checking whenever a paper reports both an ablation and a SOTA comparison.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it's checking for.

Problem 1 · easy
X-Cache uses tiny 16-byte blocks. You'd expect it to badly underperform InnerSP's 64-byte blocks on matrices with long fibers. The paper found the gap smaller than expected — why?
Show answer
Because neither fixed size actually handles long fibers well — a 64-byte block still overflows on a genuinely long fiber, just at a higher length threshold than 16 bytes. Both designs discard the tail of long fibers as misses; X-Cache just does it sooner. The real fix isn't "pick a bigger fixed size," it's removing the fixed-size constraint altogether via packing and splitting.Misconception: that block size alone determines how well long fibers are served. Any single fixed size eventually overflows — only variable mapping (P&S) actually solves it.
Problem 2 · medium
A 64-byte block normally costs 20 (tag) + 512 (data) = 532 bits. SeaCache adds 2 Cnt bits and 16 Extra bits. What's the percentage overhead, and does it change if you also add 4 virtual tags per 16-way set?
Show answer
P&S alone: 18/532 ≈ 3.4%. Adding 4 virtual tags costs ~160 bits per 16-way set (20 bits × 4 tag-only ways + 4-bit counters × 20 total ways); relative to a full set's ~8800 bits (16 × 550 bits with P&S already included), that's another ~1.8%. Combined, the paper reports the total as about 5.3% more bits — the two overheads compound rather than simply add, but the difference is small at this scale.Misconception: that stacking overheads always sum linearly. They compound multiplicatively (1.034 × 1.018 ≈ 1.053); it happens to look additive here only because both percentages are small.
Problem 3 · medium
Figure 5 shows idealized gLFU (15.9% miss rate) slightly beating idealized gLRU (16.5%), even though gLRU is the one that's "provably optimal." How is that possible?
Show answer
"Provably optimal" (Belady's) assumes unlimited knowledge of the future. Both idealized gLRU and idealized gLFU in this comparison are still limited by the same finite prefetch window of tensor 𝐴 — gLRU's reuse-distance ordering degrades in a way that a simple frequency count doesn't, when the true next-reuse event falls outside that finite window. So within a bounded lookahead, "recency of next use" and "count of future uses" aren't guaranteed to rank the same, and empirically counting edges out ordering here.Misconception: that gLRU's theoretical optimality guarantees it wins any head-to-head comparison. Optimality is conditioned on full future knowledge; with a finite prefetch window, that guarantee no longer applies.
Problem 4 · hard
Why does gLRU's online (non-transposed) implementation cost scale with the tensor dimension 𝐾, rather than with the number of non-zeros?
Show answer
Because gLRU needs a head and tail pointer for every column — even columns with zero prefetched non-zeros still need a (null) head/tail entry to check against — plus one next-pointer per buffered non-zero element. The head/tail structure is sized to 𝐾 (or the tiled 𝑇𝐾), not to the sparsity of the data; a very sparse but wide matrix (large 𝐾, few non-zeros) still pays for 𝐾 pointers it barely uses. gLFU's counters are also sized to 𝐾, but each is only 4 bits instead of ~20, so the fixed 𝐾-proportional cost shrinks roughly 5×.Misconception: that a "guided" policy's cost scales with how much data there is. It scales with how many possible categories (columns) exist to track, which is a structural property of the tensor shape, not its density.
Problem 5 · medium
Why does SpArch, which also uses guided replacement, perform poorly on matrices like ldoor and dielFilterV2real specifically?
Show answer
These are very large matrices whose guide metadata (head/tail + linked-list pointers, sized ∝ 𝐾) can exceed the entire cache capacity at the tile size that would otherwise give good data reuse. SpArch is forced to shrink the tile size to keep metadata under roughly half the cache, but smaller tiles mean worse data reuse and more redundant off-chip accesses — the "fix" for the metadata problem directly undermines the reuse the tiling was meant to provide.Misconception: that any amount of guide metadata is worth it if the policy is more accurate. Past a point, the metadata's own footprint starts starving the very data cache it's supposed to help.
Problem 6 · medium
Why would a single, globally fixed prefetch size (say, always 1/16 of the cache) not work well across all matrices?
Show answer
Dense-ish matrices have a high data-to-metadata ratio: a small prefetch window already lets most columns find a non-zero quickly, so a bigger window just steals cache space from real data (e.g. mouse_gene, nd24k want small sizes). Highly sparse matrices need to look much farther ahead before a column shows any non-zero at all, so a small fixed window leaves most counters at zero and uninformative (e.g. af_shell9-like matrices want larger sizes). One size trades one group's performance for the other's — which is exactly why the paper found 1/16 the best static average, but still 1.12× worse than adapting per matrix.Misconception: that "best on average" means "good for everyone." A single static choice can be measurably suboptimal for the specific matrix you actually run.
Problem 7 · hard
Why does the two-phase mechanism use simulated annealing rather than simple hill-climbing (always move toward lower miss rate)?
Show answer
Figure 6 shows the miss-rate-vs-prefetch-size curve can have multiple local minima for a given matrix. Pure hill-climbing would stop at whichever local dip it reaches first, which may not be the global best. Simulated annealing deliberately accepts some worse moves early (high temperature, T(k)=0.2×0.99^k), letting it hop out of a shallow local minimum before the acceptance probability cools toward zero and it commits to refining the best region found.Misconception: that greedily taking the locally best move is always safe for parameter tuning. It's only safe if the objective is convex (single minimum); non-convex curves need some willingness to temporarily get worse.
Problem 8 · easy
For an extremely sparse matrix like p2p-Gnutella08 (very short fibers, few non-zeros per row), which of SeaCache's three techniques matters least, and why?
Show answer
Fiber packing likely matters most (very short fibers waste the most space in any fixed block), while the exact replacement policy matters less — with so few non-zeros, even plain LRU or X-Cache's simple approach already achieves relatively low miss rates on these matrices, because there's little data to misjudge. The paper's own results show X-Cache and InnerSP both do comparatively well on p2p-Gnutella08 and p2p-Gnutella31 specifically, versus struggling elsewhere.Misconception: that SeaCache's gains are uniform across all matrices. Each technique's payoff depends on the sparsity pattern, and extremely sparse matrices are where the mapping fix (not the replacement fix) does most of the work.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Twelve questions newcomers ask most.

Q1What exactly is a "fiber," and why not just call it a cache line?▾
A fiber is a full row or column of a sparse tensor in compressed form — a variable-length sequence of non-zero (coordinate, value) pairs. It's the natural unit of reuse in sparse tensor algebra, but unlike a cache line, its length isn't fixed, which is the entire mapping problem SeaCache solves.
Q2Is SeaCache a new dataflow or accelerator design?▾
No — it only modifies the on-chip cache (mapping + replacement + capacity sharing), and is explicitly orthogonal to the PE array design and dataflow choice. It's evaluated mainly with the Gustavson's dataflow but also shown to work with condensed Outer Product (SpArch's dataflow).
Q3Why is Belady's Optimal achievable here when it's usually theoretical?▾
In SpMSpM with Gustavson's dataflow, which 𝐵 rows get reused, and when, is fully determined by the non-zero pattern of the other operand, 𝐴 — which is already known (it's the input data, not a prediction). Reading ahead in 𝐴's structure literally reveals the future, something general-purpose caches never have access to.
Q4If gLRU realizes that optimal, why replace it with gLFU at all?▾
Not because gLRU is inaccurate, but because implementing it is expensive: either an offline transpose (up to 126% extra compute, unless amortized over reuse) or online per-column linked lists whose pointers scale with the tensor's dimension 𝐾 at ~20 bits each. gLFU replaces those pointers with 4-bit counters — about 5× narrower — while measuring empirically about as well.
Q5What's the tradeoff for giving up gLRU's optimality guarantee?▾
gLFU is no longer provably optimal — a frequency count can't fully capture ordering information a linked list preserves. In practice, on the evaluated matrices, idealized gLFU (15.9% miss rate) performed at least as well as idealized gLRU (16.5%) under the same finite prefetch window, so the practical gap is small or even favors gLFU here.
Q6Why do virtual tags matter so much?▾
A counter can only exist where a fiber has a cache slot. Without virtual tags, the counters for fibers not yet cached — often exactly the ones that matter most for an upcoming decision — simply don't exist, degrading miss rate 2.6× versus the idealized (unlimited-metadata) case. A modest 4 virtual tags per set recovers most of that gap for a small (~1.8%) bit-overhead.
Q7How does fiber packing know which fibers can share a block?▾
Only fibers with contiguous IDs mapping to the same set can pack together, since their tags must be storable as one base tag plus a small count. SeaCache widens the "Tag-L" bits it skips when computing set IDs (to 4 bits by default) specifically to make more contiguous IDs land in the same set, enabling more packing opportunities.
Q8Is there a limit to how long a fiber can be before splitting breaks?▾
Yes — because segment disambiguation relies on the low 16 bits of the original fiber ID, fibers longer than about 21,845 elements risk address aliasing between segments. This is essentially never hit in the evaluated SuiteSparse matrices, and tiling (which shortens fibers per tile) makes it even less likely in practice.
Q9Why share cache space between data and guide metadata instead of a separate SRAM buffer?▾
A dedicated buffer sized for the worst case would either be wastefully large for matrices needing little guide data, or too small for very sparse ones. Sharing lets the same physical SRAM serve whichever role is more valuable at the moment, with the two-phase mechanism deciding the split per matrix instead of per hardware instance.
Q10Does SeaCache beat scratchpad-style buffering too, not just other caches?▾
Yes — 2.1× on average over an optimized scratchpad design (Tailors). Scratchpads condense data well when tiles fit exactly, but can't easily reclaim space from an arbitrary-length fiber once it's evicted, and can't skip fetching fibers that turn out unneeded the way an on-demand cache can.
Q11Does any of this generalize beyond SpMSpM?▾
The paper evaluates 𝐹ᵀ×𝐹, 𝐹×𝐷 (SpMM), and 𝐹ᵀ×𝑆 (one MS-BFS iteration) in addition to self-multiplication SpMSpM, with fiber packing/splitting applying unchanged (any workload accesses data as fibers) and guided replacement applying wherever one operand's structure predicts another's reuse order.
Q12How does this connect to OS-level memory tiering research?▾
The pattern rhymes strongly: gLFU's narrow per-column counters are structurally similar to the lightweight access-frequency counters used by page-hotness trackers like DAMON or multi-gen LRU, chosen for the same reason (avoid expensive precise ordering machinery). Virtual tags are a hardware analogue of the "ghost" tracking used in ARC-style and exclusive caches — and conceptually close to how systems like HeMem or Memtis track candidate hot pages before they're promoted. The two-phase adaptive prefetch sizing (offline estimate, then online feedback-driven correction) is the same shape as adaptive hot-tier sizing in Memtis or dynamic pool sizing in Pond for CXL memory — start from a cheap static estimate, then let runtime signals correct it. None of this is claimed by the SeaCache paper itself; it's a useful cross-domain lens, not a paper claim.

Virtual tagA cache-set slot holding only a gLFU counter, no data — extra room to track fibers not yet cached that matter for future decisions.
Two-phase adaptive prefetch sizingOffline sparsity-based initial estimate, then online simulated-annealing correction, for how much cache the guide buffer should occupy.
Cnt / Extra bitsPer-block tag metadata: Cnt (2 b) marks packing mode and fiber count; Extra (16 b) holds offsets (packing) or the original low ID bits (splitting).
Tag-H / Tag-LThe split fiber-ID tag: Tag-L bits are skipped when forming the set index (to let contiguous IDs land together for packing), Tag-H is the rest.
Supporting terms
Fiber-ID-based mappingX-Cache's technique of indexing the cache directly by fiber (row/column) ID instead of physical address.
Belady's OptimalEvict the block used farthest in the future; the theoretical best, realizable here because one operand's structure reveals the other's future.
Gustavson's (Gust) / IP / OP dataflowThree SpMSpM loop orders; Gust is SeaCache's default, chosen for balanced reuse across operands.
Meta-tagA tag storing a fiber ID directly, avoiding compressed-format pointer chasing to find a physical address.
Prefetch buffer / guide metadataThe window of the guiding tensor (𝐴) kept on-chip to compute reuse counts for the other tensor (𝐵).
Discard rate / miss rate (online signals)Discard rate: fraction of dropped counter increments — high means too much metadata was prefetched, so shrink the window. Miss rate: fraction of accesses lacking any counter — high means insufficient prefetching, so grow the window.
Simulated annealingSearch that accepts some worse moves early (high "temperature," cooling over iterations) to avoid getting stuck in a local minimum of the miss-rate curve.
SuiteSparse Matrix CollectionThe real-world sparse matrix benchmark suite used for evaluation, spanning densities from 0.0006% to 0.356%.
