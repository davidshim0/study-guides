ISCA 2025 · A Field Guide

Don't prefetch what the power failure will erase anyway

A battery-less sensor runs on a capacitor the size of a coin cell. It reboots, computes for a moment, and dies — over and over. This is the plain-English story of IPEX, a tiny extension that stops prefetchers from wasting hard-harvested energy on data that never survives to be used.

📄 "Rethinking Prefetching for Intermittent Computing" · Fang, Zeng, Gupta, Jung 🎓 Some intermittent-computing basics covered in Start Here ⏱ ~25 min read
🔋

Prefetch only what will be USED before the next outage — not whatever the prefetcher would fetch if power were infinite.
Read the capacitor voltage like a countdown clock, and throttle accordingly.

Before you begin

Quick glossary, then eight things unique to intermittent computing

You already know caches, LRU, and prefetching cold. What's new here is the failure model: the whole machine dies and reboots, repeatedly, mid-execution. That's what these primers cover.

Quick glossary

1Why EHSs even need this: capacitors, not batteries▾

An EHS has no battery — it trickle-charges a tiny capacitor from RF, solar, or thermal energy. It boots only once that capacitor is charged enough, runs until the capacitor drains, dies, and starts recharging. This repeats for the device's entire life, so "execution" is really a string of short, unpredictable power cycles.

Now you're ready: everything in IPEX is about making the most of one of these short, doomed power cycles before it ends.
2What survives a power failure, and what doesn't▾

Main memory is NVM (nonvolatile) so it survives. But the SRAM cache sitting in front of it, and all CPU registers, are volatile — they're wiped instantly on power loss. A JIT-checkpointing controller races the voltage monitor: the moment it senses an outage coming, it copies dirty cache lines to NVM and registers to nonvolatile flip-flops so execution can resume from that exact point after reboot.

Now you're ready: anything sitting only in the volatile cache when power dies — including a freshly prefetched block never touched — is gone. That's the entire vulnerability IPEX targets.
3Why the cache can't just be bigger▾

You'd expect a bigger cache to always help — fewer misses. But every SRAM cell leaks current even when idle, and that leakage is paid continuously, cycle after cycle, win or lose. Past roughly 2kB, the paper shows leakage grows faster than the miss-rate benefit shrinks, so speedup actually declines: at 8kB, over half the total system energy (54.38%) goes to ICache/DCache leakage alone.

Now you're ready: that's why the paper's EHS is stuck with a small, fixed 2kB cache each for ICache/DCache — which is exactly what makes every cache miss so costly and prefetching so tempting.
4Why in-order cores, and why misses hurt more here▾

EHSs use simple in-order cores for low power. An in-order core has no reorder buffer to hide a stall behind other useful work — on a cache miss it just stops and waits for the NVM access to complete. In the paper's default config, 23.45% of total execution time is lost to ICache-miss stalls and 18.64% to DCache-miss stalls (over 60% for some benchmarks).

Now you're ready: reducing misses via prefetching has outsized value here — but so does avoiding a wasted prefetch, since the pipeline was never designed to hide either kind of cost.
5Reuse distance, applied to a shrinking clock▾

Reuse distance is just "how soon will this block be touched again." Normally you use it to decide what to keep in cache. IPEX repurposes the same idea against a hard deadline: if a block's expected reuse falls beyond the current power cycle (i.e., after the next outage), fetching it now buys nothing — it'll be erased unread.

Now you're ready: IPEX's whole design goal is to only prefetch blocks whose reuse distance fits inside the time remaining in this power cycle.
6Capacitor voltage as a proxy for "time left"▾

You can't directly measure "how many cycles until failure," but capacitor voltage tracks remaining stored energy, and — under a given input-energy quality — falling voltage correlates with an approaching outage. It's an imperfect, indirect signal (input energy fluctuates), which is exactly why IPEX doesn't trust one fixed threshold and instead adapts it (Section 4.1.1).

Now you're ready: "voltage crosses a threshold" is IPEX's stand-in for "the deadline is approaching" throughout the paper.
7Prefetch accuracy vs. coverage▾

Two standard prefetcher scorecards. Accuracy = fraction of issued prefetches that turn out useful (hit before eviction/use). Coverage = fraction of all misses that a prefetcher successfully turns into hits. A prefetcher can have high coverage by being aggressive, at the cost of low accuracy (lots of wasted fetches) — precisely the trade IPEX is built to rebalance.

Now you're ready: IPEX's central result (Table 2) is raising accuracy substantially while barely touching coverage — throttling the wasteful fetches, not the useful ones.
8Sequential and stride prefetchers (the baselines here)▾

The paper's default prefetchers are simple: a sequential instruction prefetcher fetches the next line(s) after the current one; a stride data prefetcher detects a constant address delta (e.g., A, A+4, A+8, …) and keeps fetching ahead by that stride. Both have a prefetch degree register controlling how many blocks they fetch per trigger — the exact knob IPEX turns.

Now you're ready: IPEX doesn't replace these prefetchers — it sits alongside them and dynamically resets their existing degree register.
The whole story in one place

IPEX in a nutshell

The problem, the signal that fixes it, and how the pieces connect.

Prefetching is a bet: fetch a block before it's needed, hoping it gets used. On a normal system, a wrong bet just costs a bit of bandwidth. On an EHS, a wrong bet can cost far more — because if power fails before the prefetched block is touched, the entire volatile cache is erased, and the energy spent fetching it is gone for nothing. Worse, if that block was genuinely needed, the program pays for it again as an expensive NVM read after reboot.

IPEX's fix is to treat the capacitor voltage as a countdown clock and throttle the underlying prefetcher's degree — how many blocks it fetches at once — accordingly. When voltage is high (failure unlikely soon), IPEX leaves the prefetcher alone in high-performance mode. As voltage crosses one of several voltage thresholds on the way down, IPEX halves the degree, entering energy-saving mode; crossing back up doubles it again. IPEX also learns from experience: each reboot, it checks last cycle's throttling rate and nudges its thresholds — lowering them (more prefetching) if it over-throttled, raising them (less prefetching) if it under-throttled.

The payoff: across 20 embedded benchmarks, IPEX cuts energy consumption by an average of 7.86% (up to 21.64%) and improves performance by an average of 8.96% (up to 23.49%) over a conventional, always-on prefetcher — using just 4 registers per cache (0.0018% of core area) and reusing the prefetcher hardware that's already there.

🔋 Key takeaways

  • A prefetched-but-unused block isn't just neutral on an EHS — power failure erases it, wasting the energy spent fetching it (and often forcing a costlier re-fetch later).
  • Prefetching is worthwhile only if its hit probability exceeds a threshold set by leak vs. prefetch energy: P > 1 − Eleak/(Eprefetch+Eleak). In the paper's default config, that minimum is 46.04%, comfortably below the observed 54.03%/52.88% baseline accuracy.
  • IPEX reformulates "when to throttle" as crossing voltage thresholds, and "how much" as halving/doubling the prefetch degree.
  • A single fixed threshold under- or over-throttles; IPEX uses multiple graduated thresholds plus a throttling-rate feedback loop that retunes them every power cycle.
  • Cost: just 4 registers per cache (99 bits × 2 = 198 bits total, 0.0018% of core area) — no new memory structures, all logic reused from the existing prefetcher.
  • Result: 7.86% avg energy reduction (up to 21.64%), 8.96% avg speedup (up to 23.49%) over a conventional always-on prefetcher.
💡
For tiering/CXL readers: this maps loosely onto over-eager page promotion in a DRAM/NVM (or CXL) tier under a tight promotion-bandwidth or capacity budget — promoting a page you won't touch again soon wastes the same "budget" a truly hot page needed. It's a useful analogy, not a claim from the paper: IPEX throttles on a cheap proxy signal (capacitor voltage) rather than perfect foresight, in a similar spirit to budget-aware promotion throttling in systems like TPP or HeMem, just triggered by remaining energy instead of remaining bandwidth/headroom.
Five everyday pictures

How to think about IPEX

Each stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with where the picture stops being true.

The core idea · Timeliness under a deadline

Packing a bag for a trip that might get cut short

You're packing for a trip, but there's a real chance it ends abruptly at any moment. Anything you pack that you won't use before the cutoff was wasted effort — worse, if you still need it, you'll have to go get it again later anyway. IPEX applies exactly this logic to prefetching: only fetch what you'll use before the next power failure.

Where it breaks: a traveler usually knows roughly how long the trip is. IPEX never knows the exact cutoff — only a noisy proxy (voltage) — which is why it hedges with graduated thresholds instead of one hard cutoff.
Bi-modal control · High-performance vs. energy-saving mode

A phone's low-battery mode

When your phone's battery drops below a threshold, it dims the screen and pauses background refresh — not because those features are bad, just because it's not the moment for them. It un-throttles once you plug in. IPEX's two modes work the same way: voltage above the threshold, the prefetcher runs at full degree; voltage below, IPEX halves it; voltage recovers, it doubles back.

Where it breaks: a phone typically has one low-battery threshold. IPEX defaults to two voltage thresholds so it can back off in graduated steps rather than one abrupt cliff (Section 6.7.1 shows a single threshold performs worst).
Adaptive thresholds · Throttling-rate feedback

A thermostat that learns from last week

A smart thermostat doesn't just react to today's temperature — it checks how often it had to intervene last week and adjusts its setpoint accordingly. IPEX does the same at every reboot: it computes last cycle's throttling rate Ptr and, if it throttled too often (≥5%), lowers the voltage threshold by 0.05V so it prefetches more; otherwise it raises it by 0.05V to save more energy.

Where it breaks: the thermostat adjusts continuously; IPEX only re-tunes once per power cycle, at reboot — so within a single (possibly long) cycle, the threshold is frozen regardless of how conditions change mid-cycle.
Graduated throttling · Multiple thresholds beat one

Feathering the brakes, not slamming them

Approaching a stop sign, a good driver eases off the gas, then brakes gradually as the sign gets closer — not full brake pressure applied once, far away, and left there. The paper's naive single-threshold design does exactly that (Fig. 8): it drops the degree once and never adjusts again, even as the voltage keeps falling toward failure. IPEX's multiple thresholds (default: two) let it keep tightening — halving the degree again at each successive crossing.

Where it breaks: a driver can brake continuously; IPEX only has as many discrete steps as it has thresholds (2 by default), and adding more (3+) starts to plateau or overshoot (Section 6.7.1).
Avoiding duplicate work · Prefetch buffer lookup

Checking if you already hit "order" before ordering again

If your food delivery hasn't arrived yet, you don't place a second identical order — you check the app first. IPEX does this on a genuine cache miss: it checks the prefetch buffer for a pending request to the same block, and if found, just waits for it instead of issuing a redundant NVM read (Section 5.1).

Where it breaks: checking your delivery app is free; here it's a real (if cheap) buffer lookup on every miss — and it only prevents a duplicate request, it doesn't make a late prefetch arrive any sooner.
The full explanation

How it actually works

Section by section, with the paper's key figures rebuilt as diagrams you can read at a glance.

1 · The problem — a prefetch is a bet the power failure can void

Prefetching moves a block from NVM to cache before it's requested, hoping for a hit. On an EHS, if power fails before that block is used, the entire volatile cache — including the prefetched block — is wiped. The energy spent fetching it (Eprefetch) is gone for nothing, and if the block really was needed, the program now pays for an expensive NVM read anyway after reboot. That's the exact scenario in Fig. 5 of the paper: Block A gets used and hits; Block B is prefetched, never touched before failure at T2, and has to be re-read from NVM at T3.

2 · Does prefetching even pay off? — the energy inequality

The paper formalizes this with two energy-waste equations. Without prefetching, the only waste is leakage during a miss stall:

E_waste_no_prefetch = E_leak

With a prefetcher, a useful prefetch wastes nothing; a useless one wastes both the fetch energy and the leakage during whatever stall still occurs:

E_waste_prefetcher =
  0                      # useful prefetch
  E_prefetch + E_leak      # useless prefetch

For prefetching to help at all, its expected waste must undercut the no-prefetch baseline. Requiring (1 − P) × E_waste_prefetcher < E_waste_no_prefetch rearranges to a minimum required hit probability:

P > 1 − E_leak / (E_prefetch + E_leak)
🔋
Plugged into the paper's default config: the minimum required P works out to 46.04%. The observed baseline accuracy — 54.03% for the instruction prefetcher, 52.88% for the data prefetcher — clears that bar, confirming prefetching is worth doing on this EHS before IPEX even gets involved. IPEX's job is to push accuracy higher still by cutting the useless fraction.

3 · IPEX overview — bi-modal control

IPEX doesn't replace the prefetcher; it wraps it in two modes. In high-performance mode (voltage comfortably above threshold), the prefetcher runs at its normal degree. In energy-saving mode (voltage has dropped below a threshold), IPEX halves the degree, so only the blocks most likely to be used before failure get fetched. Crossing back above the threshold doubles the degree again — IPEX is happy to be aggressive whenever there's reason to think power is not imminent.

4 · When to throttle — voltage thresholds, and learning the right one

A threshold set too high causes eager throttling — reached soon after reboot, far from any real danger, needlessly starving useful prefetches. Too low causes lazy throttling — reached only right before failure, after energy has already been wasted on prefetches that won't survive. IPEX starts from empirical defaults (3.3V and 3.25V) and then adapts.

The adaptation uses four small per-cache registers: R_throttled, R_total (both 32-bit counters), R_tr (the computed throttling rate), and R_ipd (the 3-bit initial prefetch degree, since IPEX caps degree at 4). At reboot, IPEX restores the checkpointed counters, computes R_tr = R_throttled / R_total, and adjusts:

at reboot:
  R_tr = R_throttled / R_total
  if R_tr >= 5%:      # over-throttled last cycle
      V_thres -= 0.05V     # prefetch more next time
  else:                 # under-throttled
      V_thres += 0.05V     # save more energy next time

5 · How much to throttle — why one threshold isn't enough

A naive design uses one threshold and drops the degree once when crossed — but then does nothing further as voltage keeps falling. The paper's Fig. 8 example shows exactly this failing: degree drops from 6 to 3 at one crossing, but only 1 of those 3 prefetches turns out useful before failure — the naive scheme never adapts further as the outage gets closer.

IPEX instead uses multiple voltage thresholds (2 by default), halving the degree at each successive crossing on the way down and doubling it on the way up:

on voltage crossing threshold i going down:  R_cpd = R_cpd / 2
on voltage crossing threshold i going up:    R_cpd = R_cpd × 2

6 · Hardware cost, exactly

Per cache (ICache and DCache each get their own, independent set): R_throttled, R_total, R_tr at 32 bits each, plus R_ipd at 3 bits (enough since IPEX caps degree at 4) = 99 bits/cache. Two caches = 198 bits total. Measured against a 0.54 mm² core (including caches) via CACTI at 45nm, that's 0.0018% of core area — reusing the prefetcher's existing hardware rather than adding new memory structures.

Follow the numbers, step by step

Worked examples

Four traces through the paper's real numbers — the energy inequality, a threshold adaptation, a degree ladder, and the accuracy payoff.

① Does prefetching pay off? — the energy inequality

Using Fig. 4's shape (Pmin = 1 − E_leak/(E_prefetch + E_leak)), suppose a cheaper NVM makes E_leak = 20pJ and E_prefetch = 60pJ. What accuracy would prefetching need to be worthwhile — and would the paper's baseline hit that bar?

  1. Plug in: Pmin = 1 − 20/(60+20) = 1 − 0.25 = 75%.
  2. Compare to the paper's observed baseline accuracy (54.03% ICache, 52.88% DCache) — both fall short of 75%. In this hypothetical, plain prefetching would hurt, not help.
  3. Now use the paper's actual default config instead: it works out to Pmin = 46.04% — a much lower bar, because its E_leak is comparatively larger relative to E_prefetch.
  4. Both observed accuracies (54.03%, 52.88%) clear 46.04%, so prefetching is worthwhile in the paper's actual setup — the hypothetical above just shows how fragile that conclusion is to the leak/prefetch energy ratio.
💡
Insight: whether prefetching helps at all on an EHS is not a given — it's a knife-edge condition set by the memory technology's own energy numbers. IPEX doesn't change this inequality; it improves P after the fact by cutting the useless-prefetch fraction.

② Threshold adaptation from Fig. 7's register trace

At reboot, IPEX restores R_throttled = 1 and R_total = 2 from NVM (1 block prefetched, 1 suppressed, during the prior cycle). Current threshold is 3.30V. What happens?

  1. Compute the throttling rate: R_tr = R_throttled / R_total = 1/2 = 50%.
  2. Compare to the 5% trigger: 50% ≥ 5%, so IPEX concludes it over-throttled last cycle — plenty of prefetches were suppressed that might have been useful.
  3. Lower the threshold by the fixed step: 3.30V − 0.05V = 3.25V. A lower threshold means IPEX will stay in high-performance mode longer next cycle, issuing more prefetches.
  4. Reset R_cpd to R_ipd (the original degree, e.g. 2) for the fresh cycle, and clear R_throttled/R_total to start counting again.
💡
This is the exact register arithmetic behind Fig. 7 — a 50% throttling rate is a loud signal that the threshold was set too conservatively, so IPEX relaxes it by one 0.05V step.

③ Stepping the degree with two thresholds (Fig. 9)

V1 = 3.30V, V2 = 3.25V, initial degree = 2. Trace the capacitor voltage: 3.35V → 3.28V → 3.35V → 3.28V → 3.22V.

  1. 3.35V (above V1): high-performance mode, degree stays at its full value, 2.
  2. 3.28V (crosses below V1): energy-saving mode, degree halves, 2 → 1.
  3. 3.35V (crosses back above V1): high-performance mode resumes, degree doubles, 1 → 2.
  4. 3.28V (crosses below V1 again): degree halves again, 2 → 1.
  5. 3.22V (also crosses below V2): degree halves once more, 1 → 0 — no prefetches at all while failure appears imminent.
🔋
Insight: the degree isn't just "on" or "off" — it's a small state machine driven entirely by which thresholds the voltage has most recently crossed, in either direction.

④ The accuracy payoff, and why it's cheap

Table 2 reports ICache accuracy rising from 54.03% (baseline) to 72.88% (with IPEX), and DCache from 52.88% to 64.93%, while coverage barely moves (−3% and −5% relatively). What's the relative improvement, and what did it cost?

  1. ICache relative gain: (72.88 − 54.03) / 54.03 = 18.85 / 54.03 ≈ 34.9% more of every prefetch turns out useful.
  2. DCache relative gain: (64.93 − 52.88) / 52.88 = 12.05 / 52.88 ≈ 22.8%.
  3. Coverage cost is small: ICache coverage drops only ~3% relatively (80.56% → 78.24%), DCache ~5% (64.51% → 61.44%) — IPEX is mostly cutting wasteful fetches, not useful ones.
  4. Hardware cost: just 198 bits total (4 registers × 2 caches), ≈0.0018% of a 0.54 mm² core — a rounding error next to a ~35%/~23% accuracy jump.
💡
Punchline: the entire energy/performance win traces back to this accuracy shift — fewer wasted fetches, almost as many useful ones, for a hardware cost too small to matter.
Test yourself

Practice problems

Try each before opening the answer. Every one ends with the misconception it checks for.

Problem 1 · easy
Figure 1 shows speedup rising then falling as cache size grows from 256B to 8kB, even though a larger cache generally has a lower miss rate. Explain the falling part.
Show answer
Past ~2kB, the cache's own leakage energy — paid continuously regardless of hits or misses — grows faster than the extra hits reduce stall energy. At 8kB, leakage eats more than half (54.38%) of total system energy, offsetting the miss-rate benefit and dragging speedup back down.Misconception: that a lower miss rate always means better performance/energy. On an EHS, a cache's fixed leakage cost can outweigh its miss-reduction benefit once it's "big enough."
Problem 2 · medium
Suppose a different NVM has E_leak = 30pJ and E_prefetch = 30pJ. What's the minimum accuracy P required for prefetching to help at all, and is a 52% baseline prefetcher (like this paper's DCache prefetcher) still worth using?
Show answer
P_min = 1 − 30/(30+30) = 1 − 0.5 = 50%. A 52% accurate prefetcher clears 50%, so yes — barely. But it's much closer to breaking even than the paper's actual 46.04% threshold, showing how sensitive the "is prefetching worth it" answer is to the leak/prefetch energy ratio for a given NVM technology.Misconception: that prefetching is unconditionally good "because it reduces misses." Its energy benefit depends on clearing a technology-specific accuracy threshold — below it, prefetching actively hurts.
Problem 3 · medium
At reboot, R_throttled = 1 and R_total = 40 (very few prefetches were throttled last cycle). Should IPEX raise or lower its voltage threshold, and why?
Show answer
R_tr = 1/40 = 2.5%, which is below the 5% trigger — IPEX concludes it under-throttled last cycle (barely held anything back). It raises the voltage threshold by 0.05V, making it enter energy-saving mode sooner next cycle, to actually capture some energy savings.Misconception: that a high throttling rate is always bad. A very low rate can also be a problem — it means IPEX barely intervened, leaving energy-saving opportunities on the table.
Problem 4 · hard
V1 = 3.30V, V2 = 3.20V, initial degree = 4. Trace the degree through this voltage sequence: 3.40V → 3.15V → 3.35V.
Show answer
3.40V (above V1): degree stays at 4. 3.15V drops below both V1 and V2 in one move — but IPEX applies one halving per threshold crossed, not per volt dropped, so degree halves twice: 4→2 (crossing V1), then 2→1 (crossing V2), landing at 1. Recovering to 3.35V (above V1) doubles it back up twice (crossing V2, then V1): 1→2→4.Misconception: that only the "final" threshold reached matters. Each threshold crossed in a single move triggers its own halving/doubling step, so jumping past two thresholds at once still applies two adjustments.
Problem 5 · medium
A naive single-threshold design drops degree from 6 to 3 the moment voltage first dips below 3.3V, then does nothing else as voltage keeps falling toward failure. Why does this still waste energy, per the paper's Fig. 8 example?
Show answer
The single adjustment assumes those 3 remaining prefetch slots will all be used before failure — but as voltage keeps dropping, that assumption gets worse, not better. In the paper's example, only 1 of the 3 blocks prefetched after the one throttling event is actually used before power dies; the other 2 are wasted anyway, because the design never re-checks as the deadline gets closer.Misconception: that throttling once, at the first sign of trouble, is "playing it safe." Without further adjustment as conditions worsen, a single threshold degrades to nearly the same waste as no throttling at all.
Problem 6 · easy
Section 6.7.7 shows IPEX gets a bigger speedup with PCM (12.84%) than with ReRAM (8.96%), even though the mechanism is identical. Why?
Show answer
PCM has higher per-access latency and energy than ReRAM, so every useless prefetch — and every re-fetch after a wasted one — costs more. IPEX's benefit scales with how expensive the wasted memory accesses it avoids actually are; a slower, costlier NVM technology means there's more to save.Misconception: that IPEX's benefit is a fixed percentage regardless of hardware. It scales with how expensive the underlying memory (and its mistakes) are — the same logic behind Section 6.7.6's memory-size trend (6.02% at 2MB → 11.17% at 32MB).
Problem 7 · hard
Section 6.3 notes that a high reduction in prefetch operations doesn't always translate into a proportional energy or performance win. Give a concrete way this can happen.
Show answer
IPEX's throttling isn't perfectly selective — it can occasionally suppress a prefetch that would have been useful, turning what would've been a hit into a real miss. That miss stalls the in-order pipeline and burns leakage energy during the stall, partly or fully offsetting the energy saved by not issuing the (mistakenly judged useless) prefetch. So a large raw reduction in prefetch count can coexist with only a small net energy/performance gain if too many of the suppressed prefetches were actually useful.Misconception: that "fewer prefetches" and "less waste" are the same thing. What matters is which prefetches are removed — removing useless ones helps, removing useful ones hurts, and a percentage count alone can't tell them apart.
Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

Ten questions newcomers ask most. Tap any to expand.

Q1What does IPEX actually change in the hardware?▾
Almost nothing new: it adds 4 small registers per cache (99 bits each, 198 bits total) and a bit of control logic that watches capacitor voltage and writes to the prefetcher's existing prefetch-degree register. It doesn't replace or redesign the prefetcher itself.
Q2Why is a prefetch more dangerous on an EHS than on a normal system?▾
On a normal system a wrong prefetch just wastes some bandwidth and cache space. On an EHS, power can fail before the prefetch is used, erasing the entire volatile cache — so the fetch energy is wasted outright, and if the data really was needed, it gets fetched again from NVM after reboot, paying the cost twice.
Q3How does IPEX know when a failure is coming?▾
It doesn't know exactly — it uses capacitor voltage as an imperfect proxy for "time remaining." Falling voltage generally correlates with an approaching outage, which is why IPEX reformulates "when to throttle" as crossing a voltage threshold rather than predicting failure directly.
Q4Why use multiple voltage thresholds instead of one?▾
A single threshold either throttles too early (eager, wasting useful prefetch opportunity) or too late (lazy, wasting energy on doomed prefetches) and, worse, only adjusts once — even as voltage keeps falling. Multiple thresholds (2 by default) let IPEX progressively tighten the prefetch degree as the danger increases, matching the pace of the real countdown more closely.
Q5How does the adaptive threshold actually adjust?▾
Every reboot, IPEX computes the throttling rate P_tr = R_throttled / R_total from the prior power cycle. If P_tr ≥ 5%, it lowers the voltage threshold by 0.05V (prefetch more next time); otherwise it raises it by 0.05V (save more energy). This closes the loop between "how much did I throttle" and "should I throttle more or less."
Q6Does IPEX increase cache misses noticeably by being too conservative?▾
Barely — ICache miss rate rises by only 0.08 percentage points and DCache by 0.02, on average. Combined with a 7.11% average reduction in prefetch operations, this is why the accuracy gain (Q9) translates into a net energy and performance win rather than just fewer, less useful prefetches.
Q7Does IPEX work with fancier prefetchers, not just sequential/stride?▾
Yes — any prefetcher that exposes a prefetch-degree-like control knob can use IPEX. The paper reports 7.89%–9.05% speedups across Sequential, Markov, and TIFS instruction prefetchers, and 8.76%–8.96% across Stride, GHB, and BO data prefetchers. More aggressive prefetchers (e.g., TIFS) benefit slightly more, since they generate more useless prefetches for IPEX to catch.
Q8When does IPEX help less?▾
With large capacitors (fewer, longer power cycles) or very stable energy sources (solar, thermal), power failures are rarer, giving IPEX fewer opportunities to throttle. The paper notes the gap between its most-stable and least-stable tested power traces is still only 1.14%, because even "stable" sources include enough poor-energy periods to keep outages frequent with the small 0.47µF capacitor used.
Q9What accuracy/coverage tradeoff does IPEX actually make?▾
It raises prefetch accuracy substantially — ICache 54.03%→72.88% (+34.9% relative), DCache 52.88%→64.93% (+22.8% relative) — while barely touching coverage (ICache −3%, DCache −5% relative). It's mostly filtering out the wasteful fetches, not the useful ones.
Q10Is IPEX tied to a specific crash-consistency mechanism?▾
Not entirely — IPEX targets cache-equipped EHSs whose volatile cache contents are lost on power failure; that's the specific vulnerability it exploits, and it's largely independent of how checkpointing/restoration itself is implemented. An EHS with a nonvolatile cache, or a very different memory hierarchy, wouldn't have this problem in the same form.

Glossary & further reading

Every term in one place, then results at a glance and provenance notes.

Core terms
IPEXIntermittence-aware Prefetching EXtension: throttles a prefetcher's degree based on capacitor voltage so it doesn't fetch blocks that won't survive to be used.
EHSEnergy Harvesting System — a batteryless device powered by a capacitor charged from ambient energy, running in short, repeated power cycles.
Power cycleOne reboot-to-outage interval; the unit of "session" execution IPEX optimizes within.
Prefetch degree (R_cpd)Number of blocks fetched at once by a prefetcher; IPEX's control knob, capped at 4, halved/doubled at voltage-threshold crossings.
Voltage threshold (V_thres)A capacitor-voltage tripwire that toggles IPEX between high-performance and energy-saving mode; default 2 thresholds, 3.3V and 3.25V.
Throttling rate (P_tr)R_throttled/R_total from the prior power cycle — the feedback signal used to adapt thresholds by ±0.05V at reboot.
Eager / lazy throttlingThreshold set too high (throttles too soon, wastes useful prefetches) vs. too low (throttles too late, wastes energy on doomed prefetches).
E_leak / E_prefetchLeakage energy during a stall vs. the energy to fetch a block; their ratio sets the minimum prefetch accuracy P needed for prefetching to be worthwhile.
Supporting terms
NVP / NVMNon-Volatile Processor / Memory — main memory that survives power loss, unlike the volatile SRAM cache and registers.
JIT checkpointJust-in-time save of dirty cache lines and registers to nonvolatile storage, triggered right before a detected power failure.
Prefetch accuracy / coverageFraction of issued prefetches that hit, vs. fraction of all misses eliminated by prefetching.
Reuse distanceHow soon a block will be accessed again — repurposed by IPEX against the deadline of the current power cycle.
Sequential / stride prefetcherThe paper's default ICache/DCache prefetchers: fetch the next line, or continue a detected constant address stride.
Prefetch bufferSmall buffer holding prefetched blocks separate from ICache/DCache, avoiding cache pollution; IPEX checks it on a cache miss to avoid issuing a redundant request for a block already in flight.
McPAT / NVSim / gem5The power/area model, NVM circuit model, and cycle-level simulator used to build and evaluate the paper's EHS.
