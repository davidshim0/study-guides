Cache Prefetching · CPU Microarchitecture · HPCA 2025

The prefetcher wants to jump the page boundary. About half the time, it's wrong.

L1D prefetchers see virtual addresses and could legally cross into the next page — CPU vendors permit it, academia mostly forbids it, and nobody publishes a policy. This paper measures what happens if you just let prefetchers cross (spoiler: coin-flip accuracy) and builds MOKA, a learned gate that decides per-request. Scope note: this is L1D/TLB microarchitecture, not DRAM/NVM tiering — but the TLB-pressure and page-walk-cost machinery here is the same substrate your tiering work sits on.

🖥️ VIPT L1D caches · Berti / BOP / IPCP prefetchers 🎓 Assumes virtual memory, TLB, and hardware prefetchers ⏱ ~35 min read
🛂

Score every page-crossing prefetch against cheap per-PC/delta history plus live TLB pressure; only issue it once the score clears a self-tuning threshold — otherwise discard it before it triggers a wasted speculative page walk.
Static "always cross" and "never cross" both lose. The winning move is per-request, per-phase judgment.

Before you begin

Nine ideas this paper builds on

You already know virtual memory, TLBs, and hardware prefetchers, so these skip the basics and go straight to what "crossing a page boundary" means for a prefetcher, why it's risky, and what MOKA/DRIPPER actually add. Tap any open; read in order the first time.

1Why an L1D prefetcher gets to decide about page boundaries at all▾

First-level data caches are almost always VIPT (Virtually Indexed, Physically Tagged): they're probed with the virtual address before translation even finishes. That means the L1D prefetcher sits right next to the virtual-address stream and has direct access to the TLB — unlike L2/LLC prefetchers, which sit downstream of translation and only ever see physical addresses (PIPT caches).

So when an L1D prefetcher's pattern (e.g., "stride +1 cache line") would land past the end of the current 4 KB page, it has a real choice: stay inside the page (in-page prefetch), or reach into the next page (page-cross prefetch) by consulting the TLB it already has access to.

Now you're ready: "page-cross prefetching" is a decision that is only architecturally possible at VIPT structures like L1D — that's why this whole paper is scoped to L1D, not L2/LLC.
2Two TLB levels, two very different sizes▾

The paper's simulated core (Table IV) has a small first-level dTLB — 64 entries, 4-way, 1-cycle — backed by a much larger unified second-level sTLB — 1536 entries, 12-way, 8-cycle. A translation that hits dTLB never has to touch sTLB; a translation that misses dTLB but hits sTLB avoids a page walk but pays the 8-cycle sTLB latency.

Because dTLB is ~24× smaller than sTLB, a handful of extra or avoided misses moves dTLB's MPKI (misses per kilo-instruction) far more than it moves sTLB's — this is exactly the asymmetry the paper measures in Figure 4 and Figure 12.

Now you're ready: whenever this guide says "dTLB is more sensitive than sTLB," it's because of this capacity gap, not some property of page-cross prefetching itself.
3What a page walk actually costs when it's speculative▾

The simulated system uses a 5-level radix page table with an x86 hardware page-table walker and split Page Structure Caches (PSCs) at 4 levels (L5: 1-entry, L4: 2-entry, L3: 8-entry, L2: 32-entry) to cache intermediate walk state. When a translation isn't TLB-resident, the walker can still initiate a walk speculatively — i.e., before any real (demand) access needs it — to serve a page-cross prefetch.

The paper's own accounting: a fully-missed speculative page walk costs up to 4 memory accesses (one per uncached radix level) plus 1 for the actual prefetch fetch itself — up to 5 useless memory accesses total if the prefetch turns out wrong.

Now you're ready: "5 useless accesses" is the paper's recurring cost unit for one bad page-cross prefetch — you'll see it again in Worked Examples.
4Meet the three L1D prefetchers this paper filters▾

Berti (MICRO'22) — a state-of-the-art "local delta" prefetcher: per-PC, it tracks recent address deltas and prefetches the best one(s) ahead. BOP (Best-Offset Prefetcher, HPCA'16) — searches for a single best constant offset that historically produced timely hits. IPCP (Bouquet of Instruction Pointers, ISCA'20) — classifies each load PC into a pattern class (stream/CS/GS/etc.) and prefetches accordingly.

All three normally compute their next address as "current virtual address + delta/offset" and, near a page boundary, that arithmetic can spill into the next page. The paper doesn't change any of these prefetchers' internal logic — it adds an external gate that only vetoes their cross-page requests.

Now you're ready: MOKA/DRIPPER is prefetcher-agnostic by design — it wraps Berti, BOP, or IPCP without touching their prediction logic (Table II shows each gets its own small feature set).
5The two "obvious" static policies — and why neither wins▾

Permit PGC: always let the prefetcher cross pages. Discard PGC: never let it — the traditional academic default. The paper's motivational study (Section II-C) runs both, for all three prefetchers, across memory-intensive SPEC/GAP/LIGRA/PARSEC/GeekBench/Qualcomm workloads.

Result: Permit PGC beats Discard PGC on some workloads (e.g., astar, cc.road, MIS, vips) by a lot, and loses badly on others (sphinx3, fotonik3d_s, bc.web). There is no single static answer — it's workload- and phase-dependent.

Now you're ready: this workload-by-workload split (Figure 2 in the paper) is the entire motivation for building a dynamic filter instead of picking a static policy.
6Coverage vs. accuracy — two different prefetching scorecards▾

Coverage: of all demand misses that occurred, what fraction did prefetching eliminate? Accuracy: of all prefetches issued, what fraction were actually used (hit at least once) before eviction? A prefetcher can have high coverage and low accuracy — it catches most misses, but wastes a lot of bandwidth doing it.

The paper deliberately does not optimize DRIPPER for accuracy directly (a threshold demanding very high accuracy leaves performance on the table) or for coverage (different misses matter differently for performance) — it optimizes for IPC speedup and reports coverage/accuracy afterward as explanatory evidence.

Now you're ready: when Section V says "DRIPPER matches Permit PGC's coverage but with much higher accuracy," that's the mechanism behind its speedup, not the optimization target itself.
7A hashed perceptron predictor, in one paragraph▾

A hashed perceptron predictor keeps one small table (a Weight Table, WT) per feature. To predict, it hashes the feature's current value (e.g., the PC), indexes the WT, and reads out a small signed saturating counter (a "weight"). Weights from every feature are summed; the sign/magnitude of the sum drives the decision. Training nudges the weight up or down (increment/decrement, saturating at the counter's bit-width limits) after the outcome is known.

This is the same family of predictor used for branch prediction and reuse prediction — MOKA repurposes it here to score one narrow question: "will this specific page-cross prefetch be worth it?"

Now you're ready: everywhere this guide says "weight," picture one small saturating counter, indexed by a hash of some program feature, that gets nudged after every training event.
8Program features vs. system features▾

A program feature is derived from the current memory request itself — PC, virtual address bits, the delta the prefetcher used, XORs of these. It answers "does this kind of request usually pay off when it crosses a page?" A system feature is a live runtime metric (sTLB MPKI, sTLB Miss Rate, LLC MPKI, …) compared against a threshold; it answers "is the system currently in a state where crossing pages tends to help or hurt?"

Program features alone can't see that the same instruction's cross-page prefetch might be great during one phase and harmful during another (e.g., under LLC pressure) — that's exactly what system features add.

Now you're ready: DRIPPER's final designs (Table II) each use exactly 1 program feature + 2 system features — the two feature families are complementary, not redundant.
9MOKA vs. DRIPPER — framework vs. prototype▾

MOKA is the general framework: a menu of 55 program features + 6 system features, a hashed-perceptron scoring mechanism, a training loop (via two small buffers, vUB and pUB), and an epoch-based scheme that re-tunes the activation threshold Ta at runtime. It's a recipe for building a "Page-Cross Filter" for any VIPT L1D prefetcher.

DRIPPER is what you get when you run that recipe's feature-selection process (Section III-D3) against Berti, BOP, and IPCP specifically — three concrete, small (1.44 KB/core), production-shaped filters (Table II).

Now you're ready: you're ready for the rest of the guide. Everywhere you see "the filter" or "the Page-Cross Filter," that's MOKA's mechanism; everywhere you see "DRIPPER," that's one trained, feature-selected instance of it.
The whole story in one place

MOKA / DRIPPER in a nutshell

The motivational finding, the mechanism, and the headline numbers — recomputed and cross-checked against the paper's tables.

CPU vendors let L1D prefetchers cross page boundaries (they have direct TLB access, being VIPT structures) but disclose nothing about how they decide when. Academic prefetchers, meanwhile, are almost universally restricted to in-page prefetching and simply discard anything that would cross a page. This paper asks the obvious question nobody had measured: if you just let three state-of-the-art L1D prefetchers (Berti, BOP, IPCP) always cross pages, does it help?

The answer is a firm "it depends." Across memory-intensive workloads, Permit PGC (always cross) beats Discard PGC (never cross) on some workloads by a wide margin and loses badly on others — there is no static winner. Digging into why, the paper finds that of the page-cross prefetches actually issued under Permit PGC, only ~50% are useful on average (the rest pollute the cache and TLB and, when the translation isn't resident, trigger a wasted speculative page walk of up to 5 memory accesses). State-of-the-art L1D prefetchers, in other words, are good at prefetching but genuinely bad at knowing when to cross a page.

The fix is MOKA: a framework for building a Page-Cross Filter that sits between "prefetcher wants to cross a page" and "prefetch is actually issued." It scores each candidate cross-page request with a hashed-perceptron sum over a handful of program features (PC, address bits, delta) and system features (TLB/cache pressure), compares the sum to an activation threshold Ta, and trains itself using two small buffers that watch L1D demand accesses and evictions. An epoch-based scheme continuously re-tunes Ta to the current workload phase.

DRIPPER is MOKA applied to Berti, BOP, and IPCP: three tiny (1.44 KB/core), prefetcher-independent filters (1 program feature + 2 system features each) that beat both static policies for every prefetcher tested, hold up on 178 unseen workloads and 300 random 8-core mixes, and keep winning when 2 MB large pages enter the picture.

🍯 Key takeaways

  • No static page-cross policy wins across workloads — Permit PGC and Discard PGC each win on a disjoint subset (Fig. 2), so the right question is per-request, not per-system.
  • When always permitted, only ~50% of issued page-cross prefetches are useful, across all three prefetchers (Fig. 3) — the inaccuracy is real, not an edge case.
  • One inaccurate page-cross prefetch costs up to 5 useless memory accesses (4 for a speculative page walk + 1 for the fetch) plus cache/TLB pollution.
  • MOKA = hashed-perceptron program features + threshold-gated system features + an epoch-based adaptive Ta, packaged as a reusable recipe for any VIPT L1D prefetcher.
  • DRIPPER (Berti) needs just 1 program + 2 system features and 1.44 KB/core, yet beats Permit PGC by +2.5% and Discard PGC by +1.7% geomean IPC (218 seen workloads).
  • DRIPPER matches Permit PGC's miss-coverage gain (~4.1% vs ~4.2%) while turning Permit PGC's -2.6% accuracy loss into a +1.2% accuracy gain, both over Discard PGC.
  • Gains generalize: +2.1%/+1.2% on 178 unseen workloads, +3.3%/+2.0% on 300 8-core mixes, and DRIPPER still wins with 2 MB large pages in the mix.
Six everyday pictures

How to think about MOKA / DRIPPER

Each picture stands alone — read in any order. Every card ends with an honest note about where the analogy stops being true.

The core decision · page-cross prefetch

Passport control at the page border

Every request that wants to leave Page A for Page B has to go through a checkpoint. Requests that stay inside Page A never see it. The Page-Cross Filter is that checkpoint: it doesn't slow down local traffic, it only interrogates the requests trying to cross — and it decides using the requester's "travel history" (program features) and today's border conditions (system features like TLB pressure).

Where it breaks: a real checkpoint's decision is final; MOKA's is continuously retrained — a request pattern denied today can be approved next epoch once vUB (Primer/analogy 6) proves it should have been let through.
The mechanism · hashed perceptron

A committee of narrow specialists

Instead of one expert judging "is this a good page-cross prefetch," MOKA convenes a small committee: one specialist per feature (one for the PC, one for the delta, one for the virtual-address bits). Each specialist has learned, from a lifetime of saturating-counter nudges, one narrow opinion. Their votes (weights) are simply summed — the committee's total confidence is the sum of independent, cheap opinions.

Where it breaks: real committees deliberate; these specialists never see each other's votes or reasoning — the sum is computed the same way whether the features agree wildly or barely correlate, which is precisely why feature selection (Section III-D3) matters so much.
The system features · phase awareness

A thermostat that reads the weather report first

A program feature is like knowing a person's habits; a system feature is like checking today's weather before trusting that habit. "This PC's cross-page prefetches are usually good" is a habit — but during a phase of high sTLB pressure or low sTLB pressure, that habit's payoff can flip. MOKA's system features are threshold-gated dials that only speak up when the weather (TLB/cache pressure) actually matches their specialty.

Where it breaks: a thermostat reacts to one signal; MOKA layers two deliberately complementary sTLB signals (MPKI for low-pressure phases, Miss Rate for high-pressure ones) precisely because one metric alone misses half the relevant weather.
The training loop · vUB & pUB

Two detectives' notebooks

The vUB (Virtual Update Buffer) is the notebook of "suspects released without charge" — page-cross prefetches the filter discarded. If a later demand miss lands exactly there, it's proof the release was a mistake, and the responsible weights get a positive nudge. The pUB (Physical Update Buffer) is the case file on everyone the filter let through: if the fetched block gets used before eviction, weights go up; if it's evicted with zero hits, weights go down.

Where it breaks: real detectives use one address book; MOKA needs two because vUB must record virtual addresses (a discarded prefetch never got a physical translation) while pUB must record physical addresses (L1D evictions are checked against the physically-tagged cache).
The adaptive scheme · epoch-based Ta

A bouncer who adjusts the door policy by the hour

A single fixed admission bar (a static Ta) is either too strict for a quiet night or too loose for a packed one. MOKA's bouncer checks the room every epoch — recent accuracy, IPC, how full the "waiting area" (ROB) is, how loud the "kitchen" (LLC) is — and quietly raises the bar during dangerous conditions (tightening to th) or eases it back down as accuracy improves, one notch (Ta±1) at a time.

Where it breaks: a human bouncer uses judgment; MOKA's scheme is a fixed set of threshold rules (Fig. 8, steps i–vii) — it can't invent a new heuristic mid-shift, only re-weight the ones it was built with.
Static vs. learned · the paper's core contrast

A blanket rule vs. asking every time

Discard PGC is "never bring an umbrella" — safe on sunny days, soaked on rainy ones. Permit PGC is "always bring one" — dry in the rain, needlessly burdened on sunny days. DRIPPER is the person who actually checks the sky (and the forecast trend) before each trip. It doesn't need to be right every single time to beat both blanket rules in the long run — it only needs to be right more often than either fixed bet.

Where it breaks: DRIPPER isn't infallible even in aggregate — the paper reports a real tail of short-running QMM INT/FP workloads where it briefly underperforms Discard PGC (Fig. 10, Fig. 14), because a few phases don't give the adaptive scheme enough time to calibrate.
Section by section

How MOKA and DRIPPER actually work

The motivational measurement → the filter's five hardware components → prediction → training → the adaptive threshold → feature selection and storage. Structure and numbers follow Sections II–III of the paper.

1 · The motivational measurement (Section II)

Before proposing anything, the paper runs Berti, BOP, and IPCP in two fixed modes — Discard PGC (never cross) and Permit PGC (always cross) — across memory-intensive SPEC 2006/2017, GAP, LIGRA, PARSEC, GeekBench, and Qualcomm CVP-1 workloads, on ChampSim with a full page-table-walk model (5-level radix tree, x86 walker, split PSCs). Figure 2 of the paper shows per-workload IPC of Permit PGC over Discard PGC: strongly positive for some workloads, strongly negative for others, for all three prefetchers.

Splitting Berti's workloads into "Permit PGC wins" vs. "Discard PGC wins" and comparing dTLB/sTLB/L1D/LLC MPKI (paper's Fig. 4) shows a consistent asymmetry: the "Permit wins" group sees dTLB MPKI drop far more than sTLB MPKI (because dTLB is the 64-entry structure and sTLB the 1536-entry one — Primer 2); the "Discard wins" group sees the opposite — all four MPKIs increase under Permit PGC, dTLB again moving the most.

◆
The governing tradeoff (memorize this). An accurate page-cross prefetch improves TLB hit rate (prefetches a translation ahead of the demand access), improves cache hit rate, and improves prefetch timeliness. An inaccurate one triggers up to 5 useless memory accesses (a wasted speculative page walk plus the wasted fetch) and pollutes both the TLB and the cache. Since ~50% of Permit PGC's cross-page prefetches are useless on average (Fig. 3), a system that always permits is gambling on a near coin flip every time a prefetcher's arithmetic spills past a page boundary.

2 · Design overview — where the filter sits (Fig. 5)

MOKA assumes a VIPT L1D. On every L1D access, the prefetcher produces a candidate request. The system first checks (A) whether the request crosses a page boundary; if not, it's issued immediately, no filter involved. If it does cross, the Page-Cross Filter (B) activates and decides issue-or-discard. A prefetch that passes still has to walk the TLB hierarchy (C) to resolve the target page's translation; if it misses, a speculative page walk (D) is triggered to fetch it before the prefetched block can actually be requested from L2C/LLC/DRAM.

3 · The five hardware components of the filter

Every Page-Cross Filter built with MOKA uses the same five pieces: (1) Perceptron predictors — one hashed-perceptron Weight Table (WT) per selected program feature, weights realized as saturating counters. (2) System-feature weights — saturating counters gated by a threshold comparison against a live system metric. (3) Virtual Update Buffer (vUB) — records discarded page-cross prefetches by virtual address, to catch false negatives. (4) Physical Update Buffer (pUB) — records issued page-cross prefetches by physical address, to catch both true positives and false positives. (5) Adaptive Thresholding Scheme — epoch-based logic that retunes the activation threshold Ta.

4 · Prediction — four stages (Fig. 6)

For every page-cross candidate: Stage 1 extracts each selected program feature from the current request, hashes it, indexes the matching WT, and reads out a weight wi. Stage 2 evaluates each selected system feature against its own threshold (e.g., is sTLB MPKI < TsTLB_MPKI?) — only features whose condition holds contribute their weight. Stage 3 sums every contributing weight into wfinal. Stage 4 compares wfinal to the activation threshold Ta: greater → issue; otherwise → discard.

5 · Training — three triggers (Fig. 7)

Every L1D block gets one extra bit, the Page Cross Bit (PCB), marking whether it was fetched by a page-cross prefetch. Training fires on three events: (1) L1D demand miss → search vUB → a hit means the filter wrongly discarded a prefetch that would have saved this exact miss → positive training (increment weights) using the hash indexes stored in that vUB entry. (2) L1D demand hit on a block with PCB=1 → search pUB → a hit means an issued page-cross prefetch is proving useful right now → positive training. (3) L1D eviction of a block with PCB=1 that received zero hits during its lifetime → search pUB → negative training (decrement weights) — the filter issued a prefetch that turned out useless.

6 · The adaptive thresholding scheme (Fig. 8)

A fixed Ta is fine for one application profile and suboptimal across many — different programs and phases have different optimal thresholds. Every epoch, the scheme collects runtime stats (useful/useless page-cross prefetch counts, IPC, LLC miss rate, ROB pressure, L1I MPKI) and reacts:

These rules layer on top of each other: the extreme-condition overrides (ROB/L1I/LLC pressure) act "on the spot" within an epoch, while the accuracy/IPC-trend rules apply at epoch boundaries using the previous epoch's statistics.

7 · Feature selection and DRIPPER's storage budget

MOKA ships 55 candidate program features and 6 system features (Table I lists the best-performing subset, built from bits of the virtual address, PC, and prefetcher delta). Feature selection is offline and greedy: rank all single-feature filters by geomean IPC speedup across the 218 seen workloads; start with the best one; add another feature only if it improves geomean IPC by more than 0.3% over the current best combination; repeat until every candidate has been tried. The optimization metric is IPC speedup specifically — not coverage (different misses matter differently) and not accuracy (forcing high accuracy leaves performance on the table).

Put real numbers on it

Worked examples

Three calculations redone by hand from the paper's own tables, then an interactive widget that runs the actual DRIPPER-for-Berti decision rule (Table II, Fig. 6).

Example 1 — the cost of one wrong page-cross prefetch

The paper states the cost of an inaccurate page-cross prefetch directly (Sections I & II-A), in memory accesses, not cycles — we keep it that way rather than inventing a latency number the paper doesn't give.

  1. If the target page's translation is not TLB-resident, a speculative page walk is triggered: up to 4 memory accesses, one per uncached level of the 5-level radix page table (fewer if the Page Structure Caches — 1/2/8/32-entry across L5–L2 — serve some levels).
  2. The prefetch request itself adds 1 more memory access to fetch the (as it turns out, unwanted) cache block.
  3. Total: up to 4 + 1 = 5 useless memory accesses for one inaccurate page-cross prefetch — exactly the figure the paper repeats in its abstract, introduction, and motivation.
  4. This is a ceiling, not an average: a hit in a Page Structure Cache serves that level from a tiny on-chip structure rather than memory, so most real speculative walks cost fewer than 4 accesses. The paper does not report an average speculative-walk depth, so treat "5" as the worst case it explicitly analyzes, not a per-prefetch average — this qualification is our own interpretive framing, not stated numerically in the paper.
◆
Takeaway: this 5-access ceiling, multiplied across every cross-page prefetch a prefetcher fires, is why "always permit" (Permit PGC) can lose to "never permit" (Discard PGC) even though crossing is sometimes hugely beneficial — the downside is asymmetric and pays out in extra memory traffic and TLB/cache pollution, not just a missed opportunity.

Example 2 — reconciling DRIPPER's geomean numbers (a ratio, not a subtraction)

Table V reports Berti+Permit PGC and Berti+DRIPPER geomean speedups, both measured over the same baseline, Berti+Discard PGC: Permit PGC = −0.8% (seen), DRIPPER = +1.7% (seen). Section V-B1's prose separately states "DRIPPER outperforms Permit PGC... by 2.5%... in geomean." Let's check that claim ourselves.

  1. Treat Discard PGC as the reference point, value 1.000. Then Permit PGC ≈ 1 − 0.008 = 0.992, and DRIPPER ≈ 1 + 0.017 = 1.017 (both relative to Discard PGC).
  2. Geomean speedups compose multiplicatively, not additively. DRIPPER's speedup over Permit PGC is the ratio of their speedups over the common baseline: 1.017 / 0.992 = 1.0252, i.e. +2.52%.
  3. That matches the paper's stated +2.5% almost exactly (rounding accounts for the last 0.02 points) — confirming the numbers in Table V and Section V-B1 are internally consistent once you compose them correctly.
  4. Same check for unseen workloads: Permit PGC = −0.9% → 0.991; DRIPPER = +1.2% → 1.012. Ratio = 1.012/0.991 = 1.0212 → +2.1%, matching Section V-B8's stated "DRIPPER outperforms... Permit PGC... by 2.1%... for the unseen workloads" exactly.
▲
Misconception it kills: naively subtracting the two percentages (1.7 − (−0.8) = 2.5) happens to land close to the correct answer here only because the percentages involved are small — it's a first-order approximation of the true multiplicative ratio, not the actual method. At larger percentages the two would visibly diverge; always compose geomean speedups as ratios of (1 + speedup), never as differences.

Source-ordering note: the paper's abstract states these same four numbers (1.7%, 1.2%, 2.5%, 2.1%) but pairs them with Permit/Discard PGC in the opposite order from the body text (Section V-B1, V-B8) and Table V. We follow the body text and table, which agree with each other and with the ratio arithmetic above — see Reference > Source notes.

Example 3 — what DRIPPER's 8-core numbers say about workload mixing

Single-core (Berti, seen workloads): DRIPPER beats Permit PGC by +2.5% and Discard PGC by +1.7%. Across 300 randomly generated 8-core mixes, the margins widen: +3.3% over Permit PGC and +2.0% over Discard PGC (Section V-B10).

  1. Both margins grow from single-core to 8-core — +2.5%→+3.3% over Permit PGC, +1.7%→+2.0% over Discard PGC.
  2. The paper's own explanation is qualitative, not a decomposed number: shared last-level cache and shared memory bandwidth across 8 cores make an inaccurate page-cross prefetch's pollution and extra memory traffic costlier system-wide than in isolation — and DRIPPER was not even re-tuned for the 8-core context, so the paper notes further exploration could do better still.
  3. Our framing, not the paper's: this is consistent with a general pattern in shared-resource systems — a filter that avoids wasted memory traffic tends to pay off more as contention for that shared resource increases, since the "waste" it prevents competes with more consumers.
◇
Grounded vs. framing: the four percentages (2.5%, 1.7%, 3.3%, 2.0%) are directly from the paper. The general "shared-resource contention amplifies filtering value" explanation in step 3 is our interpretation for a broader mental model — the paper does not decompose why the multi-core margin is larger beyond noting it is untuned.

Work the answer before opening it. Each answer ends with the misconception the question is designed to catch.

Warm-up
Why does the paper say an inaccurate page-cross prefetch can cost "up to 5" memory accesses, and where do the two numbers (4 and 1) that make up that 5 come from?
Show answer
4 comes from the speculative page walk needed to fetch the target page's translation if it isn't already TLB-resident — up to one memory access per uncached level of the paper's 5-level radix page table. 1 is the actual prefetch request fetching the (unwanted) cache block. 4 + 1 = 5. Catches: assuming a wrong prefetch only wastes "one fetch." For page-cross prefetches specifically, the translation lookup itself can be the larger part of the waste.
Diagnose
A colleague argues: "Just always let L1D prefetchers cross pages — vendors already do it, and accurate crossing clearly helps." Using the paper's own Figure 3 finding, what's wrong with "always"?
Show answer
Figure 3 shows that under an always-permit policy, only ~50% of issued page-cross prefetches are useful on average, across Berti, BOP, and IPCP — the other half are useless and each one can cost up to 5 wasted memory accesses plus TLB/cache pollution. "Always" bakes in that ~50% waste rate; a per-request filter (DRIPPER) can keep the useful half's benefit while suppressing most of the useless half. Catches: treating "vendors permit it" as evidence that indiscriminate crossing is good policy — vendors disclose that they permit it, not how they decide, which is exactly the gap this paper studies.
Diagnose
Table IV gives dTLB = 64 entries and sTLB = 1536 entries. Given that, explain why Figure 4 shows dTLB MPKI moving much more than sTLB MPKI when Permit PGC helps or hurts a workload.
Show answer
dTLB is roughly 24× smaller than sTLB, so for the same number of translations added or removed by page-cross prefetching, dTLB's miss rate (misses per kilo-instruction) shifts much more than sTLB's — a small structure is more sensitive to the same absolute change in traffic. Also, a page-cross prefetch's translation gets installed in both dTLB and sTLB, so a useful prefetch that already hits dTLB doesn't even register as an sTLB event, further muting sTLB's response. Catches: assuming "TLB" is one undifferentiated resource — the two levels have very different capacities and therefore very different sensitivities to the same intervention.
Design
The paper's "ISO Storage" baseline gives the underlying prefetcher (not a filter) DRIPPER's 1.44 KB budget, and it performs like Permit PGC (no page-cross benefit). What does this rule out, and what does it NOT rule out, about why DRIPPER works?
Show answer
It rules out "the prefetcher just needed more state/history to be more accurate in general" — extra storage spent inside Berti/BOP/IPCP's own structures doesn't fix cross-page accuracy. It does not rule out that a differently-designed use of the same 1.44 KB (not shaped as a perceptron filter with program+system features+adaptive threshold) might also work — the experiment isolates "more prefetcher storage" as the wrong lever, but it isn't a proof that MOKA's specific architecture is the unique solution. Catches: over-reading a single ablation as proof of necessity rather than sufficiency — ISO Storage shows one alternative fails, not that no alternative could succeed.
Design
DRIPPER(filter@2MB) — which decides at 2 MB granularity when large pages are used — underperforms plain DRIPPER (which always decides at 4 KB granularity) by 0.5% geomean, even in a system that actually uses 2 MB pages. Why doesn't page-size-aware filtering win here?
Show answer
The paper measures that more than 99% of page-cross prefetches never travel far enough to cross a 2 MB boundary — they only cross 4 KB boundaries, regardless of whether the underlying physical pages are 4 KB or 2 MB. A filter tuned to fire only at 2 MB boundaries is therefore evaluating almost nothing; a filter that keeps discriminating at 4 KB boundaries (plain DRIPPER) still catches nearly all the real decisions, whether or not the memory underneath happens to be backed by a large page. Catches: assuming "page-size-aware" automatically means "better" once large pages are in play — the right granularity to filter at is set by how far the prefetcher's own deltas actually reach, not by the page size the OS happens to be using.
Loose ends

Frequently asked

The questions that come up once the mechanism clicks.

QWhy doesn't this paper also filter page-cross prefetching at the L2/LLC?▾
Because L2C/LLC prefetchers operate on physical addresses (PIPT caches) — they don't have the L1D's direct, pre-translation view of the address stream. Crossing physical page boundaries at those levels is also a documented security concern (physical-address contiguity isn't guaranteed, and prior work has shown side-channel exploits from page-cross prefetching at lower-level caches on real hardware). The paper scopes its study to L1D specifically because that's where the VIPT/virtual-address decision is architecturally available.
QIs DRIPPER one fixed design, or does it change per prefetcher?▾
It changes per prefetcher, but only in which features it uses — the mechanism (hashed perceptrons + system features + adaptive threshold) is identical. Table II: Berti's DRIPPER uses Delta as its program feature; BOP and IPCP both use PC⊕Delta. All three share the same two system features, sTLB MPKI and sTLB Miss Rate. This is what "MOKA is a framework, DRIPPER is an instance" means concretely.
QHow does DRIPPER compare to prior prefetch filters like PPF?▾
PPF (ISCA'19) was designed to filter in-page L2C prefetches from the SPP prefetcher, using only program features tied to SPP's own metadata and a static threshold. The paper adapts PPF into a page-cross filter for comparison and finds it doesn't improve over Discard PGC — it lacks system features entirely, and its program features were never designed for this task. DRIPPER outperforms adapted-PPF by 2.4%/1.4%/1.6% geomean for Berti/BOP/IPCP respectively.
QDoes DRIPPER need to be retrained for every new workload?▾
No — the weight tables train continuously online via vUB/pUB during execution, and the adaptive threshold re-tunes every epoch at runtime. The feature selection (which program/system features to include, Table II) is done once, offline, on 218 "seen" workloads. The paper explicitly tests generalization on 178 "unseen" workloads never used during that offline selection, and DRIPPER still gains +2.1%/+1.2% over Permit/Discard PGC there.
QDoes an L2C prefetcher underneath Berti change DRIPPER's benefit?▾
The trend holds regardless: with NoL2Pref, SPP, IPCP, or BOP as the L2C prefetcher, Permit PGC still degrades performance vs. Discard PGC and DRIPPER still delivers the highest speedups (Fig. 17). The benefit is slightly larger with no L2C prefetcher present, because then there's no L2C prefetcher independently catching the misses that an accurate L1D page-cross prefetch would also have caught.
QIs this paper about memory tiering, CXL, or DRAM/NVM at all?▾
No. This is entirely about L1D cache prefetching and TLB/page-walk cost within a single memory tier — it never discusses NVM, CXL, or OS-managed tiering. The connection to that world is indirect: this paper's careful accounting of TLB MPKI sensitivity and speculative-page-walk cost is background machinery that also underlies tiering-adjacent questions (e.g., how expensive address-translation misses are, why TLB reach matters), but treat any bridge to tiering/CXL in this guide as our own framing, not the paper's claim — see Reference > Source notes.

Reference

Glossary, a dated summary card, further reading, and the honest provenance notes.

Core terms
Page-cross prefetchA prefetch request whose target address lies on a different page than the triggering access — requires a TLB lookup (and possibly a page walk) for the new page.
In-page prefetchA prefetch request that stays within the current page's translation — no additional TLB/page-walk work needed.
VIPT / PIPTVirtually-Indexed-Physically-Tagged (L1D — sees virtual addresses, enabling page-cross decisions) vs. Physically-Indexed-Physically-Tagged (L2C/LLC — physical addresses only).
Permit PGC / Discard PGCThe two static baselines: always permit vs. never permit page-cross prefetching. Neither wins across all workloads (Fig. 2).
MOKAThe general framework for building Page-Cross Filters: hashed-perceptron program features + threshold-gated system features + epoch-based adaptive threshold.
DRIPPERA MOKA-built Page-Cross Filter prototyped for Berti, BOP, and IPCP (Table II), 1.44 KB/core.
Page-Cross FilterThe microarchitectural gate (Fig. 5, step B) that decides issue-or-discard for candidate page-cross prefetches.
Activation threshold (Ta)The cutoff that w_final must exceed for a page-cross prefetch to be issued; re-tuned every epoch.
Supporting terms
Program featureA feature derived from the current request (PC, VA bits, delta, XOR combinations) — 55 candidates in MOKA, best subset in Table I.
System featureA live runtime metric (sTLB/L1D/LLC MPKI or miss rate) gated by its own threshold — 6 candidates in MOKA.
Weight Table (WT)Hashed-perceptron table of saturating-counter weights, one per program feature.
vUB / pUBVirtual/Physical Update Buffers — training structures capturing false negatives (vUB) and outcomes of issued prefetches (pUB).
Page Cross Bit (PCB)Extra per-L1D-block bit marking whether the block was fetched by a page-cross prefetch, used to trigger training on hit/eviction.
dTLB / sTLBFirst-level data TLB (64-entry, per Table IV) and unified second-level/last-level TLB (1536-entry) in the paper's simulated core.
MPKIMisses per kilo-instruction — the paper's standard sensitivity metric for TLB and cache levels.
Speculative page walkA page-table walk triggered by a prefetch (not a demand access) to resolve a translation ahead of need; up to 4 memory accesses in the paper's 5-level model.
Coverage / AccuracyCoverage = fraction of demand misses eliminated by prefetching; accuracy = fraction of issued prefetches later used at least once.
Discard PTWBaseline that permits page-cross prefetching only when the translation is already TLB-resident (never triggers a speculative walk).
ISO StorageBaseline that spends DRIPPER's 1.44 KB budget enlarging the underlying prefetcher's own structures instead of building a filter.
ChampSim / SimPointThe trace-driven simulator used for all experiments; SimPoint selects representative execution intervals (weight < 0.05 discarded).

📌 Summary card · as of 2026-08-30 · HPCA 2025

  • What: the first characterization of page-cross prefetching for VIPT L1D caches, plus MOKA, a framework for building learned Page-Cross Filters, prototyped as DRIPPER.
  • Why: vendors permit L1D prefetchers to cross pages but disclose no policy; academia forbids it entirely; neither extreme is right — Permit PGC and Discard PGC each win on disjoint workload subsets.
  • Core measurement: under always-permit, only ~50% of issued page-cross prefetches are useful (Fig. 3); a wrong one costs up to 5 memory accesses (4-access speculative walk + 1 fetch).
  • Mechanism: hashed-perceptron program features (always counted) + threshold-gated system features (counted only when their condition holds) summed into w_final, compared to an epoch-adaptive Ta.
  • DRIPPER (Berti): 1 program feature (Delta) + 2 system features (sTLB MPKI, sTLB Miss Rate), 1.44 KB/core.
  • Headline results (Berti, single-core geomean): +2.5% over Permit PGC, +1.7% over Discard PGC (218 seen); +2.1%/+1.2% (178 unseen); +3.3%/+2.0% across 300 8-core mixes.
  • Coverage/accuracy story: DRIPPER matches Permit PGC's ~4% coverage gain over Discard PGC while turning its −2.6% accuracy loss into a +1.2% accuracy gain.
  • Holds under large pages: +2.2%/+1.3% geomean with 4 KB+2 MB pages mixed; filtering at 4 KB granularity beats filtering at 2 MB granularity (>99% of crossings never reach 2 MB boundaries).
  • Not about: DRAM/NVM tiering, CXL, or OS-managed placement — purely L1D cache prefetching and TLB/page-walk cost within one memory tier.

Bridging to CXL / OS-managed tiering your framing, not the paper's

Conceptual connection — not discussed in the source paper

This paper's dTLB/sTLB MPKI accounting and its explicit page-walk cost model (5-level radix walk, split PSCs, up to 4 accesses per speculative walk) are useful reference points for reasoning about address-translation overhead more generally — including in tiered-memory systems where page-table and TLB behavior interact with page placement and migration. The paper itself makes no such connection; treat this purely as a bridge for your own research context.
